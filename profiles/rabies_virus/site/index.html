IMMUNOHISTOCHEMICAL EVALUATION OF RABIES VIRUS DISSEMINATION TO PERIPHERAL TISSUES AND ORGANS IN NATURALLY INFECTED STRIPED SKUNKS (MEPHITIS MEPHITIS) FROM NORTHERN COLORADO, USA

Authors: Terry R. Spraker, Molly R. Selleck, Todd Bass, Amy T. Gilbert
Original abstract (English)
We describe the diagnostic utility of peripheral tissues from naturally infected free-ranging rabid striped skunks (Mephitis mephitis) to explore enhanced rabies surveillance testing using alternative tissues to the brainstem. Fifty-two dead or moribund adult striped skunks were collected by the Larimer County Health Department (Colorado, USA) as part of routine public health rabies surveillance during November 2018–July 2019 throughout Larimer County. Skunk carcasses were submitted to the Colorado State University Diagnostic Laboratory for rabies diagnosis using the direct fluorescent antibody test on the brainstem at the level on the pons and cerebellum. Multiple additional tissues were opportunistically collected from skunk carcasses and fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, including brain, trigeminal ganglion, mandibular and parotid salivary glands, eyes, tongue, lip (including vibrissa), ear pinna, and uterus and fetuses if pregnant. Fixed tissues were trimmed for histology, stained with H&E, and immunolabeled for detection of the rabies virus (RV) antigen. We detected RV immunopositive cells in multiple sections of brain, oral and nasal cavities, skin, and eyes as well as in the placenta and skin of fetuses from rabid skunks, suggesting the potential for transplacental transmission. We detected RV in the nerves and ganglia of salivary glands before detection in the salivary parenchymal cells in rabid skunks. The serous demilunes of the mandibular salivary gland appeared to be the first parenchymal cells to be infected with RV. We detected minimal RV immunopositive cells in the serous adenomeres of the parotid salivary gland and the mucous adenomeres of salivary tissue of the tongue. The results suggest that swabs from the oral and nasal cavities and cornea and biopsies of the skin could be of value for enhanced rabies surveillance in cases where the animal's brain has been destroyed or is unavailable.

Stray dog culling for rabies control: a stochastic modelling study.

Authors: Mervin Kambuwa, Solomon Kadaleka, Herieth Rwezaura, Milliward Maliyoni
Original abstract (English)
A stochastic continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) model, rooted in a deterministic approach, investigates rabies dynamics within human and stray dog populations. Stability analysis confirms the disease-free equilibrium is globally asymptotically stable for Re 1. Through a Galton-Watson branching process, the stochastic threshold is derived, providing extinction probabilities that align closely with 10,000 numerical sample paths. The CTMC model predicts two distinct outcomes, disease extinction or persistence, and demonstrates a positive probability of rabies extinction in finite time even when Re>1. This contrast is significant because the deterministic model predicts a certain outbreak under identical conditions, whereas the stochastic framework captures the possibility of early extinction due to demographic fluctuations. Sensitivity analysis identifies the natural death rate and culling intensity as the most influential parameters. Results show a direct correlation between culling intensity and the probability of rabies elimination.

A qualitative exploration of perceptions, practices, and systemic barriers to rabies post-exposure prophylaxis in a rural area of Uttarakhand, India

Authors: Pallavi Singh, Rohit Katre, Abhishek Sadasivan, Vartika Saxena
Original abstract (English)
Rabies continues to be a universally fatal yet preventable neglected tropical disease (NTD). While most studies on animal bites are quantitative and describe the burden of exposure, there is limited literature capturing the socio-cultural, behavioural and systemic factors that shape response and health-seeking behaviour following an animal bite, particularly in rural settings. To explore perceptions, practices, and barriers related to rabies prevention and its post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) among individuals with animal bite/s. A qualitative descriptive-exploratory study was conducted among 21 individuals who had presented with animal bites at a rural health facility in Uttarakhand, India. Participants were purposively sampled from a record-derived line listing to ensure variation in exposure category, health-seeking behaviour and PEP completion. Semi-structured interviews (16 face-to-face, five telephonic) were conducted in Hindi until data saturation was reached. Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase reflexive thematic analysis, and the resulting themes were subsequently mapped onto the Health Belief Model and the Socio-Ecological Model. Five themes were identified: perceived invulnerability and normalization of animal bites; misconceptions and fragmented knowledge about rabies; culturally embedded and inadequate wound care practices; structural and cognitive barriers to completion of PEP; and dependence on informal health information networks. Non-completion of PEP was driven less by absence of information than by locally coherent risk judgements, culturally sanctioned wound-care practices that displaced evidence-based first aid, and the unavailability of rabies immunoglobulin at the point of first contact. Prevention of rabies in this study setting was shaped by interconnected cultural, behavioural and systemic factors operating simultaneously at individual, interpersonal, community and health-system levels; because these determinants sustain one another, interventions addressing any single level are unlikely to improve adherence to the recommended PEP schedule in similar rural contexts.

This study aimed to assess the safety and immunogenicity of an animal-component-free, antibiotic-free, freeze-dried human rabies vaccine (Vero Cell) with the "Essen" regimen and boosted two doses in China. Methods 1,200 healthy participants aged 10–60 years were randomized into two groups to receive the test vaccine or a licensed freeze-dried human Rabies Vaccine (Vero Cell) with the five doses Essen regimen. Debunking myths about the rabies vaccine Before diving in, let’s clear up some common misconceptions about rabies and the rabies vaccine. Rabies is a zoonotic virus that comes in two types: furious rabies and paralytic rabies. Both types of rabies attack the central nervous system and both are fatal. Any mammal can be infected with rabies, and if infected, can transmit it to other animals or humans through a bite or scratch—even if you can’t see or feel the wound. Though rabies doesn’t turn its victims into people-eating zombies, as Hollywood would have us believe, it does deliver an acutely agonizing death. The modern vaccine for rabies is made from cell culture. A cell culture vaccine contains no live virus. It is literally impossible for you to get rabies from the vaccine, which, you may be surprised to hear, is also pretty painless. For household dogs, cats and ferrets, rabies is the only vaccine required by New Hampshire state law. (Photo by Sebastian Condrea/Getty Images) Last month, the House Environment and Agriculture Committee recommended reconsidering the contents of a sidelined bill from last session: House Bill 1488 , which sought to increase rabies vaccine exemptions for New Hampshire pets. The bill was sent for interim study by the full New Hampshire House in March and, with the 2026 session closed and the biennium winding down, was no longer eligible for passage. Instead, the committee voted, 10-1, to recommend the topic be addressed in future legislation. That move came as a surprise to some. "Truthfully, we didn't think it was going to go as far," said Dr. Jane Barlow Roy, who chairs the legislative committee of the New Hampshire Veterinary Medical Association. That's because there is broad consensus among veterinarians that the rabies vaccine — which protects against a disease both transmissible to humans and universally fatal — is safe and life-saving. Yet the bill was driven forward by pet owners concerned about the rare but non-zero occurrence of adverse reactions to the vaccine, reflecting the increasing hesitancy around both human and pet shots that is driving the resurgence of some infectious diseases in the U.S. Committee members said at an October work session they wanted to keep the door open to potential future changes to rabies vaccine guidance and ensure the topic is revisited if more information becomes available. But answering some of the questions raised by pet owners and legislators, veterinarians said, will require research that is complicated and ethically fraught. For household dogs, cats and ferrets, rabies is the only vaccine required by New Hampshire state law. The viral illness is transmitted when saliva from an infected animal touches broken skin or mucous membranes like those inside the mouth, nose and eyes. The virus moves slowly through nervous tissue, incubating for weeks or more before symptoms like fever, headache, confusion, vomiting, and, later, aggression, difficulty swallowing or paralysis begin to manifest. While post-exposure treatment can prevent the disease from progressing if it is administered soon enough, once symptoms appear, the disease is considered incurable. Wildlife are the most common transmitters of rabies in the U.S., where the prevalence of vaccines has reduced both the disease's incidence in domestic animals and the rabies death rate in humans and pets. But globally, about 70,000 people die of rabies each year, and more than 99% of people who get the disease outside the U.S. are infected by a dog , according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. In the U.S., there was an unusually high number of human exposures documented this year, according to the CDC. And recent research suggests that the disease is responsible for more human deaths nationwide than scientists once believed. Kelly Arsenault, a Sandown Realtor, is skeptical about some vaccines. But rabies, she said, is not one that she skips when she brings her small dogs to the vet. "I believe in the ability of the body to heal," she said. "However, rabies is not one of them — and I know that — it is not one of those that the body is going to heal from." Yet Arsenault, who has for years worked to help rehome and rescue small dogs, also has questions about the rabies vaccine. More than 150 dogs, most of them fluffy and weighing under 15 pounds, have passed through Arsenault's care over the years, she said. To date, one that she is aware of has had a significant negative reaction to a vaccine, she said, becoming severely ill after injection. Treating the dog with allergy medication managed the reaction, she said, but it was unsettling. She wonders why it happened and whether the incident could have been prevented. Negative reactions to vaccines in pets are uncommon, but most often appear similar to what many people experience after a flu shot, like injection site soreness and mild discomfort, said Dr. Lisa Boyer, a Seacoast area veterinarian, in an online seminar this October. Less commonly, severe reactions can involve facial swelling, vomiting or difficulty breathing. These reactions are managed with veterinary care, said Barlow Roy. And in New Hampshire, as in some other states, a pathway exists in state law to allow owners of pets susceptible to reactions to seek an exemption from the requirement. "The process is there for those dogs and cats that truly should not be getting the rabies vaccine," said Barlow Roy. That exemption must be approved by the New Hampshire state veterinarian; after it is granted, exempt animals must be kept away from other animals or muzzled. Owners also assume more liability for their unvaccinated animals. For these reasons and the lethal nature of the rabies virus, the veterinarians consider the vaccine more than worthwhile for almost all pets. Yet the online proliferation of information and misinformation about vaccines has raised more curiosity among pet owners, who are asking more questions and starting conversations the veterinarians said they welcomed. However, in some cases, they said, pet owners have also become more likely to push back against professionals' recommendations. HB 1488 sought to broaden the allowed cases for rabies vaccine exemptions in New Hampshire under one condition: A pet has received a series of tests attempting to demonstrate that their immune system has responded to the vaccine. Those tests, known as antibody titers, can measure the level of rabies-specific antibodies, or immune system proteins, in a pet's blood. The pet's own white blood cells produce antibodies after they are exposed to the inactivated virus molecules in a vaccine; then the antibodies remain in the pet's blood, ready to raise the alarm if they ever encounter the rabies virus again, such as if the pet is exposed to rabies from a wild animal. The idea of a titer testing protocol appeals to Arsenault, she said, because if a test could demonstrate that her pet is protected against rabies, she could skip additional "booster" shots. But the problem, according to veterinarians, is that there is currently no test that can prove definitively whether or not a pet has enough antibodies to be protected against rabies. If a titer comes back showing rabies antibodies in a pet's blood, that confirms that the animal has some degree of immunity against rabies from a previous vaccination, Barlow Roy said. But it takes a certain threshold amount of antibodies to actually combat the virus in the case of a real exposure. That makes the rabies titer test unhelpful for two reasons. One limitation, according to American Veterinary Medical Association President Dr. Jennifer Quammen, is that the test is a snapshot in time of a dynamic situation. The amount of antibodies in a pet's blood changes as time goes on; this is part of the reason booster shots are called for. Second, scientists don't have a reliable way of knowing what threshold amount of antibodies a pet's blood must contain in order for them to be fully protected against the rabies virus. Therefore, in the face of a deadly disease like rabies, veterinarians recommend pets get boosters on schedule to ensure they are protected. The limitation of the rabies titer test doesn't apply to some other diseases for which scientists have developed titer testing protocol. Pet owners may opt to use an antibody titer test to guide decisions about booster shots for some other illnesses, like canine distemper and parvovirus, veterinarians said. With rabies, the factor that has limited the development of a titer testing protocol is the disease's lethality. Reaching a conclusion about what level of blood antibodies is enough to protect a dog requires testing on living animals. Dogs with different levels of antibodies in their blood, for example, would be intentionally exposed to the disease; some are expected to contract the illness. That result helps scientists determine how much antibody is actually protective. Animals that contract diseases for which treatments exist can be supported after that experiment with medical intervention, Barlow Roy said, making these studies less problematic for investigators. But an animal that begins showing signs of rabies is effectively facing a death sentence, making the protocol more fraught. "We're not comparing apples to apples, you know? We're in the same realm," she said, describing the differences in rabies titer research versus that on other diseases. "It's more challenging from an ethical point of view ... and that's why we hesitate," she said. Arsenault said she was conflicted: While she wants the results of the experiment, she also feels moral qualms about what it would necessitate. "That's probably where this whole thing gets difficult," she said. "Would I be willing to be part of that study and watch a dog die from rabies? No ... I don't know if I could live with myself." However, some advocates of reducing rabies vaccine frequency have pushed for that research to move ahead, and some studies of limited scope have been completed. That included a 2020 study funded by the Rabies Challenge Fund Charitable Trust that involved the euthanasia of at least 45 beagles, some of which were infected with rabies, raising objections from animal rights groups like People for the Ethical Treatment of Animals. But research has not yielded enough data to draw conclusions, veterinarians told legislators at hearings on HB 1488. Most rabies policy and vaccine guidance is based on a document called the "rabies compendium," compiled by the National Association of State Public Health Veterinarians. The document is due for revision this year, said Dr. Nathan Harvey, New Hampshire assistant state veterinarian, at an October work session on the bill. Veterinarians expect that the compendium will include updated guidance on the vaccine, possibly — but not certainly — to include guidance around the use of titer testing. "They are looking at whether or not we can start making those correlations to (titer levels) being protective, and that information may come out in the compendium. But we can't assume that information is going to be published, or not," Barlow Roy said. The reality at present continues to be that titer tests cannot replace vaccine programs, for the sake of animal and human health, Quammen said. Some pet owners have also raised concerns about particularly small animals, like the dogs that Arsenault rescues. Some negative reactions seem to occur with more frequency in small animals, Barlow Roy said, though she stressed that they are still uncommon. But while pet owners like Arsenault have asked whether a smaller "dose" of vaccine could be administered to smaller animals, that is misleading, Boyer said. Confusion can arise, she said, because many medications are dosed by body weight. But the way that vaccines work — by triggering an immune response — is not as sensitive to body size, meaning that the amount of a vaccine's active ingredient that an animal must receive for the vaccine to work is not necessarily different for, say, a Pomeranian and a Lab. "If you cut the dose in half and assume you have an appropriate dose, you will not be fully vaccinated or fully protected," she said. But there are other components besides dead viral molecules in a vaccine, and some formulations exist that deliver the same amount of active ingredient in a smaller volume, Boyer said. Veterinarians continue to recommend the rabies booster schedule that is widely accepted as the safest and best option for people and pets. South Acworth Republican Rep. Judy Aron, who chaired the House Committee on Environment and Agriculture last session but did not seek reelection this fall, said during the Oct. 6 work session that the topic could be revisited by the Legislature if more guidance is released in the revised compendium. Barlow Roy urged pet owners to discuss any concerns with their veterinarian, including by reporting any incidences of suspected vaccine side effects, which she said clinics routinely share with vaccine manufacturers. For Barlow Roy, the urgency of keeping pets protected from the virus is augmented by her compassion for the animals that visit her exam table — and the humans who love them. "If I ever, God forbid, have an animal in my care that has rabies and it is given to, you know, a child in the household, or another family member, a human, that we could have prevented, that would definitely crush me," she said. "That's always the thing in the back of my head: Rabies is 100% fatal. It results in death 100% of the time. I think that's why we are so adamant about rabies and this whole titer discussion." According to them, authorities should ensure that any animals brought from the mainland, particularly dogs and cats, are properly vaccinated and accompanied by valid vaccination certificates from recognized institutions. Proper verification of such certificates is essential before allowing animals to enter the islands. The scientists also highlighted the importance of immediate first aid in the event of an animal bite. Washing the wound thoroughly with plenty of water and seeking prompt medical attention for anti-rabies treatment can significantly reduce the risk of infection. The Pasteur Institute of India, located in Coonoor in Tamil Nadu, is one of the country’s premier government institutions dedicated to vaccine research and production. Functioning as an autonomous body under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, Government of India, the institute has played a pioneering role in the development and supply of vaccines for several decades. The institute traces its origins to 1907, when it was established as the Pasteur Institute of Southern India following the death of a young woman who could not receive timely anti-rabies treatment. Over the years, it has achieved several milestones in vaccine research and manufacturing, including the production of anti-rabies vaccines, influenza vaccines and oral polio vaccines. In 2001, the institute developed a purified rabies vaccine derived from Vero cells for human use at an affordable cost, becoming the first government-controlled institution in India to do so. The move significantly helped reduce the cost of rabies vaccines in the country. Today, the institute operates modern facilities for the production of DPT group vaccines and has an installed capacity of around 130 million doses. Its laboratories are equipped with advanced technology and equipment used for large-scale vaccine production and research. The institute is also involved in academic activities, including doctoral research in microbiology, biochemistry and biotechnology. In addition, its anti-rabies treatment centre, established in 1907, continues to treat animal bite cases and collect important epidemiological data. Looking ahead, the institute plans to expand its capabilities further by establishing new biosafety level facilities for the production of viral, bacterial, conjugate and recombinant DNA vaccines. These developments are expected to strengthen India’s vaccine security and preparedness for future public health challenges. The disease has a nearly 100% mortality rate once clinical symptoms appear, but it is completely preventable if treated and vaccinated promptly after exposure. Each year, rabies claims the lives of tens of thousands of people worldwide . Notably, approximately 40% of victims are children under 15 years old. Dogs are the primary source of rabies virus transmission, accounting for about 99% of human rabies cases. Rabies can infect all mammals, from common pets like dogs, cats, and cattle to wild animals. The rabies virus is primarily transmitted to humans through the saliva of infected animals, most commonly through bites or scratches. The virus can also enter the body when the saliva of an infected animal comes into direct contact with the mucous membranes of the eyes, mouth, or open wounds.
