Comparison of Silevimig, a Novel Bispecific Anti-Rabies Monoclonal Antibody, with Human Rabies Immunoglobulin for Post-Exposure Prophylaxis: A Phase III, Randomized, Double-Blind, Non-Inferiority Trial.

Comparison of Silevimig, a Novel Bispecific Anti-Rabies Monoclonal Antibody, with Human Rabies Immunoglobulin for Post-Exposure Prophylaxis: A Phase III, Randomized, Double-Blind, Non-Inferiority Trial.

Authors: Xiu-Qing Wang, Jing He, Yong-Xian Zha, Yangwen Jiang, Shu-Zuo Tan, Qing-Hua Li, Rui-Chun Zhao, Hong Su, Yuan-Mo Shi, Zhong-Liang Yang
Original abstract (English)
Abstracts This phase III randomized, double-blind, non-inferiority trial evaluated Silevimig, a bispecific anti-rabies monoclonal antibody targeting antigenic sites I and III, as a substitute for human rabies immunoglobulin (HRIG) in post-exposure prophylaxis. Adults (>18 years) with suspected WHO category III rabies exposure were randomly assigned (3:1) to receive Silevimig or HRIG. After wound cleansing, the study drug was administered on day 0, together with a rabies vaccine given on days 0, 3, 7, 14, and 28. The co-primary endpoints were the adjusted geometric mean concentration (GMC) of rabies virus–neutralizing antibodies (RVNAs) on day 7, the proportion of participants with an RVNA level of ≥ 0.5 IU/mL (seroresponse) by day 14, and the rabies-free survival rate in 1 year (D365). Safety was assessed through adverse events (AEs) and serious adverse events (SAEs). A total of 1,200 participants were enrolled. On day 7, the adjusted GMC of RVNAs was 0.40 IU/mL in the Silevimig group versus 0.36 IU/mL in the HRIG group, meeting the non-inferiority criterion (ratio 1.11; 95% CI: 1.01–1.21). Seroresponse rates on days 14 and 90 were slightly higher with Silevimig. No rabies cases occurred during the 1-year follow-up, yielding a 100% rabies-free survival rate in both groups. Most AEs were mild to moderate. Silevimig was non-inferior to HRIG in inducing early RVNA responses, was well tolerated, and did not interfere with vaccine-induced immunity. These findings support Silevimig as a safe and effective alternative for passive rabies post-exposure prophylaxis (ClinicalTrials, NCT05846568).

This study aimed to evaluate Silevimig, a novel bispecific anti-rabies monoclonal antibody, as a substitute for human rabies immunoglobulin (HRIG) in post-exposure prophylaxis, addressing the need for alternative treatments in rabies prevention.
Design and population
A phase III, randomized, double-blind, non-inferiority trial was conducted in adults (>18 years) with suspected WHO category III rabies exposure, with 1,200 participants enrolled and randomly assigned in a 3:1 ratio to receive Silevimig or HRIG.

On day 7, the adjusted GMC of RVNAs was 0.40 IU/mL in the Silevimig group versus 0.36 IU/mL in the HRIG group, meeting the non-inferiority criterion (ratio 1.11; 95% CI: 1.01–1.21). No rabies cases occurred during the 1-year follow-up, yielding a 100% rabies-free survival rate in both groups.
Interpretation and novelty
The authors interpreted that Silevimig was non-inferior to HRIG in inducing early RVNA responses and was well tolerated, without interfering with vaccine-induced immunity.
Scientific and public-health significance
These findings suggest that Silevimig could be a safe and effective alternative for passive rabies post-exposure prophylaxis, potentially improving treatment options for rabies prevention.
Limitations and evidence strength
The study was well conducted, but the generalizability of results may be limited to the specific population and setting, and the evidence is strong due to the non-inferiority trial design and complete follow-up.

Phylogenetic analysis of rabies virus isolates recovered in the Novosibirsk Oblast (the Russian Federation)

Authors: С. А. Чупин, Е. V. Chernyshova, Aleksandr D. Botvinkin, Ivan D. Zarva, I. A. Chvala, N. A. Nazarov, A. D. Grekhneva, I. M. Cherednikova, M. A. Timofeeva, S. A. Chupin
Original abstract (English)
The Novosibirsk Oblast is classified as a rabies-endemic region within the Siberian Federal District of the Russian Federation, ranking first in the number of reported cases among all constituent territories of the district. However, there is a notable lack of research on the genetic properties of the rabies virus field isolates. This study seeks to assess how rabies outbreaks in the rabies endemic Novosibirsk Oblast are connected with those in the neighbouring regions, by analyzing genetic diversity of rabies virus variants and their spatial distribution. Materials and methods . Two overlapping genome fragments containing full-size nucleoprotein (N) gene of rabies virus were Sanger-sequenced following purification, using a previously established procedure. Phylogenetic analysis was performed using a Bayesian approach as implemented in the Bayesian Evolutionary Analysis Sampling Trees (BEAST Х) software package. The number of registered animal rabies cases after 2012 declined markedly and has not exceeded 60 cases per year. All the studied isolates belong to genetic group C. The isolates are classified into two genetic clusters – C2c (35 isolates) and C2b (18 isolates). One of the rabies virus isolates was recovered in mouse neuroblastoma cell culture. All group C clusters found in the Novosibirsk Oblast have previously been reported in Kazakhstan as well. The most stable connections are observed within the East Kazakhstan (Сlusters C2c, C1b) and North Kazakhstan (Сlusters C2c, C2b) Oblasts. Results of the phylogenetic analysis are consistent with earlier descriptions of “fox” rabies and with the findings of epizootiological monitoring in adjacent territories of Western Siberia and Kazakhstan. At present, two independently derived virus clusters – C2b and C2c – are circulating in the Novosibirsk Oblast.

Original abstract (English)
Although rabies incidence in Russia has decreased over the past ten years, it still remains a highly relevant disease. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) in its various modifications is one of the tools for detecting and characterizing rabies virus in pathological samples. To ensure reliable diagnosis, the current data on genetic properties of the isolates circulating in particular area should be considered when developing PCR. Providing a theoretical foundation and optimizing the test system based on combined reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (C-RT-PCR) for the detection of a rabies virus genome fragment using endogenous internal control. Materials and methods. Rabies virus vaccine strain “RV-97” and laboratory fixed rabies virus strain “CVS”, brain samples from animals of various species, both rabies virus positive and negative, were used in the work. Oligonucleotide design and in silico specificity testing were performed using Primer Blast online tool. Syntol reagents (Russia) were used for C-RT-PCR. As a result of the analysis of 193 N-gene sequences of rabies virus isolates circulating in Russia and neighboring countries, six primers and two TaqMan probes were designed. Two primers and a probe, optimal for the development of the test system, were experimentally identified. During a series of experiments, C-RT-PCR parameters such as oligonucleotide and magnesium ion concentrations, as well as annealing temperature were optimized. To control the reaction conditions in each tube, an oligonucleotide system was developed for the amplification and detection of the mammalian beta-actin gene region. The possibility of simultaneous application of two PCR systems in one test tube has been experimentally demonstrated. Analysis of 193 N-gene sequences of various rabies virus isolates showed that the developed oligonucleotides can theoretically ensure detection of the vast majority of the viruses circulating in the Russian Federation and neighboring countries. The main components and reaction conditions of the test system for detecting the rabies virus genome by C-RT-PCR using mammalian beta-actin gene-based endogenous internal control have been developed and optimized.

Original abstract (English)
INTRODUCTION: Rabies remains a persistent public health threat in India, while gaps in surveillance and coordination of control measures undermine progress towards ending human rabies deaths. The WHO advocates a One Health approach, combining mass dog vaccination, postexposure prophylaxis (PEP) and Integrated Bite Case Management (IBCM). IBCM is not yet incorporated into India's national rabies action plan for rabies elimination. This study seeks to generate robust evidence on the effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, feasibility, facilitators and barriers of IBCM implemented within Kerala's Rabies Control Programme. METHODS AND ANALYSIS: This implementation research adopts a stepped-wedge cluster randomised controlled trial (RCT) design. IBCM will be implemented across six administrative blocks in Thiruvananthapuram District, Kerala, across diverse rural and urban settings and including tribal population representation. The IBCM intervention includes stakeholder training and support and active animal surveillance aligned with WHO guidance. Prior to IBCM implementation, baseline data will be collected on management of dog bites, bite patient incidence and healthcare seeking behaviours. Hospital-based event tracking of bite cases will be conducted at enrolled public health facilities before and during IBCM implementation. The resulting RCT data will be used to model the impacts and cost-effectiveness of IBCM, including the potential for cost savings through judicious PEP if scaled across Kerala. To understand the barriers and facilitators to IBCM implementation, we will use in-depth interviews and focus group discussions with stakeholders.There is an urgent need to strengthen rabies surveillance, including coordination across health and animal health sectors and outbreak responses to accelerate rabies elimination. IBCM is expected to lead to improved PEP completion, increased detection and laboratory confirmation of rabid dogs, strengthened dog vaccination coverage and targeted outbreak responses in high-risk areas. This RCT will provide critical evidence to inform policy, support scale-up, generate insights into contextual barriers and facilitators for IBCM implementation, and guide India's One Health rabies elimination strategy. This study will evaluate a health-sector-initiated IBCM approach that identifies cases through dog-bite patients presenting to health facilities, supporting comprehensive One Health integration and improved patient care. Question and background
The study was needed to address gaps in surveillance and coordination of control measures for rabies in India, where rabies remains a persistent public health threat. The objective was to generate evidence on the effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, feasibility, facilitators, and barriers of Integrated Bite Case Management (IBCM) within Kerala's Rabies Control Programme.

A stepped-wedge cluster randomised controlled trial (RCT) design was adopted to evaluate IBCM across six administrative blocks in Thiruvananthapuram District, Kerala, including diverse rural and urban settings and tribal populations.
Core methods
IBCM will be implemented through stakeholder training, active animal surveillance, and community-based hotline tracking of suspect or probable rabies exposures. Original abstract (English)
Abstract The baculovirus/insect cell system is widely used for recombinant protein and virus-like particles (VLP) production, but its performance at high cell density is limited by infection-associated metabolic stress. In this study, a targeted metabolic footprinting approach was applied to characterize the extracellular metabolome of Spodoptera frugiperda Sf9 cells during baculovirus-mediated production of rabies VLP in chemically defined medium. Infected and uninfected cultures were cultivated in controlled bioreactor runs, and extracellular carbohydrates, amino acids, organic acids, and nucleosides were quantified by HPLC and LC-MS/MS. Metabolite data were converted into specific uptake and release rates, then also analyzed using Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA), and Variable Importance in Projection scoring (VIP). Although infected and control samples partially overlapped in PCA, supervised analysis improved group discrimination and identified sucrose, leucine, lysine, glycine, and pyruvate as the main variables associated with infection. Sequence-based compositional analysis of the recombinant proteins supported the relevance of amino acid-related metabolic changes, while transmission electron microscopy confirmed successful formation of rabies VLP with expected morphology. Together, these results provide a quantitative description of the metabolic burden imposed by rabies VLP production in Sf9 cells and highlight candidate metabolites for medium redesign and targeted feeding strategies to reduce the cell density effect and improve process productivity in baculovirus-based VLP manufacturing.

Authors: Felix J. Lankester, Ahmed Lugelo, Joel Changalucha, Danni Anderson, Christian Tetteh Duamor, Anna Czupryna, Kennedy Lushasi, Elaine Ferguson, Emmanuel Senyael Swai, Maganga Sambo
Original abstract (English)
BACKGROUND: Approximately 60,000 people die from rabies annually, mostly in Africa and Asia. Mass dog vaccination is critical for elimination. Herd immunity has been calculated to be attained when >40% (critical threshold) of dogs are vaccinated. A common delivery method implemented in Tanzania and other countries typically involves non-local teams travelling to villages implementing dog vaccination annually through static-point clinics using cold chain stored vaccines (Team-based delivery). Determination that a canine rabies vaccine is thermotolerant enables novel Community-based dog vaccination strategies whereby community vaccinators use vaccines stored locally throughout the year (Community-based delivery). METHODS: Two dog vaccination strategies were compared using a three-year cluster randomized controlled trial in Tanzania (Clinical Trials Registration Number ISRCTN14813279). The strategies were designed to represent implementation using resources typical of government-led initiatives. Wards (n = 112) were randomly assigned to either Team-based delivery or Community-based delivery. Coverage was estimated using household surveys implemented biannually. FINDINGS: Mean coverage achieved by Community-based delivery (55%; 95% Confidence Interval (CI): 45-65%) was higher than Team-based delivery (37%; 95% CI: 28-46%). Moreover, because coverage was lower and declined over each year only in Team-based delivery, the probability of coverage being below the critical threshold was higher for Team-based delivery (60%) compared to Community-based delivery (18%). To ensure coverage does not dip below the critical threshold by the year end, coverage at the start of the year in Team-based delivery needed to be higher (61%) than in Community-based (42%). INTERPRETATION: Community-based delivery achieved higher and more consistent vaccination coverage across a range of settings typical of many sub-Saharan African countries. Generalisation to different sociocultural/ agroecological settings requires further evidence. Although direct evidence on impact on rabies elimination was not measured, this approach could play a role in national elimination strategies, developed for the global 'Zero by 30' strategy to end dog-mediated rabies deaths by 2030, and in response to investment by Gavi, the vaccine alliance.

Original abstract (English)

Original abstract (English)
Wild boar-related trauma is uncommon but may result in severe injuries that pose diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, particularly in wilderness or resource-limited environments. Most scientific literature consists of isolated case reports, regional series, and forensic descriptions; clinical guidance is therefore fragmented. This study synthesizes two complementary evidence sources: a structured observational cohort documenting 101 injuries sustained by boar hunters during organized hunts in Germany, and a comprehensive systematic review of all globally published wild boar- and feral pig-related human injuries. The observational cohort provided internally consistent epidemiologic information on who was injured, under what circumstances, how quickly assistance was obtained, and how injuries were treated and ultimately healed. The systematic review added detailed insight into injury morphology and severity across diverse settings, including reports of deep wound tracts, extensive soft-tissue disruption, significant contamination, and the risk of hemorrhage, neurovascular compromise, or thoracoabdominal penetration. The systematic review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 principles and included explicit study identification, screening, and eligibility assessment. Integrating these sources enabled a field-oriented approach to assessment and management, emphasizing early hemorrhage control, broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy, appropriate use of imaging, thorough surgical exploration when indicated, and region-specific tetanus or rabies prophylaxis. Together, these findings support clinicians working in remote or austere environments who may encounter these rare but potentially serious injuries.

Post-exposure prophylaxis, a series of shots, works well and saves lives. (SACRAMENTO) Rabies is something you probably don’t expect to encounter, but UC Davis Health experts say if you’re exposed, there’s little room for error. A recent advisory about increased rabies exposures nationwide has prompted a lot of questions about rabies exposure and management. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) warning said the use of rabies post-exposure prophylaxis, a series of shots, was up significantly over the summer in several states. “The reason that we care so much about rabies is that it’s nearly 100% fatal,” said Dean Blumberg, chief of pediatric infectious diseases at UC Davis Health. The good news, Blumberg noted, is that post-exposure prophylaxis is very effective and saves lives. But it’s critical to know what counts as an exposure and when you should seek medical care. What is rabies and how is it spread? Rabies is a viral disease that spreads mainly through bites or scratches from infected animals, most commonly bats in California. In other parts of the country, raccoons, skunks and foxes may also spread the disease. The old stereotype about rabies being spread by dogs is no longer accurate. Thanks to a strong pet vaccination program, the last known case of rabies transmitted by a dog to a human in California was more than 70 years ago, Blumberg said. Rabies in humans is rare. Fewer than 10 deaths are reported each year, according to the CDC. But it is found in wildlife nationwide (except Hawaii) so it’s important to know what to look for. Can you get rabies without being bitten? You can get rabies without being bitten. Scratches or exposure to oral secretions from an infected animal can also lead to infection. It is common in many parts of California to see bats flying around outside, especially at dusk. Blumberg said being outside and seeing them fly overhead does not count as an exposure. “The real concern is if somebody touches a bat or if they pick up a bat,” he explained. “Children are naturally curious and may come across a wounded, injured or dead bat and may pick it up, touch it or examine it. Even touching it would be considered a significant exposure.” Blumberg said that type of exposure poses a risk for infection and medical intervention would be needed. Should I worry about rabies if there is a bat in my house? Bats are the animals most likely to spread rabies in California. The concern with a bat in the house is whether anyone comes into contact with it or touches it. The goal would be to get the bat out of the house without touching it. But if you wake up and find a bat has been in the house while you are sleeping, experts say you may not know whether you’ve been exposed or not, which could be a concern. “The teeth are so small, that if people are asleep, for example, they may not even know that they were bitten by a bat,” Blumberg said. Even with a careful examination, it may not be clear that there was a bite, so if you wake up with a bat in the house, Blumberg said a primary care provider can help you determine next steps. When should I seek medical care after a bat encounter? If there is any chance that you’ve been exposed to secretions, a bite or scratch, or have touched the bat, contact your primary care provider. What should I do after a possible bat exposure? Do not touch the bat. Wash any bite or scratch with soap and water. Contact your primary care provider immediately. Do not wait for symptoms to develop before seeking care. What are the symptoms of rabies? You will want to seek medical care after a possible rabies exposure before any symptoms are present. Don't wait. There are no real symptoms at first, as it can take weeks to months for the rabies virus to travel to the central nervous system — to the brain, where infections can be potentially fatal. If it does infect the brain, then rabies causes encephalitis. People start off with a fever and very non-specific symptoms, but then eventually they become confused. They might become afraid of water, they might have seizures, they might become aggressive and eventually they end up in a coma and pass away. Seeking medical care as soon as you suspect you might be exposed is critical. Learn more about zoonotic diseases What is the care for possible rabies exposure? If you have a known or suspected rabies exposure, you should reach out to your primary care physician right away. They will likely also contact public health authorities to determine if wildlife in the area have tested positive for the virus. Typical care includes cleaning of the wound (if there is one) and a series of shots, including rabies immune globulin and vaccine, called post-exposure prophylaxis, or PEP. “If we know somebody has been bitten or scratched or exposed, then we can prevent it from infecting the brain by giving post-exposure prophylaxis,” Blumberg explained. Post-exposure prophylaxis is highly effective. You may remember hearing stories about a long series of painful rabies shots, but Blumberg noted that things have changed. “The vaccine has been improved and there are fewer doses and fewer side effects, so that should not deter someone from getting the injections if they have been exposed,” he said. About 100,000 Americans are treated for possible rabies exposure each year, according to the CDC. The bottom line about rabies Rabies is nearly always fatal without intervention, but post-exposure prophylaxis, a series of shots delivered after exposure, is effective and readily available. If you think you’ve been exposed to rabies through contact with a bat or other animal, contact your primary care provider right away. Health & Fitness Rabies Cases Prompt Nationwide Health Advisory: What Long Island Residents Should Know Reports of people exposed to rabid or possibly rabid animals are rising across the country. Here's where Long Island cases stand. LONG ISLAND, NY — Reports of people exposed to rabid or possibly rabid animals are rising across the country, and health officials say it’s important that Long Island residents know what to do after a bite or scratch to prevent a nearly always fatal disease. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention said in a Sept. 10 health advisory that it received 17 percent more rabies-related inquiries in July and August compared with the same time last year. At least eight state health departments also reported increased use of preventive treatment, errors in administering it, or both. The agency did not name the eight states. The federal advisory comes amid a continuing resurgence of terrestrial rabies in Suffolk County. As recently as November 2025, cases were largely concentrated in western Suffolk, with rabid animals reported in Amityville, Deer Park, Lindenhurst and Wyandanch. Since then, cases have been confirmed in communities including Melville, Islip, Bay Shore, Babylon, Northport, Kings Park and Smithtown. Suffolk County's most recent case was confirmed Sept. 18, when a raccoon found outside a Smithtown home tested positive for rabies. A pet dog may have been exposed but was current on its rabies vaccination and received a booster, officials said. No human exposures were reported. That animal marked the county's 27th terrestrial rabies case of 2026 and 46th since Feb. 7, 2025. Find out what's happening in Long Islandfor free with the latest updates from Patch. Days earlier, a Babylon resident came into contact with an ill raccoon that later tested positive for rabies. The resident sought medical attention and was reported to be in good health. Another rabid raccoon was found dead on a Bay Shore property during the same period. Those animals became Suffolk County's 43rd and 44th confirmed terrestrial rabies cases since February 2025. A raccoon found at Sunken Meadow State Park in Kings Park also tested positive this month. A park employee found the apparently ill animal Sept. 11 before it died a short time later. No human or pet exposures were reported. Other recent cases have been confirmed in Northport, Islip and Bay Shore, continuing a geographic spread that health officials have been tracking since terrestrial rabies reemerged in the county last year. In April, a rabid raccoon found near Cranberry Court in Melville became the first reported rabies case in the Town of Huntington during the current outbreak. The resurgence initially appeared concentrated farther west. By November 2025, Suffolk County had confirmed 15 terrestrial rabies cases, including nine in Amityville, three in Deer Park, two in Lindenhurst and one in Wyandanch. Fourteen involved raccoons and one involved a feral cat. Before 2025, Suffolk County had not reported a locally acquired terrestrial rabies case since 2009, according to county health officials. Knowing what to do is vital as reports of rabid or potentially rabid animals increase across the country, according to the CDC. Incidents have involved multiple people and animals that are both commonly and less commonly associated with rabies. Rabies is nearly always fatal after symptoms begin. It is preventable, however, when treatment is given promptly and correctly after an exposure. Here’s what Long Island residents should know: Which Animals Pose A Risk? Rabies is a viral disease that attacks the central nervous system. An infected animal can spread it through bites, scratches or saliva that enters an open wound or the eyes, nose or mouth. Wildlife that commonly carry rabies in the United States include bats, raccoons, skunks and foxes, but any mammal can become infected. Nearly 300 people were exposed to goats with rabies at a petting zoo in North Carolina earlier this summer. A person generally cannot tell whether an animal has rabies simply by looking at it. Do This Immediately Anyone bitten or scratched by wildlife or an animal acting strangely should immediately wash the wound with soap and water, or water alone, for 15 minutes. The CDC recommends promptly contacting a medical provider or public health official for an assessment. The type of animal, its behavior, whether it can be tested or observed, and the nature of the contact all help determine whether treatment is needed. People should not try to capture a potentially rabid animal unless they can do so safely. Local animal control or health officials can assist. What’s The Treatment? Post-exposure prophylaxis, or PEP, treatment includes rabies vaccines and immune globulin for unvaccinated individuals. Though rabies treatments are not in short supply, improper administration leads to side effects, wasted resources, and steep costs averaging $11,000 to $14,000 per patient. Around 100,000 Americans receive PEP annually. Common errors include incorrect injection sites, improper schedules, inadequate wound care, and unnecessary immunoglobulin administration. Pharmacy records through Sept. 2 show a 33 percent increase in rabies vaccine usage and a 76 percent surge in immune globulin usage compared to last year. How To Reduce Risk The same human rabies vaccine can be given before exposure, but it is generally recommended only for people at higher risk, such as veterinarians, animal-control workers, rabies laboratory workers and some travelers. This pre-exposure vaccination does not eliminate the need for treatment after a possible exposure. A previously vaccinated person still needs two follow-up vaccine doses but generally does not need rabies immune globulin. The CDC recommends keeping pets current on rabies vaccinations and away from wildlife whenever possible. Anyone whose pet comes into contact with wildlife or a sick animal should contact a veterinarian and local public health or animal control officials for guidance.
