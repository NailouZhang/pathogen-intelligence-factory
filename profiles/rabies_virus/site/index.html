侧流装置可以加速狂犬病病毒 基因组监测

Lateral flow devices can fast-track rabies virus genomic surveillance
作者： Bea Crisostomo, Criselda T. Bautista, Mary Elizabeth Miranda, Daria Llenaresas MANALO, Shynie Vee. M. Telmo, Gian Rei Mangcucang, Sheryl Pablo, Michael Ralph Tanedo, Eleanor M. Rees, Sandeep Kasaragod
摘要中文翻译病毒基因组数据可以解决狂犬病 传播并指导暴发响应,但监测通常受限于从偏远采集点获取合适样本到实验室的物流挑战,而非测序能力,这一过程可能需要数月。在这里,我们展示了侧流装置(LFDs)用于狂犬病 诊断时,也可以作为基因组样本使用,将诊断和基因组采样整合到单一工作流程中,无需额外样本采集。我们评估了LFDs和FTA卡片在实验室和现场条件下对狂犬病病毒 基因组的回收效果。在菲律宾MIMAROPA群岛的监测期间,Bionote LFD对金标准直接荧光抗体检测(n = 319)的敏感度为99.1%,特异度为100%,采样后1天内即可获得结果,而DFAT确认需要5天。在模拟现场储存条件的实验室条件下(常温或37摄氏度储存最多21天),残留的LFD材料和FTA卡片均支持近乎完整的基因组测序,尽管储存时间越长,基因组回收率下降,尤其是在高温条件下。与此一致的是,现场采集的LFD在常温储存23天内可获得近乎完整的基因组,但在29-77天后仅能获得部分基因组,表明其实际使用窗口约为三周。将已嵌入诊断流程中的LFD重新利用,可以实现无需额外样本采集或冷链的去中心化基因组监测。FTA卡片提供了互补的保存选项,而进一步优化样本制备、提取和储存方法,可以提高基因组的回收率并延长测序窗口。

Lateral flow devices can fast-track rabies virus genomic surveillance

Authors: Bea Crisostomo, Criselda T. Bautista, Mary Elizabeth Miranda, Daria Llenaresas MANALO, Shynie Vee. M. Telmo, Gian Rei Mangcucang, Sheryl Pablo, Michael Ralph Tanedo, Eleanor M. Rees, Sandeep Kasaragod
Original abstract (English)
Viral genomic data can resolve rabies transmission and guide outbreak response, but surveillance is often limited less by sequencing capacity than by getting suitable specimens from remote collection sites to a laboratory, a process that can take months. Here, we show that lateral flow devices (LFDs) used for rabies diagnosis can also serve as genomic specimens, integrating diagnosis and genomic sampling into a single workflow without additional specimen collection. We evaluated LFDs and FTA cards for rabies virus genome recovery under laboratory and field conditions. During surveillance across the island provinces of MIMAROPA, Philippines, the Bionote LFD had 99.1% sensitivity and 100% specificity against the gold-standard direct fluorescent antibody test (n = 319), with results available a median of 1 day after sampling versus 5 days for DFAT confirmation. Under laboratory conditions simulating field storage (ambient temperature or 37 degrees Celsius for up to 21 days), both residual LFD material and FTA cards supported near-complete genome sequencing, although genome recovery declined with storage duration, particularly at elevated temperatures. Consistent with this, field-collected LFDs yielded near-complete genomes up to 23 days of ambient storage but only partial genomes after 29-77 days, indicating a practical window of around three weeks for use. Repurposing LFDs already embedded in diagnostic workflows offers a practical route to decentralised genomic surveillance without additional sample collection or a cold chain. FTA cards provide a complementary preservation option, while further optimisation of specimen preparation, extraction and storage could improve genome recovery and extend the sequencing window.

Pasture area increases livestock rabies risk in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil: a geospatial analysis of neurological syndrome cases (2020–2024)

Authors: Rafael Veríssimo Monteiro, Renata Falcão Rabello da Costa, Leda Maria Silva Kimura, Glenda Ribeiro de Oliveira, Natiane Rodrigues Viana, Alessandro de Sá Guimarães, Márcio Roberto Silva, Guilherme Nunes de Souza
Original abstract (English)
Abstract Animal rabies poses a risk to livestock in Brazil due to transmission by hematophagous bats. The Brazilian sanitary surveillance system mandates rabies testing for any livestock exhibiting signs of neurological syndrome. This geospatial, retrospective, longitudinal, observational study analyzed rabies test results for 204 cattle and 56 horses presenting with neurological syndrome in Rio de Janeiro State, Brazil, from 2020 to 2024. We calculated correlations and linear models between rabies results and municipal land-use types, as well the population of herbivores. Most neurological syndrome cases and positive rabies results occurred in cattle. The final linear model for the number of neurological syndrome cases and positive rabies results incorporated pasture area, shrub area and cattle population. Pasture area was positively correlated with higher numbers ( p = 0.011 and p = 0.005, respectively). Original abstract (English)
Abstract Objective Describe the epidemiologic landscape of rabies and rabies testing in the US during 2024. Methods Animal rabies testing results from approximately 130 public health, agriculture, and academic laboratories are collected by the US National Rabies Surveillance System (NRSS) each month. Data reported in 2024 were analyzed to examine temporal and geographic trends in animal rabies cases by rabies virus variant (RVV). Results In 2024, 3,834 cases of animal rabies were reported to the NRSS (4.2% of 90,343 samples submitted), representing a 2.0% increase from 2023. Of positive samples, 331 (8.6%) were domestic animals, and 3,503 (91.4%) were wildlife. The most commonly found rabid domestic animal was cats (243 positive [1.3% positivity]), and the most commonly found wildlife were bats (1,376 positive [5.7% positivity]) followed by raccoons (1,080 [10.1% positivity]). Two nonenzootic RVVs gained entry into the US in 2024. Four people died from rabies, including 2 who experienced bat exposures that were left untreated and 1 person whose case involved international travel. Conclusions The expansion of the historical range of enzootic RVVs can lead to substantial public health and animal health burden. Domestically acquired human rabies cases in 2024 underscore the need for continual public education on the risks of rabies exposures to ensure timely and effective administration of postexposure prophylaxis. The internationally acquired human rabies case demonstrates that the US remains vulnerable to human and animal rabies importations. Clinical Relevance The NRSS plays a crucial role in protecting public and animal health by monitoring rabies trends in the US.

Original abstract (English)
The experimental application of canine adipose-derived mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (cAD-MSC) has demonstrated excellent therapeutic potential in the treatment of a number of previously untreatable or difficult-to-treat diseases. However, the production of cAD-MSC for clinical application necessitates in vitro isolation and cell expansion, introducing the risk of microbiological contamination. Studies indicate that stored MSCs may be contaminated with bacteria, fungi, or viruses, resulting in cell transformation, cell death, or persistent infection. This ultimately compromises therapeutic efficacy and potentially facilitates therapy-induced pathogen transmission. In accordance with the European Medicines Agency guidelines mandating sterility testing as an integral component of the manufacturing process, this study aimed to assess the sterility of the cAD-MSC bank at the Croatian Veterinary Institute. To test for the most clinically significant canine pathogens, nucleic acids were extracted from 32 cAD-MSC preparations and analyzed using standard molecular PCR methods. The pathogen screening panel included rabies virus (Lyssavirus), canine herpesvirus (Varicellovirus canidalpha1), parvovirus (Carnivore protoparvovirus 1), coronavirus (Alphacoronavirus 1), distemper virus (Morbillivirus canis), Rotavirus A, Leptospira spp, Brucella canis, Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato, Anaplasma spp, Ehrlichia spp, Mycoplasma spp (hemotropic), and Neospora caninum. No tested gene fragments of the aforementioned pathogens were detected, indicating that the cAD-MSC preparations were free from the presence of clinically significant pathogens. Rigorous implementation of sterility control during the cell isolation and expansion process is essential to ensure safe cell-based therapy in canine patients.

Rabies reports are rising in California. The reason why is a mystery

News Brief
Rabies reports are increasing in California, with nine rabid bats identified in Santa Cruz County this year, up from three in the previous two years. The rise is unexplained, but public health officials advise vaccination of pets and caution against approaching wild animals, especially if they appear sick or injured. Nationally, rabies inquiries after potential exposure are up 17% this year.
Original source text (English)
Rabies reports are up across the United States this year, including in one greater Bay Area county where human exposures to rabid animals have tripled, prompting a local advisory to stay away from sick or injured creatures. Scientists can't yet explain the uptick in rabies, which could be due to climate change, more awareness among the public or some unknown factor. Nationally, the number of rabies inquiries after a potential exposure is up 17% this year over last, and use of post-exposure prophylaxis also has increased, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. In Santa Cruz County, authorities have identified nine rabid bats so far this year. Last year and the year before, they recorded three, and in 2023, just one. In each case, at least one individual was exposed to the bat and needed treatment. "I don't find this increase alarming, but I do want to make people aware," said Dr. Maris Brenn-White, veterinarian for the Santa Cruz County Animal Shelter. "I want them to know that rabies is scary and we think there's more of it, but just don't touch wildlife and vaccinate your pets, and you don't need to worry about it." Rabies is rare but almost always fatal once a person develops symptoms; fewer than 10 Americans die of it every year. When someone does survive, it makes headlines . The disease once was spread mostly through dogs, but domestic dogs are now required by law to be vaccinated almost everywhere in the U.S., so most human cases are from bat bites or scratches; among pets, cats are much more likely than dogs to be infected. Last year, California reported more than 300 rabid bats, plus 19 skunks and a few foxes and cats. Statewide, there were 342 reports of rabid animals last year, and 368 so far this year. In the Bay Area, there were 48 rabies exposures reported last year and 47 so far this year, almost all of them wild animals but at least one domestic cat in Alameda County . The illness is caused by a virus that spreads via saliva. The virus attacks the nervous system, eventually getting into the brain. It causes widespread damage, eventually disabling basic body functions like breathing and swallowing. The incubation period can be weeks, and early symptoms include fever, headache and fatigue; once a person feels ill, they almost certainly will die. Post-exposure prophylaxis, though, is incredibly effective, and almost everyone who is treated will avoid illness. Treatment includes the rabies vaccine, which takes a couple of weeks to kick in, so patients also are given rabies immunoglobulin, a fast-acting antibody that provides immediate protection. The shots can be painful when they're injected directly into a wound, but they're not as awful as they once were, infectious disease experts said. "Rabies is endemic to California and the United States, so it's something always in the background," said Dr. Matt Willis, former Marin County health officer who now writes a Substack on public health statewide called Your Local Epidemiologist . He said typically about 10% of bats tested in Marin County are positive for rabies year after year, "so we know that we have this perpetual reservoir of this lethal virus very close by." "Sometimes it's actually surprising we're not seeing more human cases," he said. "But that has a lot to do with the fact that the animals that carry rabies are not in frequent contact with people." The rise in rabies reports is concerning but not yet alarming, Willis and other infectious disease experts said. They noted that rabies is rare enough that the increases reported so far this year are small and may be a one-off event. Perhaps, they said, cases will go down again next year. But most likely, there is something happening that is increasing exposures, several infectious disease experts said. Climate change could be a factor: Warm weather is known to increase viral reproduction, for example, so maybe there's more rabies virus in circulation. Bats may also reproduce more during hotter weather, which could lead to more human interactions. "I'm not going to pretend to have an answer" to the role of climate change on rabies, Willis said. "But I think these are the kinds of signals we need to pay attention to, because we honestly don't know all the ways that climate contributes to changes in our environment." Bat exposures may be up simply because people are spending more time outdoors, public health experts said. Or people may just be more aware of infectious diseases in general, said Dr. Monica Gandhi, a UCSF infectious disease expert. The COVID-19 pandemic made many people more sensitive to the role of pathogens in the community, she said. And then this year, several outbreaks of infectious diseases have made headlines: hantavirus , Ebola , cyclospora . "I definitely think there's a component of 'we're awash in infectious disease,'" Gandhi said. "It could be that people are just getting more worried. It's a very scary time for infectious disease." Rabies, too, has been in the news more this year with several reports of unusual encounters between rabid animals and humans. Rabid baby goats were reported at a North Carolina petting zoo attended by hundreds of people this summer. A Virginia woman woke up with a bat in her mouth in August. Last month, a rabid beaver attacked a child in Maryland. In August, two people were attacked, in separate incidents, by an otter while swimming in Lake Tahoe. The animals were not found, so it's not known if they were rabid, but both individuals had to get rabies shots. Each of the incidents alone is not necessarily striking, but to have them all in one summer is curious, infectious disease experts said. Still, said Dr. Dean Winslow, a Stanford infectious disease expert, "I'd be more concerned if there was a dramatic increase in exposure from cats and dogs." He noted that rabies surveillance among wildlife isn't great in the United States, so it's hard to say for sure how much rabies is truly circulating among bats or other animals. People are most commonly exposed to rabies through bats, which can bite or scratch someone without the person knowing it. One scenario is waking up with a bat in the bedroom - that is cause for concern, infectious disease experts said, and people should contact a doctor and animal control in that case. Mostly, though, avoiding rabies comes down to common sense. Pets should be vaccinated, both dogs and cats. Dogs should be leashed and kept at least 150 feet from sick wildlife, public health officials said. Never approach wild animals, especially if they seem sick or injured. Bats are nocturnal, so seeing one in the daytime would be cause for concern. Also, a bat that cannot fly or is simply sitting or lying on the ground is likely not healthy. According to Santa Cruz public health officials, if someone comes across a downed bat, they should never touch it. They should put on gloves and use a broom or similar tool to nudge the animal and determine if it is alive or dead. If the animal is alive but can't fly away, cover it with a box or bucket and contact local animal control. If the animal is dead, push it into a box or bucket with the broom and cover it, then take it to animal control or call to have it removed. Some counties, including Santa Cruz, also offer a free bat home inspection, which they especially recommend for homes with a history of finding bats indoors. "We don't want to scare people" by talking about the increase in rabies exposures, said Dr. Lisa Hernandez, the Santa Cruz County public health officer. "We just want to remind people of some common sense things, like don't touch a dead or hurt wild animal and make sure your dogs and cats are vaccinated." The incidents were reported across the Hunter Region, the Mid North Coast, northern NSW, and the Central Coast. Australian bat lyssavirus, a virus related to rabies, can cause a fatal illness in humans. There is no effective treatment once symptoms begin, although vaccination after exposure can help prevent disease. "We want people to assume that any bat could carry Australian bat lyssavirus, which is a close relative to the rabies virus and very dangerous to humans," said Keira Glasgow, director of health protection at NSW Health. "It is incredibly rare for Australian bat lyssavirus to transmit to humans, but once symptoms start in people who are scratched or bitten by an infected bat, sadly there's no effective treatment," she said. "If you are bitten or scratched by a bat, urgent medical assessment is crucial." People who are bitten or scratched should immediately wash the wound with soap and water for 15 minutes, apply an antiseptic with antiviral action, and seek urgent medical attention. The warning comes as wildlife experts report rising bat activity linked to food shortages. "Consequently members of the community are seeing flying foxes remaining in their backyard trees during the day," Ingrid Emery, executive director of conservation programmes at the Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water, said, according to Nine. "This food shortage event is occurring along the eastern coast from the Clarence Valley in the north to the South Coast." lyssavirus is found in bats, including flying foxes, and is distinct from rabies, which isn't found in the country. The infection can cause flu-like symptoms, including fever and headache, before progressing to paralysis, delirium and seizures. Death usually occurs within a week or two of symptoms developing. Symptoms typically begin about a week after exposure but can sometimes take years to appear. The breakthrough is designed to help improve surveillance of one of the world's deadliest infectious diseases and deliver practical, laboratory-ready tools that can be adopted in endemic countries. The research, published in PLOS Neglected Tropical Diseases, found that 86.1 percent of bite victims delayed initiating post-exposure vaccination beyond the critical 48-hour window, a figure that public health experts describe as alarmingly high even by the standards of rabies-endemic regions. The study, led by Bezawit Kassaw Hailu and colleagues, set out to quantify how long victims of dog bites wait before beginning post-exposure vaccination, known clinically as PEV, and to identify the factors that influence that timing. Between January and June 2025, the researchers conducted face-to-face interviews with 260 dog bite victims or their guardians across the Gondar zones. The team defined delayed initiation as beginning vaccination more than 48 hours after the exposure incident, a threshold grounded in the biology of the rabies virus, which travels along peripheral nerves toward the central nervous system and can reach the brain before protective immunity develops if prophylaxis is postponed. A majority of respondents, 88.5 percent, were aware that rabies exists in their area. Some 71.5 percent could identify the signs of the disease, 75.4 percent knew how it is transmitted, and an overwhelming 96.5 percent understood its fatal nature. Even more encouraging, 95.4 percent recognized the need for post-exposure vaccination after a bite. Knowledge of the disease itself, in other words, was widespread and remarkably detailed for a rural setting where health literacy resources are often scarce. Attitudes toward the vaccine were similarly favorable. A majority of respondents believed the cost of the vaccine was fair, and 95 percent considered post-exposure prophylaxis effective. When the researchers probed the reasons for delay, cost of vaccination emerged as a barrier for only a minority of participants; 76.2 percent said the expense was not a reason they postponed treatment. This combination of high awareness, positive attitudes toward vaccination, and minimal financial barriers presents a genuine scientific puzzle: if victims know the disease is deadly, know the vaccine works, and do not cite cost as an obstacle, why do so few seek timely care? The authors’ conclusion points toward a critical gap in understanding. Respondents, they suggest, may simply not be aware of the risk posed by delay itself. Knowing that rabies kills and knowing that a vaccine exists are not the same as knowing that the effectiveness of that vaccine is time-sensitive, and that the difference between seeking care on the day of the bite and two days later can be the difference between survival and death. Health messaging in endemic regions has traditionally emphasized the lethality of rabies and the availability of treatment, but this study indicates that the urgency of timing has not been communicated with comparable force. Using logistic regression models, the researchers identified two factors significantly associated with the timing of vaccination initiation. The first was the severity of the bite. Victims who suffered deep bites were substantially less likely to delay, with an odds ratio of 0.22 and a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.07 to 0.66, and a P value of 0.008. In practical terms, a deep bite more than halved the odds of postponement compared with superficial wounds. This pattern is consistent with the intuitive logic of threat perception: the more dramatic and painful the injury, the more urgently the victim recognizes the danger and seeks medical attention. People bitten by stray dogs were also less likely to delay initiation of vaccination, with an odds ratio of 0.51, a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.27 to 0.95, and a P value of 0.033. This finding may seem counterintuitive at first glance, since stray dogs cannot be observed for signs of illness the way a neighbor’s vaccinated pet can. But it makes sense in the context of risk assessment: a bite from an unknown, unowned animal carries unmistakable uncertainty about the animal’s rabies status, prompting swifter action, whereas a bite from a familiar dog may lull victims into a false sense of security, encouraging them to wait and watch the animal for symptoms that may never become obvious before the virus has already taken hold. The implications of these findings extend well beyond the Gondar zones. Rabies remains one of the most lethal zoonotic diseases worldwide, claiming an estimated tens of thousands of lives annually, with the vast majority of deaths occurring in Asia and Africa and with children in rural communities bearing a disproportionate burden. The virus is a lyssavirus transmitted through the saliva of infected mammals, most commonly dogs, and it hijacks peripheral nerve fibers to reach the central nervous system. Once neurological symptoms such as hydrophobia, agitation, and paralysis begin, no treatment has ever been shown to halt the disease. Post-exposure prophylaxis, which combines wound washing, rabies vaccine doses, and in severe cases rabies immunoglobulin, works because it stimulates immunity during the days or weeks the virus spends traveling from the wound site to the brain. That window, however, is finite, and every study that documents delay in initiation helps explain why deaths persist in regions where vaccines are nominally available. The Ethiopian study’s authors conclude that the high rate of delay, despite widespread knowledge and few reported barriers, underscores the need for interventions focused specifically on the risk of delayed PEV initiation. Such interventions might include community education campaigns that emphasize the 48-hour benchmark as explicitly as they emphasize the disease’s fatality, integration of bite management guidance into school and community health programs, and strengthening of access points so that victims can reach vaccination services quickly when the urgency is understood. The research also highlights the value of targeting messaging toward owners of domestic dogs, who may underestimate risk when the biting animal is familiar. As the authors note, respondents in their study knew many aspects of rabies control and faced fewer barriers to uptake than might have been expected, yet delay remained the norm rather than the exception. Closing that gap between knowledge and timely action may prove to be one of the most important steps toward reducing rabies deaths in Ethiopia and across the endemic world.
