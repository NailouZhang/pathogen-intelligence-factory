Identification of a novel conserved B-cell epitope in the glycoprotein of rabies virus.

Authors: Yumei Chen, Xiaohui Zhang, Yanhua Qi, Hongliang Liu, Enping Liu, Xifang Zhu, Jingming Zhou, Chao Liang, Sixuan Wu, Aiping Wang
Original abstract (English)
Rabies virus (RABV), a single-stranded RNA virus, invades the central nervous system and causes acute, zoonotic diseases worldwide. The glycoprotein (G) of RABV is the only surface antigen responsible for inducing potent neutralizing antibodies, making it a key target for medical countermeasures. In this study, two anti-G protein monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) were prepared using hybridoma cell technology, with their high specificity and robust reactivity validated by indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (i-ELISA), indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) and Western blot. Through systematic epitope mapping using a series of truncated G protein fragments, two mAbs (1G2 and 4E5) were found to recognize a minimal linear epitope, AEDFVEVHLP (amino acids 412-421) through Dot-ELISA and i-ELISA. Alanine-scanning mutagenesis further defined the core binding residues within this epitope as 412 Ala-Glu-Asp-Phe-Val 416 , 418 Val, 420 Leu, and 421 Pro. Bioinformatic analysis with MEGA software revealed highly conserved with conservative substitutions of this epitope across diverse RABV of genotype 1. Homology modeling with SWISS-MODEL localized the epitope " 412 AEDFVEVHLP 421 " to a solvent-exposed α-helical conformation on the G protein surface. And this epitope is located in the extracellular C-terminal region, Prior studies have demonstrated that this region plays a role in stabilizing the trimerization of the rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG). In conclusion, our study identifies a novel, highly conserved with conservative substitutions linear B-cell epitope on the RABV glycoprotein, providing a precise molecular target for the rational design of next-generation epitope-based vaccines and specific diagnostic tools.

Immunogenicity evaluation of recombinant rabies virus-vectored multivalent vaccines expressing feline calicivirus VP1 and feline herpesvirus 1 gB/gD genes.

Authors: Yueze Wang, Songze Zhao, Hongling He, Xiaolong Li, Yinyi Liang, Jiahao Wang, Lerong Xin, Nuan Chen, Wanxing Xie, Shile Huang
Original abstract (English)
ABSTRACT Feline calicivirus (FCV) and feline herpesvirus 1 (FHV-1) are major pathogens threatening feline health, and prophylactic immunity remains the primary preventive approach. To develop a safe and effective multivalent vaccine against the two viruses, two recombinant rabies virus (RABV) vaccines expressing the FCV VP1 and FHV-1 gB/gD genes were constructed. The exogenous genes showed stable inheritance following serial viral passages, and the recombinant RABVs exhibited growth kinetics comparable to the parental strain, with a delayed peak titer. After verifying the viral pathogenicity, the recombinant RABV vaccines elicited specific IgG antibodies against VP1, gB, and gD, as well as neutralizing antibodies against FCV, FHV-1, and RABV in mice. Immunized mice were also protected against lethal RABV challenge. In cats, the vaccines induced neutralizing antibodies against the three pathogens, and immunized cats showed milder clinical signs and less severe histopathological lesions following FHV-1 and FCV challenge. Collectively, these findings indicate that the two recombinant vaccines induce robust humoral immune responses against FCV, FHV-1, and RABV in both mice and cats and confer protection against viral challenges.

Mathematical analysis and passive control strategies for a fractional-order dog-to-human rabies model in danger zones

Authors: A. Anandharaj, K. Malar, S. Divya, Bijender Singh
Original abstract (English)
Abstract Rabies is an invariably fatal zoonosis and a serious public health problem among dogs and humans within the danger zones due to large concentrations of stray dog populations. In order to provide insights into the transmission dynamics of the disease between dogs and humans, we present in this paper a new fractional-order SEIVQRD compartmental model using Caputo derivative. While in most traditional SEIR models it is difficult to investigate the role of control interventions, we explicitly incorporate the roles of various passive control methods such as mass vaccination of dogs and human post and pre exposure prophylaxis. The use of fractional calculus allows one to account for the nonlocal properties and hereditary effects in the highly variable incubation period of the virus. We prove some important properties of the model such as positivity and boundedness of solutions and calculation of the basic reproduction number of the canine population ( $$R_{0d}$$ ). It is proved that the disease free equilibrium is globally asymptotically stable when $$R_{0d} . Moreover, through normalization of sensitivity analysis, it is found that canine vaccination is the most dominant parameter for controlling the epidemic threshold and hence preventing human death. Lastly, numerical simulation through Adams Bashforth Moulton predictor corrector method has been done to prove the theoretical results.

Authors: Rabera OGEGA, Odhiambo O. TOBIAS, Evelyn MWIHIA, Caleb ORENGE, Gilbert Kirui
Original abstract (English)
Free-roaming dogs create public health, environmental, and animal welfare challenges, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. In Kenya, dogmediated rabies remains an important public health concern. Understanding community perceptions can inform locally appropriate dog population management within a One Health framework. A cross-sectional survey was conducted among 106 eligible respondents in Ongata Rongai, Kajiado County, using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire. Descriptive statistics summarized respondent characteristics and perceptions. Associations between demographic characteristics and dichotomized perception outcomes were assessed using Pearson's chi-square test or Fisher's exact test where expected cell counts were small; Bonferroni adjustment was applied across 16 demographic comparisons. Results showed that respondents strongly supported community education on responsible dog ownership (95.3%), partnerships with animal welfare organizations and the local community (93.4%), increased county government investment (93.4%), and spaying and neutering (93.4%). Proper dog ownership was the most frequently selected single management option (42.5%). Most respondents perceived free-roaming dogs as sources of fear and insecurity (95.3%), environmental contamination (85.8%), disease or rabies transmission (82.1%), and dog-bite risk (76.4%). No demographic association remained statistically significant after Bonferroni adjustment. Respondents favored humane, integrated approaches to free-roaming dog management and perceived stronger public health and welfare concerns. Responsible ownership, community education, vaccination, reproductive control, and multisectoral collaboration warrant consideration in locally appropriate programs for managing dog populations.

RABIES TREATMENT CASCADE, SURVEILLANCE GAPS, POST-EXPOSURE PROPHYLAXIS ADHERENCE AND HEALTH-SYSTEM BARRIERS IN THE NIGER DELTA REGION OF NIGERIA: A SCOPING REVIEW

Authors: A. G. Essiet, A.A. Gordon, E.E. Offiong, I.P Etukudo, N. F. Masok, I.C. Nwaopara, I.O. Usuanlele, O.O. Ajibola, A.W Ime
Original abstract (English)
Rabies remains a fatal yet vaccine-preventable zoonosis, with Nigeria accounting for an estimated 1,600-10,000 human deaths annually. Despite effective vaccines, weak surveillance systems, inadequate laboratory confirmation, and poor health-seeking behaviour obscure the true disease burden, particularly in southern states such as Akwa Ibom. Rabies remains an almost entirely preventable but near-uniformly fatal zoonosis, and dog-mediated transmission continues to impose a disproportionate burden on low-resource settings, with Nigeria ranked among the countries with the highest absolute rabies mortality worldwide Within Nigeria, the Niger Delta region presents a distinct combination of riverine geographic fragmentation, documented veterinary and surveillance capacity gaps, and health-system constraints that may shape a treatment cascade different from that described elsewhere in the country, yet no prior review has systematically synthesized this evidence. This scoping review, incorporating systematic elements, sought to consolidate and critically appraise available evidence on the rabies treatment cascade, surveillance gaps, post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) adherence, and health-system barriers within the Niger Delta region of Nigeria. The review followed the Arksey and O'Malley framework and reported in accordance with the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). The evidence was charted across the population-concept-context (PCC) domains of rabies-exposed individuals, treatment-cascade and surveillance outcomes, and the Niger Delta setting, drawing on peer-reviewed literature, WHO and NCDC programmatic sources, and comparator studies from analogous Nigerian states. The results as evidenced, indicates a treatment cascade that leaks at multiple points rather than at a single stage: unprovoked bites disproportionately affecting children, inconsistent formal care-seeking, and highly variable PEP completion, including a documented instance in which no sampled Cross River victims completed a full regimen. Surveillance in the region has historically been passive and fragmented, with systematic real-time tracking only introduced in Cross River State in 2022. Recurrent barriers included PEP cost, facility distance, and workforce and community knowledge gaps. The Niger Delta's rabies burden appears sustained less by any single point of failure than by the absence of coordinated human-animal-environmental surveillance and financing structures. Emerging One Health coordination mechanisms piloted elsewhere in Nigeria offer a transferable, though yet unproven, template for the region.

Errors in administering rabies treatment include misclassifying vaccination history, incorrect schedules, and unnecessary restarts. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention issued a “Health Alert Network Health Advisory” in response to a recent uptick in human exposure to rabies or rabid animals. Rabies is a viral disease that is practically always fatal but preventable when the proper treatment is given before the patient shows symptoms. There were 17% more cases of “rabies-related inquiries” during July and August than there were during the same period last year, according to the CDC. The agency also reported a rise in “mass exposure” cases where more than one person was exposed to rabies or a rabid animal. Recently, nearly 300 people may have been exposed to rabies after interacting with goats at a mobile petting zoo in North Carolina. Three baby goats tested positive for rabies, while another goat was not tested but presumed to have rabies, according to the North Carolina State Laboratory of Public Health. A suspected rabid animal is believed to have attacked people in Lebanon, New Hampshire. There have been three reported attacks, with witnesses reporting the animal appeared to be unusually aggressive. Witnesses described the animal as either a gray fox or a fisher, but officials cannot confirm what it is. In Maryland, a large part of Cunningham Falls State Park was shut down in early August after a 19-year-old fisherman was attacked by a beaver that later tested positive for rabies. The attack was the second of its kind to happen at the park this summer. According to the Maryland Department of Natural Resources, a 13-year-old swimmer was attacked by a beaver on July 26. That beaver also tested positive for rabies. A dead bat found in a popular campground at Yosemite National Park tested positive for rabies, although the park said there was no reported human contact with the animal. The CDC has also seen a rise in the use of preventive rabies treatment known as post-exposure prophylaxis or PEP. According to the CDC, eight state health departments have reported an increase in the use of rabies PEP. The agency also reported that the use of two rabies vaccines rose by 33% in September 2026 compared to last year while the use of two human rabies immunoglobulins rose by 76%. Along with the rise in preventive rabies treatment, the CDC has seen a rise in errors when administering the treatment. The CDC said errors can occur when assessing if a patient needs PEP. For example, someone who has previously been vaccinated for rabies would not need a dose of human rabies immunoglobulins. According to the CDC, these errors can lead to unnecessary treatments, potential adverse effects and avoidable costs. A typical course of PEP treatment can range from $11,000 to $14,000 per person. “Following rabies PEP administration guidance helps ensure that lifesaving vaccines are available for persons who need it and extraneous costs are avoided,” the CDC said. Other errors reported during preventive treatment include: Administering rabies vaccine in the wrong area Administering HRIG and vaccine in the same syringe or anatomical site Forgetting to give HRIG to someone who needs it Misclassifying previous vaccination history or immune status Using an incorrect rabies vaccine schedule Unnecessarily restarting the rabies vaccine series after a schedule deviation In order to prevent exposure to rabies, the CDC advises the public to stay away from wildlife. The agency says never to approach an animal that appears to be sick or wounded and to report it to local animal control instead. The CDC also recommends keeping your pets up to date on their rabies vaccines as well as keeping them away from wildlife as much as possible. Should you or your pet come in contact with a wild or unknown animal, particularly if you or your pet have been scratched or bitten, the CDC recommends: Washing the wound immediately with soap and water for 15 minutes Telling your doctor or veterinarian what animal it was and whether it showed signs of behavior outside the norm Talking with a health professional about your rabies risk and if you will need treatment If the animal was not your own pet, asking for its owner to provide proof of its up-to-date rabies vaccination Capturing or confining the animal if it is safe to do so and contacting local animal control Copyright 2026 Gray Local Media, Inc. CDC Issues Health Alert Over Increase in Reported Human Rabies Exposures

News Brief
The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) issued a health alert on September 10, 2026, regarding a significant increase in reported human rabies exposures across the United States during the summer of 2026. This surge prompted the CDC to warn healthcare providers about the importance of proper treatment and administration of rabies post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP). The CDC noted that the use of rabies vaccines and human rabies immune globulin (HRIG) rose by 33% and 76%, respectively, compared to the same period last year. At least eight state health departments reported increased PEP use, treatment errors, or both. The CDC emphasized that while there is no national shortage of these treatments, unnecessary administration can lead to adverse effects, increased costs, and supply strain. The agency also highlighted that raccoons, skunks, and foxes remain major rabies threats, with 75% of Americans at risk. The CDC urged individuals who had contact with rabid animals to seek individual risk assessments, particularly in North Carolina where three baby goats tested positive for rabies and were linked to events involving close human interaction. The alert underscores the need for accurate PEP protocols to ensure effective protection and prevent waste of resources.

(Madalina Vasiliu/The Epoch Times) Reports of human rabies exposures have increased across the United States this summer, prompting health officials to warn doctors about proper treatment and administration. “During the summer of 2026, reports of animal exposures and rabies post-exposure vaccinations have increased across the country,” the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) said in a statement Sept. 10. Rabies is a zoonotic viral disease that is almost always fatal once symptoms develop. However, the CDC said it can be prevented if post-exposure prophylaxis is administered before symptoms appear. “Proper administration of rabies vaccine and human rabies immune globulin is critical to ensuring patients receive effective protection while avoiding unnecessary treatment, costs, and strain on supplies,” the CDC added. Use of the nation's two licensed rabies vaccines rose an estimated 33 percent compared with the same period last year, while use of human rabies immune globulin jumped 76 percent, according to weekly pharmacy data through Sept. 2, the CDC reported. The CDC also released separate updated guidance on rabies post-exposure prophylaxis on Sept. 10. The rabies vaccine helps the body develop immunity to the virus, and the human rabies immune globulin provides immediate protection until the body's immune response takes effect. The CDC's guidance noted, however: "People who have been previously vaccinated or are receiving pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) for rabies should not receive human rabies immune globulin (HRIG)." From July through August, the agency said it received 17 percent more rabies-related inquiries than during the same period last year. At least eight state health departments also reported increased use of rabies post-exposure prophylaxis, treatment errors, or both. The CDC said there is no national shortage of rabies vaccine or immune globulin, but says unnecessary treatment and mistakes can cause adverse effects, increase costs, and put pressure on supplies. “A typical course of PEP can cost between $11,000 and $14,000 per person,” the CDC stated. “Following rabies PEP administration guidance helps ensure that lifesaving vaccines are available for persons who need it and extraneous costs are avoided,” it added. Reported mistakes included injecting the vaccine in the buttocks, failing to place immune globulin around wounds, and administering both products at the same injection site. Other errors included omitting immune globulin when needed, giving it to previously vaccinated patients, and restarting vaccine series after a scheduling delay. Several outbreaks have been reported this year. In North Carolina, three baby goats from a mobile petting zoo tested positive for rabies Aug. 28, while a fourth was presumed infected, according to the state’s Department of Health and Human Services. The goats appeared at events in several counties from July 28 through Aug. 27, including an assisted-living facility and a back-to-school gathering. State officials said the animals were probably exposed after a skunk entered their pen. Because visitors may have bottle-fed, cuddled, or closely handled the goats, officials urged anyone with direct contact to seek an individual risk assessment. “Raccoons, skunks, and foxes continue to pose significant rabies threats to 75% of Americans,” the CDC states. Rabies can be transmitted from infected animals to humans and other animals through bites, scratches, or exposure to saliva. The virus primarily attacks the central nervous system, often resulting in brain inflammation and, if not treated promptly before symptoms appear, is almost always fatal. Each year, about 1.4 million people seek medical care after animal contact, and about 100,000 receive preventive rabies treatment, according to the CDC. The agency also reports roughly 4,000 rabid animals annually, more than 90 percent of them wildlife, such as in bats, raccoons, skunks, and foxes

(Photo by Smith Collection/Gado/Getty Images) The Brief The CDC is warning the public and health officials about an increase in animal rabies activity and human exposures. In the United States, people are usually exposed to rabies by coming in contact with wild animals infected with the virus. There are about 100,000 vaccinations due to potential rabies exposure in humans each year in the U.S. There has been an increase in rabies exposure this summer, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention warned on Tuesday. Rabies in humans What we know: Reports of animal exposures and rabies post-exposure vaccinations have increased across the U.S. What we don't know: The number of possible exposure cases reported this summer was not immediately shared. Big picture view: Rabies can be a fatal viral disease, but it is also very preventable. The CDC is advising all health care professionals and the public to be vigilant and to avoid contact with sick-acting mammals and wildlife. Rabies in the US By the numbers: Cases of potential exposure to rabies in humans in the U.S. amount to about 100,000 each year, according to the CDC. There are about 4,000 animal rabies cases reported each year. About 90% of those animal rabies cases occur in the wild like in bats, raccoons, skunks and foxes. Rabies primarily impacts the central nervous system and, if left untreated, can lead to severe brain disease and possibly death. The CDC advises anyone who may have been in contact with an animal that was acting out of the ordinary, to get checked by a health care professional for possible rabies exposure. Dig deeper: After a person is exposed to rabies, the types of symptoms they experience can be twofold. The first symptoms of rabies may include: Flu-like illness including weakness or discomfort, fever, or headache Discomfort, prickling, or an itching sensation at the site of the bite Usually, severe disease appears within 2 weeks of the first symptoms.
