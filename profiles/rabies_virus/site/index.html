Epigallocatechin gallate inhibits rabies virus glycoprotein‐mediated infection in vitro

Authors: Denis Pasqual, Maria Francesca Priore, Silvia Fabi, Yasaman Shamsizadeh, Maira Zorzan, Chiara Rampazzo, Marta Trevisan, Arianna Loregian, Claudia Del Vecchio, Paola De Benedictis
Original abstract (English)
Rabies is one of the most lethal zoonotic disease and causes approximately 60,000 human deaths annually. The high cost of immunoglobulin prophylaxis and the need for cold chain maintenance highlight the need for accessible antiviral strategies, particularly in resource-limited settings. Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), the major polyphenolic compound of Camellia sinensis, has broad-spectrum antiviral activity. We therefore investigated its activity against Rabies virus (RABV). Because RABV requires BSL-3 containment, we first used a recombinant Vesicular Stomatitis Virus pseudotyped with RABV glycoprotein (pRABV) as a surrogate. EGCG inhibited pRABV infection in a concentration-dependent manner, with an EC50 of approximately 0.15 μg/mL and a selectivity index > 551.72. Time-of-addition, attachment, and entry assays showed that EGCG reduced pRABV infectivity when present during viral adsorption or after pre-incubation with viral particles, whereas no inhibition was observed when cells were treated before infection or after virus internalization. Viral infectivity remained almost completely abolished in a virucidal assay even after EGCG was diluted below its effective antiviral concentration, supporting a direct and irreversible effect on viral particles. EGCG activity was further confirmed using authentic RABV and disease-relevant cell types, including myotubes and neurons. Overall, these findings indicate that EGCG inhibits early stages of the RABV replication cycle, primarily by interfering with viral attachment and directly affecting viral particles. Further studies are needed to define the molecular basis of this interaction and assess the therapeutic potential of EGCG against RABV.

Overexpression of nucleoprotein downregulates Rabies virus mRNA transcription

Authors: Luiza Barbosa, Joana Aguiar, Cesar Molina, Sueli Akemi Taniwaki, Paulo Eduardo Brandão
Original abstract (English)
Rabies is a fatal zoonotic disease of the central nervous system of mammals caused by Rabies lyssavirus (RABV), a member of the order Mononegavirales. Its single-stranded negative-sense RNA genome encodes five structural proteins: nucleoprotein (N), phosphoprotein (P), matrix protein (M), glycoprotein (G), and large protein (L). N plays a pivotal role in nucleocapsid assembly, regulation of viral transcription and replication, and the formation of Negri bodies, cytoplasmic viral factories essential for viral replication. Consequently, overexpression of N may disrupt RABV replicative dynamics, offering insight into the mechanisms governing its replication cycle while identifying potential targets for antiviral intervention. This study evaluated the effects of transient expression of RABV N mRNA on viral replication using a plasmid-based expression system in BHK-21 cells. BHK-21 cells were transfected with the pcDNA3.1(+)/C-DYK plasmid containing the N gene from the Challenge Virus Standard (CVS-11) strain of RABV. Viral mRNA transcription and total genomic viral load were quantified using absolute RT-qPCR. Overexpression n N mRNA led to a downward trend in viral mRNA transcription levels, although total viral genomic load remained largely unaffected. These findings provide further insight into the regulatory function of N during RABV replication and suggest that modulating N expression influences viral transcriptional dynamics.

Original abstract (English)
Pathogen adaptations to new hosts are central to the disease ecology of cross-species transmission and host switching of viruses. The rabies virus is a model for adaptations to different hosts as it induces behavioral changes that facilitate transmission, while being maintained in distinct species-specific cycles of infection. Here, we demonstrate that the rabies virus requires distinct transmission dynamics in two hosts with different ecologies to establish maintenance, using agent-based modeling, analyses of virus glycoprotein-mediated manipulation of neuroreceptor function, and phylogenetic analysis. Epidemiological models suggest that rabies virus variants adapted to arctic foxes, which sustain rabies virus at very low host densities, require a higher probability of transmission than viruses adapted to raccoons. Using in vitro electrophysiology, we demonstrate that the rabies glycoprotein-derived peptides from arctic fox rabies variants are more potent antagonists of alpha7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors. These findings suggest a possible functional basis for the behavioral modifications that drive enzootic rabies transmission in the context of host ecology. In addition, a large proportion of residues that may drive rabies virus adaptation to raccoons and arctic foxes are found within this neurotoxin-like region. Furthermore, a residue in the neurotoxin-like region of the mature rabies virus glycoprotein (residue 183) has been associated with pathogenicity, host switching, and disease ecology in previous studies. We propose the hypothesis that rabies virus transmission dynamics adapt to the ecology and physiology of novel hosts through the selection of glycoproteins that elicit specific degrees of behavioral modification and transmission probability.

Lyssavirus matrix protein stabilizes RGS4 to suppress ferroptosis via incomplete autophagy and iron metabolic reprogramming

Authors: Jian-Qing Zhao, Ping-Ping Chen, Mei-Xin Sun, Chuan Liang, Zhen F. Fu, Ling Zhao, Ming Zhou
Original abstract (English)
Rabies virus (RABV), the prototypical lyssavirus, suppresses neuronal ferroptosis through lipid droplet (LD) biogenesis, which is a critical immune evasion strategy we previously identified. However, the mechanistic link between LD formation and ferroptosis regulation during RABV infection remains poorly defined. Here, we report that RABV upregulate Regulator of G Protein Signaling 4 (RGS4), a ferroptosis-associated regulator, and this induction is strictly dependent on triglyceride synthesis and subsequent LD formation. Functionally, genetic or pharmacological inhibition of RGS4 elevates ferroptosis susceptibility, thereby restricting viral replication. Mechanistically, RGS4 depletion reduces HERC2, impairing the assembly of branched K48/K63 ubiquitin chains on NCOA4, which is essential for its proteasomal degradation, thereby enhancing NCOA4 stability. Stabilized NCOA4 promotes ferritinophagy, leading to degradation of ferritin heavy chain (FTH) and ultimately inducing ferroptosis. Critically, we identified that the lyssavirus matrix (M) protein directly counteracts this pathway: M protein inhibits RGS4 degradation via the Cys/N-degron system through incomplete autophagy, stabilizing RGS4 to maintain HERC2 expression, promote NCOA4 ubiquitination, and attenuate ferritinophagy. This mechanism reduces ferroptosis susceptibility and enables viral replication. Our findings unveil a novel lyssavirus-specific regulatory axis, RGS4-HERC2-NCOA4-FTH, that modulates ferroptosis, highlighting the critical role of ferroptosis in lyssavirus infection and pathogenesis.

In silico multi-epitope vaccine design against Irkut virus using immunoinformatic and reverse vaccinology

Authors: Sana Rehman Cheema, Maria Laeeq, Afrah Idrees, Muhammad Shahid
Original abstract (English)
Irkut virus (IRKV) is a member of genus Lyssavirus, which leads to fatal encephalitis in humans and is primarily found in bats and dogs that transmit the virus to humans via bites/saliva. Most cases have been reported in Russia and in Siberia and recently in China. Whole genome sequencing has shown a number of codon changes in antigenic sites common to the Rabies vaccine reducing cross-protection, which makes IRKV a serious public health threat because of its high fatality. In this study, a multi-epitope vaccine against the IRKV glycoprotein was designed via reverse vaccinology approach. Seven B-cell, seven MHC-I, and seven MHC-II epitopes were selected. These epitopes have predicted HLA binding affinity and are non-allergen, and 12 out of 21 epitopes were fully conserved across the three circulating IRKV strains, whereas remaining nine carried out one or two substitutions. The chosen epitopes covered 99.57% of the world population. The final construct which included an adjuvant (EAAAK), epitopes, and a 6xHis tag was predicted to be highly antigenic (VaxiJen score 0.8112) and non-allergenic (AllerTop), showing no sequence mimicry with human proteins by BLASTp screening. The physicochemical analysis suggested that the construct was basic, hydrophilic, and stable (aliphatic index 68.11, instability index 37.5, pI 10.43, GRAVY -0.323). The 3D structure built using SWISS-MODEL and Phyre2 and refined by GalaxyRefine depicts that 95.1% of residues lie in the favored regions of the Ramachandran plot. The structural superposition with the native viral glycoprotein showed that the native fold was well preserved (core RMSD ≈ 2.4 Å). The molecular docking with TLR4 (minimum energy -1086.3) and normal mode analysis further suggested the stability of the receptor binding and structural integrity. C-ImmSim immune simulation with two doses (prime-boost) showed an enhanced secondary immune response following boost dose, manifested by a higher level of IgM, IgG, CD4+ T-helper cells activation, and cytokine production. The immune activation profile predicted was generally similar to that for the full-length G protein, and the CD8+ T-cell response was similar between the two antigens in the one simulation run. These results indicate that the multi-epitope construct vaccine is structurally stable and immunologically promising and can be used as a vaccine candidate for further experimental evaluation against Irkut virus.

Original abstract (English)
Rabies is a fatal but preventable zoonosis that disproportionately affects vulnerable populations with limited access to healthcare. Mass vaccination of dogs and cats is the main control strategy, but evidence from Indigenous and remote settings is scarce. The aim of this study was to examine the operational, epidemiological, and sociocultural challenges associated with a mass rabies vaccination campaign implemented in response to a human rabies outbreak in Indigenous communities in Brazil. This qualitative descriptive study with an operational research component was conducted in Maxakali villages, Minas Gerais, after a rabies outbreak (April–May 2022). Data were collected through document analysis, field diaries, and direct observation, and analyzed using thematic content analysis guided by a One Health framework. Epidemiological indicators on vaccinated animals were also assessed. A total of 553 animals were vaccinated, including 427 dogs (77.2%) and 126 cats (22.8%), with heterogeneous distribution across villages. Although high operational coverage was achieved, the absence of reliable population denominators limited precise estimation of vaccination coverage. Major challenges included geographical barriers, difficulties in maintaining the cold chain, limited availability of trained personnel, and scarcity of animal restraint equipment. Sociocultural factors, such as language barriers and initial resistance to vaccination, influenced community adherence, which improved through the involvement of Indigenous leaders and health agents. Poor animal health conditions and free-roaming behavior further complicated the campaign. Mass rabies vaccination in indigenous territories is feasible but requires context-specific strategies addressing logistics, intercultural communication, and animal management. Strengthening integrated surveillance and community-engaged approaches is essential to improve rabies control.

Authors: Ravikiran Keshavamurthy, Cassandra Boutelle, Sarah Bonaparte, Crystal M. Gigante, Rene Edgar Condori, Rebecca Earnest, Jordona D. Kirby, Kathleen M. Nelson, Lillian A. Orciari, Yasmeen Ross
Original abstract (English)
Abstract Objective Describe the epidemiologic landscape of rabies and rabies testing in the US during 2024. Methods Animal rabies testing results from approximately 130 public health, agriculture, and academic laboratories are collected by the US National Rabies Surveillance System (NRSS) each month. Data reported in 2024 were analyzed to examine temporal and geographic trends in animal rabies cases by rabies virus variant (RVV). Results In 2024, 3,834 cases of animal rabies were reported to the NRSS (4.2% of 90,343 samples submitted), representing a 2.0% increase from 2023. Of positive samples, 331 (8.6%) were domestic animals, and 3,503 (91.4%) were wildlife. The most commonly found rabid domestic animal was cats (243 positive [1.3% positivity]), and the most commonly found wildlife were bats (1,376 positive [5.7% positivity]) followed by raccoons (1,080 [10.1% positivity]). Conclusions The expansion of the historical range of enzootic RVVs can lead to substantial public health and animal health burden. Domestically acquired human rabies cases in 2024 underscore the need for continual public education on the risks of rabies exposures to ensure timely and effective administration of postexposure prophylaxis. The internationally acquired human rabies case demonstrates that the US remains vulnerable to human and animal rabies importations. Clinical Relevance The NRSS plays a crucial role in protecting public and animal health by monitoring rabies trends in the US.

Emergent Entropic Forces Drive Inverse and Reentrant Phase Transitions in Condensates of Intrinsically Disordered Proteins

Authors: David A. Herrera-Martí, Marc Jamin
Original abstract (English)
We present a phenomenological, mean-field model for condensates of intrinsically disordered proteins that describes inverse phase transitions and qualitatively predicts reentrant phase behavior as a function of salt concentration. The model is based on a matrix-based formulation of the Flory-Huggins free energy, in which the enthalpic interaction parameter and the protein valence, defined here as the number of effective "sticky patches", are treated as free parameters. Its central insight is that integrating out dynamics of fast-moving molecular species generates an effective entropic force that increases the free-energy barrier to mixing, even within a mean-field description. We account for this by linking Gaussian integration of co-solutes to the Schur complement of the matrix-based Flory-Huggins free energy. This provides a general thermodynamic framework illustrating how enthalpic interactions that increase the number of available microstates necessarily induce effective entropic forces. We demonstrate the expressiveness and predictive power of the model by qualitatively reproducing recent experimental observations on the rabies virus phosphoprotein.

Authors: Jinda Khumkaew, Aree Butsorn, Putthikrai Pramuan
Original abstract (English)
Rabies remains a major public health challenge requiring coordinated multisectoral action and effective social mechanisms across the public health, veterinary, local government, and community sectors within the One Health framework. This study aimed to examine the systems and social mechanisms associated with rabies prevention within a One Health framework and to compare how these mechanisms operated across three community settings in Si Sa Ket Province, Thailand. A multiple-case qualitative design was employed. Study sites were purposively selected using epidemiological and rabies surveillance data from 2021 to 2025 and represented three community settings: rabies-free, rabies-controlled, and areas undergoing rabies control. Data were collected through in-depth interviews with 15 administrators and key actors involved in rabies prevention and control and through focus group discussions with 50 community stakeholders. Data were analyzed using inductive and deductive thematic approaches at both within-case and cross-case levels. The findings indicated that rabies-free communities were characterized by effective animal data systems, vaccine quality assurance and cold-chain management, systematic follow-up of unvaccinated animals, and community regulations that were implemented in practice. Rabies-controlled communities had operational systems and community networks but still experienced limitations in data continuity and the follow-up of certain animal populations. Communities undergoing rabies control continued to face constraints related to service accessibility, stray dog management, risk communication, and the management of environmental risk points. Cross-case analysis revealed that community-based rabies prevention was associated with the interaction among service systems, vaccine quality, information systems, animal management, environmental management, community regulations, and public trust in government services. The findings suggest that strengthening integrated information systems, vaccine quality assurance, comprehensive dog population management, and enforceable community regulations could support rabies prevention under a One Health framework and enhance the sustainability of community-based rabies prevention and control.

Question and background
The study aimed to explore the constraints to effective bite-case management and rabies post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) provision in Malawi. Rabies is a neglected, deadly, but preventable zoonotic disease that disproportionately affects rural and resource-limited populations in Africa and Asia.
Design and population
The study involved interviews and focus group discussions with human health care providers, animal health officers, and dog-bite victims to map the pathways, barriers, and bottlenecks that shape rabies-PEP access in Malawi. The study was conducted in Malawi and included participants from various sectors.
Core methods
Interviews and focus group discussions were conducted to explore perspectives on the PEP access pathway in Malawi. Participants were provided with written and oral information about the study and about confidentiality, including information about audio recording. Only those who consented to participate and signed a consent form were included.
Main results
The PEP access pathway in Malawi was found to be cumbersome, multi-step, and often lengthy, starting with a veterinary risk assessment and referral before PEP-treatment is provided. Training of service providers was poor, protocols were inconsistent, and One Health coordination between sectors was limited. Other barriers included the unavailability of vaccines, insufficient funding, shortages of assistant veterinary officers, long distances to health facilities, and a lack of community awareness.
Interpretation and novelty
The study highlights the need for simplifying the pathway to PEP for bite victims to reduce rabies deaths in Malawi. This would require clear service pathways and protocols, trained staff, and sufficient resources across settings and sectors. Structured two-way information sharing between the animal health and public health sectors, grounded in a One Health framework, could reduce delays, improve patient care and follow-up, and support vaccine forecasting and procurement.
Scientific and public-health significance
The findings can inform surveillance, diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of rabies in Malawi. Simplifying the PEP access pathway and improving intersectoral communication systems could lead to more equitable and timely access to rabies PEP, potentially reducing rabies deaths.
Limitations and evidence strength
The study design limitations include poor training of service providers, inconsistent protocols, and limited One Health coordination. The evidence strength is moderate due to the qualitative nature of the study and the potential for bias in participant selection.

Self-Amplifying RNA Vaccines After the First Approvals: Technology, Development Landscape, and Regulatory Science Considerations

Authors: Sunghyun Yi, Eunhye Seo, Kee‐Jong Hong, Pil‐Gu Park
Original abstract (English)
Self-amplifying RNA (saRNA) is a next-generation nucleic acid vaccine platform that enhances antigen expression through intracellular amplification of replicon RNA without producing infectious virions. saRNA constructs encode an alphavirus-derived replicase comprising nonstructural proteins nsP1–nsP4 and can elicit robust immune responses at low RNA doses. Replication intermediates may activate RIG-I, MDA5, and endosomal TLR3/7/8, contributing adjuvant-like activity. Kostaive (ARCT-154; zapomeran) became the first saRNA vaccine to receive full marketing approval in Japan in 2023, followed by authorization in the European Union in 2025 and the United Kingdom in 2026, demonstrating the platform’s clinical and regulatory feasibility. Development now extends beyond COVID-19 to seasonal and pandemic influenza, rabies, Crimean–Congo hemorrhagic fever, Nipah virus disease, and individualized cancer vaccines. However, the longer transcripts and additional replicase and subgenomic-promoter elements make in vitro transcription efficiency, full-length RNA content, double-stranded and truncated RNA impurities, lipid nanoparticle encapsulation, and potency testing particularly important. Regulatory assessment increasingly focuses on correct replicase and antigen expression, the magnitude and duration of self-amplification, biodistribution and persistence, and comparability. This review synthesizes the scientific basis, development landscape, manufacturing challenges, and regulatory science considerations of saRNA vaccines.

Rabies is a viral infection that affects the nervous system, including the brain. In Canada, rabies infections in humans are rare, but almost always fatal if it's not treated before symptoms develop. The virus infects the nerves around the site where it entered the body and then makes its way into the spinal cord and brain, ultimately leading to death. How is it transmitted? Rabies is transmitted to humans when they are bitten or scratched by an infected animal. Saliva is the most common source of transmission. In Canada, wild animals such as bats, skunks, raccoons and foxes are the main carriers of rabies, but they can transmit it to pets such as cats or dogs and farm animals. Bats are the primary carriers of rabies in Canada. Experts urge people to see a health-care provider if they've had suspected contact with any bat because their bites or scratches are tiny and might go unnoticed. There is no treatment for animals with rabies. The only way for the infection to be confirmed in animals is to examine the brain after death. Humans must receive treatment before symptoms appear, which usually happens about three to eight weeks after exposure, according to the Public Health Agency of Canada. People should thoroughly wash the area where they were bitten or scratched with soap and water and seek medical attention immediately. The treatment is a series of vaccines and an immunoglobulin injection. The first rabies vaccine shot is given immediately, followed by shots on days three, seven and 14. The immunoglobulin injection, a ready-made antibody that starts fighting the virus right away, is also administered because it takes time for the body to mount an immune response from the vaccinations. NSW Health has issued an urgent warning to residents across the Northern Rivers and coastal New South Wales following a significant increase in bat bites and scratches, raising concerns about potential exposure to the deadly Australian bat lyssavirus. Between September 28 and October 10, 43 people were assessed by NSW Health after being bitten or scratched by bats, compared with just 10 during the corresponding fortnight in 2025. The incidents occurred across several regions, including Northern NSW, the Mid North Coast, Hunter New England and the Central Coast. Health authorities are particularly concerned as warmer weather increases bat activity, while natural food shortages are bringing flying-foxes into closer contact with residential communities. Deadly Virus Demands Immediate Action NSW Health Director of Health Protection Keira Glasgow warned that people should assume any bat could carry Australian bat lyssavirus, a virus closely related to rabies. “We want people to assume that any bat could carry Australian bat lyssavirus, which is a close relative to the rabies virus and very dangerous to humans,” Ms Glasgow said. Although human infections are extremely rare, the consequences can be fatal once symptoms develop. “It is incredibly rare for Australian bat lyssavirus to transmit to humans, but once symptoms of lyssavirus start in people who are scratched or bitten by an infected bat, sadly there is no effective treatment,” she said. Health authorities emphasise that prompt medical assessment and appropriate post-exposure treatment can prevent infection. What To Do If Bitten Or Scratched Anyone bitten or scratched by a bat should take immediate precautions. Wash the affected area thoroughly with soap and running water for 15 minutes. Apply an antiviral antiseptic, such as Betadine, and allow it to dry. Seek urgent medical advice about post-exposure treatment. People should not delay seeking medical assistance, even when an injury appears minor. Only appropriately trained, vaccinated and protected wildlife handlers should attempt to rescue or handle bats. Food Shortages Bringing Flying-Foxes Into Backyards NSW Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water Executive Director Conservation Programs Ingrid Emery said food shortages were contributing to unusual flying-fox behaviour. “During food shortage events, flying-foxes typically stay in feed trees during the day instead of returning to their larger colonies,” Ms Emery said. Consequently, residents may notice flying-foxes resting in backyard trees during daylight hours. Government reports indicate the shortage extends along the eastern coastline, from the Clarence Valley to the South Coast. Normal behaviour is expected to resume as natural food supplies recover, although the timing will depend on seasonal conditions. Leave Bat Rescues To Professionals Residents who discover injured, distressed or trapped bats should never attempt to handle or rescue them. Instead, contact WIRES on 1300 094 737 or a licensed local wildlife rescue organisation. NSW Health stresses that avoiding direct contact is the most effective way to prevent exposure. With flying-foxes increasingly visible throughout coastal communities, authorities are encouraging residents to remain cautious, protect children and pets from contact, and leave all bat handling to trained professionals.
