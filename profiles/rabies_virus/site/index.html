Molecular Genetic Analysis of the Rabies Virus in Wild and Domestic Animals of Kazakhstan and the Development of a Diagnostic System

Authors: Kulyaisan T. Sultankulova, Aigerim K. Abubakirova, Gaukhar O. Shynybekova, Meirim D. Almezhanova, Nurlan S. Kozhabergenov, Zhumagali K. Koshemetov, Kamshat A. Shorayeva, Kuandyk D. Zhugunissov, Mukhit B. Orynbayev, Г.О. Шыныбекова
Original abstract (English)
Rabies remains one of the most significant zoonotic infections, characterized by high mortality. In Kazakhstan, the virus circulates among both domestic and wild animals, necessitating ongoing molecular genetic monitoring and improved laboratory diagnostic methods. The aim of this study was to investigate the genetic characteristics of the rabies virus circulating among animals in Kazakhstan using nucleoprotein (N) gene analysis and to develop a regionally adapted real-time RT-PCR method for its detection. Kazakhstani rabies virus isolates deposited in GenBank revealed that all belong to a cosmopolitan lineage and form a single phylogenetic cluster, regardless of host species. Isolates from neighboring countries, including China and Russia, were also included in this cluster, demonstrating the regional relationship of circulating virus variants. The obtained data were used to develop a real-time PCR method based on the genetic characteristics of the rabies virus circulating in Kazakhstan. The diagnostic performance of the method was evaluated using 144 samples of various origins obtained from domestic and wild animals, including bats. The developed assay demonstrated a sensitivity of 86.4%, a specificity of 98.4%, and a diagnostic efficiency of 96.5%. The proposed method ensures reliable detection of rabies virus variants circulating in Kazakhstan and can be used for laboratory diagnostics and epidemiological monitoring.

Atypical neuroimaging in rabies encephalitis: A case report of concurrent cerebral venous sinus thrombosis in a nine-year-old patient

Authors: Djoudline Doughmi, Amal Akammar, Imad Daoudi, Said Benlamkaddem, Adnane Berdai, Meryem Boubbou, Mustapha Harandou
Original abstract (English)
Human rabies is a fatal viral encephalitis that presents a profound diagnostic challenge in the absence of a known history of animal exposure. We report the case of a 9-year-old boy from Morocco who presented with fever, seizures, psychomotor agitation, hypersalivation and hydrophobia, despite no reported history of an animal bite or scratch. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) served as a critical diagnostic tool, strongly suggesting rabies encephalitis by revealing classic bilateral symmetric grey matter hyperintensities in the thalami and temporal lobes. Concurrently, MRI revealed an atypical and rare macrovascular finding: extensive cerebral venous sinus thrombosis of the right transverse and sigmoid sinuses, which likely accounted for additional asymmetric white matter changes. A nuchal skin biopsy analyzed via reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction confirmed the presence of rabies virus RNA. Despite aggressive neurocritical care and therapeutic anticoagulation, the patient's condition deteriorated, and he passed away on day 9 of admission. This case underscores the dual role of MRI in acute encephalitis: guiding early etiological diagnosis of rabies and identifying concurrent, clinically significant macrovascular complications like cerebral venous sinus thrombosis.

Psychological effects of rabies on the relatives of people who died from human rabies in Côte d'Ivoire between 2023 and 2025: Qualitative Observational Study

Authors: Christiane Antoinette Djoman
Original abstract (English)
Abstract Background : the psychological impact of rabies on the relatives of people who died from the disease remains largely unexplored. The aim of this study was to analyse the psychological effects of rabies on the relatives of people who died from human rabies in Côte d’Ivoire. Original abstract (English)
Probe-based genomics technologies are extending molecular analysis into intact tissues and fixed cells, yet strategies to decode complex experimental conditions encoded in cellular RNA remain limited. Here we present a modular framework that integrates custom software tools with purpose-built cloning reagents to design, assemble, validate, and deploy combinatorial DNA barcodes. Combinatorial barcodes comprise spatially adjacent collections of known sequences, enabling millions of unique molecules to be efficiently distinguished using a limited set of probes. Our software tools integrate with optimized assembly plasmids and whole plasmid long-read sequencing for high-fidelity construction and structural validation of diverse combinatorial barcode architectures. Assembled barcode libraries are flexibly transferred into user-modified expression vectors to support diverse downstream experimental applications. Following rabies virus-based delivery to the mouse brain, we validate in vivo decoding of a combinatorial barcode architecture capable of distinguishing ~16.3 million expressed RNAs through probe-based in situ sequencing. Our framework for flexible and accurate combinatorial barcode construction fills a technically demanding niche delivering cost-effective molecular reagents for multiplexed experimentation on current and evolving probe-based genomics platforms.

Clinical Rabies: Public health impacts, vaccination, and exposure Rabies cases in companion animals and human exposures continue to make news in the United States. While veterinary professionals are well aware of the risks posed by this deadly virus, challenges such as vaccine hesitancy, variations in laws by municipality, and a lack of awareness of the seriousness of this disease contribute to the continued spread of the disease both in the U.S. and around the world. Veterinary professionals can hone their expertise and learn how to collaborate with human healthcare professionals and other public health officials to reduce the risk in their communities. Advertisement A recent news story out of Florida described a feral cat, who was later determined to be rabid, who bit at least five people and a puppy before being found dead underneath a home. After an investigation was conducted, one of the local residents who routinely fed this cat and other feral cats was cited for not having vaccinated the cats. This series of events reignited debates over responsibility to vaccinate, rabies surveillance, and other public health initiatives to protect humans and animals from the threat of rabies. Stories like these also remind veterinary teams of the lack of understanding among the general public of the risks posed by rabies to both human and animal health. Advertisement In an AAHA webinar entitled Rabies Compliance Made Clear: Protecting Pets, Clients, and Clinics, Richard Ford, DVM, MS, Dip. ACVIM and (Hon) ACVPM, discussed the state of rabies in the U.S. and around the world, important facts about vaccination, and what veterinary teams need to know about immunity and exposure. He said that when it comes to rabies, all veterinary professionals need to be experts. We caught up with him after his webinar to find out what he thinks veterinary professionals need to know the most about rabies. Human rabies impacts in the U.S. and worldwide According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), at least 70,000 people die worldwide from rabies. Forty percent of human rabies cases are in children under the age of 15, and 99% of human rabies deaths are the result of bites from rabid dogs. Ford explained that these numbers have increased since the COVID-19 pandemic for a variety of reasons, including increased vaccine hesitancy that resulted in fewer pets getting vaccinated, the breakdown of some rabies control programs that provided surveillance, and an increase in the number of stray and abandoned dogs in areas around the world. Additionally, access to healthcare for individuals bitten by a potentially rabid animal was more limited during the pandemic. In the U.S., human rabies cases are much lower than in other countries, with a total of 17 cases documented by the CDC from 2015 to 2024. Also, unlike other parts of the world, the most common rabies vectors are not dogs. Ford said that wildlife such as bats, racoons, and foxes are 10 times more likely to be infected with rabies than domestic species. And even among domestic species, cats are four times more likely to be infected with rabies than dogs are. For this reason, the top reason for rabies post-exposure prophylaxis in the U.S. is exposure to a potentially rabid cat. Even with the much lower numbers of human cases in the U.S., each case of this uniformly fatal and preventable disease is taken very seriously. 2025 AAHA One Health guidelines: Navigating Cross-Disciplinary Partnerships You know all about preventing and treating zoonotic disease, but how comfortable are you when it comes to collaborating with your colleagues in human medicine on cases that require (or simply benefit from) shared reporting? AAHA’s 2025 One Health Guidelines: Navigating Cross-Disciplinary Partnerships provides key insights and expert recommendations for what to report when—and to whom—in order to make sure we’re all working toward keeping people, pets, and the planet healthy. The guidelines also provide expert insights and detailed roadmaps to help veterinary professionals work seamlessly with other healthcare professionals within a One Health System. Read up on common scenarios, case studies, and more. Vaccination laws by jurisdiction While higher vaccination rates are undoubtedly a large part of the reason the U.S. has fewer human rabies cases than other parts of the world, laws regarding rabies vaccination of pets vary significantly from one state and municipality to the next. Ford explained that only 39 states require rabies vaccination for dogs, for example, and fewer require it for cats and ferrets. Even within states, some smaller municipalities have their own individual rules, so it can get confusing for pet owners. States also differ in which vaccines they allow companies to sell (the one-year or the three-year vaccine, for example) and who is allowed to administer the vaccine to pets. To complicate matters more, states also have varying definitions of what constitutes a “currently vaccinated” pet. Ford said most states define it as 28 days after initial vaccination and immediately after a booster, but not all states agree. Ford stressed the importance of knowing the local laws regarding rabies vaccination and following labeled indications. “It must be administered by an authorized person at the appropriate age, dose, and interval,” he said. Not doing so can lead to loss or restriction of a veterinarian’s license. Ford said that the most common rabies vaccine-related reason for action against a veterinarian’s license is giving half doses of the rabies vaccine. “Give the entire dose,” he said. Are you Rabies Aware? Wondering what your state’s laws are on rabies vaccination, quarantine after exposure, and even whether you can legally exempt a pet from vaccination requirements for medical reasons? The Rabies Aware website was created to answer all these questions and more. For U.S. state, the answers to 30 different questions about which species require vaccination, how the state defines “currently vaccinated”, and who in the state has the authority to determine exposure, among other important topics. Vaccines, immunity, and titers Ford explained why pets only need one rabies vaccine during their initial vaccination period. Other vaccines like the canine distemper vaccine or the feline leukemia vaccine require at least two doses because pets need an initial priming dose followed by at least one immunizing dose within six weeks of the first vaccine. The primary rabies vaccine given to pet still acts as the priming dose, but exposure to a rabid animal is considered to be the immunizing dose. Ford said this works because the priming dose is sustained and because the pet is considered to be immunized two weeks after exposure to a rabid animal, which is less time than the months typically needed for the virus to travel from a bite wound to the central nervous system where it causes clinical disease. In cases where there is no rabies exposure, Ford said the priming effect from the first vaccine is sustained. When the first booster is given a year later, he said antibody levels go “sky high.” But, Ford said, the presence of antibodies must be interpreted carefully. Rabies titers are currently only used for export to rabies-free areas of the world. Legally, he explained, a positive titer indicates that a pet has been vaccinated. However, a positive titer cannot be used as an indicator of immunity or protection under U.S. law, Ford said, and it does not obviate the need for a booster vaccine. Additionally, Ford said the timing of titers in relation to vaccination is very important. “If you are doing an antibody test for travel, the young cat or dog with one rabies vaccine will likely fail the test,” he said, because they have not yet had their immunizing dose of the vaccine. This is why it’s so important to draw blood for a rabies titer after a patient has had their second rabies vaccine. Ford recommends drawing the sample 3–4 weeks after the second vaccine to allow for antibodies to form prior to testing. While the guidelines are designed primarily for veterinary professionals, they also include information on client education. And the entire veterinary team can learn more about the vaccine guidelines (as well as how to have productive client conversations) by taking the AAHA Canine Vaccination Guidelines Certificate Course. To make sure you’ve got all the information you need to discuss core (and non-core) vaccines with your clients, be sure to bookmark the 2022 AAHA Canine (updated in 2024) and 2020 AAHA/AAFP Feline Vaccination Guidelines. Human and animal rabies exposure Just as with vaccination requirements, definitions of rabies exposure differ by municipality and circumstance. What constitutes an exposure, for example? Is it a bite from an animal known to be rabid? What about a scratch? What if we aren’t sure if the animal has rabies? Could just being in close proximity to certain animals be considered exposure? Ford said the answers to these questions can vary based on the types of animals involved, their vaccination and exposure history, and the types of contact or possible contact. He made one point clear, however: because of the public health risk, public health officials are the ones who decide what constitutes an exposure in any given case. When the possible exposure is between two animals, that public health official may be the state public health veterinarian, often in coordination with the treating veterinarian. In cases of possible human exposure, this will likely involve physicians or other human public health officials. Veterinary teams serve an important role in taking detailed histories and carefully documenting any known vaccinations for animals involved in possible rabies exposures. They can then use this information in their consultations with public health veterinarians to determine if an exposure is likely to have occurred and take appropriate next steps. Human healthcare professionals serve the same role for potential human exposures; however, an article in the American Journal of Infection Control identified challenges in risk determination among human healthcare professionals in the U.S. that can sometimes delay diagnosis and treatment. These challenges include the rarity of human rabies cases in the U.S., making it less of top of mind as a differential diagnosis, and the relatively long incubation period in between a possible exposure and the appearance of any symptoms of disease, which can make it less likely that a potential exposure would be mentioned or considered as part of a patient’s medical history. A separate study in the Archives of Academic Emergency Medicine conducted in Iran documented “significant shortcomings among healthcare professionals” in knowledge of potential rabies vector species, routes of transmission, and post-exposure management of patients. Although veterinary and human healthcare professionals typically have a high level of understanding of rabies pathophysiology, prevention, and post-exposure management, there is great value in these two groups working together design preventive strategies, identify cases where intervention is needed, and educate the general public as to the risks of this deadly disease. The Livingston County Health Department says a bat can expose a person to rabies even without a visible bite or scratch. Contact with a bat’s saliva or brain tissue, or even finding a bat in a room upon waking up, could be enough to warrant getting tested. What to do if bitten or scratched Health officials are reminding residents to take precautions if they encounter a bat. Anyone who is bitten or scratched should immediately wash the affected area with soap and water, seek medical attention and report the incident to local health officials. Bat removal calls average 10 to 20 a day Ash Bigger, owner of Bat and Wildlife Solutions, helps dozens of families remove bats from their homes every day. “We usually average about anywhere from 10 to 20 a day with bats in the home, hearing scratching, things like that,” said Ash Bigger, owner-operator, Bat and Wildlife Solutions. Bigger says if a bat is found in a home, plan the next move carefully. “You want to try to keep an eye on the bat or corral it into a bedroom, bathroom to confine it into a certain space. If it’s loose in the house, these things hide so well, it’s virtually impossible to find them if you lose sight of them,” Bigger said. “You definitely want to protect yourself if you were to try to capture the bat and get it tested. You want to wear thick leather gloves, try to wrap it up in a towel. You definitely don’t want to try to grab it barehanded,” Bigger said. Sealing gaps can keep bats out Bigger says bats are opportunists. To keep them out of a home, he recommends sealing up any gaps around the roof line, since bats can fit through holes as small as a dime. The health department also recommends taking steps to prevent bats from entering homes, including keeping doors and windows closed and locked and sealing small openings where bats could get inside. Since May 2024, rabies cases have been confirmed in Cape fur seals along the Western Cape and Northern Cape coastlines of South Africa — the first time the virus has established itself in a marine mammal population anywhere in the world. Beyond attacks on humans, researchers worry whether the disease will spread to other marine mammals, as seals interact with different species. CAPE TOWN, South Africa — Debbie Hendrikz was walking along her usual route on the shoreline when she crouched to photograph a seal. She is familiar with seals and has captured similar images along the beach. But this was different. “It just went for me,” the 49-year-old said. “I knew instinctively by the sound it made that I was its prey. It grabbed my arm and bit hard into a metal bracelet I was wearing, which saved me. I knew I had to get away and not let it get my feet in the soft sand. I managed to shake it off my arm and just got away.” “I never imagined anything like this could happen. It took me a long while to get over it.” Seals do not normally see humans as prey, but Hendrikz’s encounter is one of a growing list of recent attacks by the marine mammals in Cape Town. According to Gregg Oelofse, the City of Cape Town’s coastal manager, the city has recorded 135 incidents of aggressive seal behavior toward humans since September 2021. More than half, 73 incidents, resulted in bite injuries ranging from broken skin to those requiring hospitalization in an intensive care unit. Researchers in South Africa suspect the incidents stem from a disease normally associated with land animals that appears to be leaping to Cape fur seals (Arctocephalus pusillus) — rabies. According to South Africa’s National Institute for Communicable Diseases (NICD), reports of rabies in seals around the world prior to 2024 were exceedingly rare. But since May 2024, multiple cases have been confirmed in Cape fur seals along the Western Cape and Northern Cape coastlines of South Africa. The government agency said it believes it is the first time rabies has established and maintained itself in a marine mammal population anywhere in the world. Now South African government officials and researchers are examining how seals contracted the disease in the first place. They are taking steps to stop the spread of rabies, but say they are also worried that, as seals travel great distances and interact with other species, the virus could jump to other marine mammals and perhaps even bring rabies to Antarctica, where it has never been. ‘No longer eradicable’ Experts at a July 2025 scientific workshop in Cape Town attended by Mongabay concluded that the disease is now likely endemic in Cape fur seals and is no longer eradicable. They categorized it as a persistent threat requiring long-term management. Scientists are now asking deeper questions about whether this outbreak is slowly burning itself out or if the cases are a prelude to something far larger, with rabies spreading to other sea mammals. To date, the NICD says rabies has not been found in any other marine mammal species along South Africa’s coast. South African scientists say they believe the rabies in seals started on land. According to a July 2024 outbreak update report by South Africa’s Department of Agriculture, Land Reform and Rural Development (DALRRD), genetic sequencing of the virus found in infected seals points to a canid origin, most closely linked to a rabies strain from black-backed jackals (Lupulella mesomelas) from Namibia. The DALRRD report came the same month as a Cape Town workshop with a wide range of participants, including leading scientists from different countries. DALRRD’s working hypothesis, developed further in an updated report published in October 2024, is that a rabid jackal bit a seal somewhere on the Namibian coast — a plausible scenario given that jackals are known to prey on seal pups there, biting seals and getting into skirmishes with adults protecting their young. DALRRD also reported there may be more than one event that introduced rabies into the seal population, including a secondary link to a strain of rabies from a bat-eared fox (Otocyon megalotis) from South Africa’s Western Cape. What happened next was then perhaps inevitable. Cape fur seals are social animals living in enormous, densely packed colonies. Physical contact, including biting, is constant. In the terminal stages of rabies infection, the virus drives animals to bite. In a colony of thousands of seals in close confines on an island or outcrop these factors would create a near perfect transmission environment. According to the DALRRD October 2024 report, genetic sequencing of multiple samples showed sequences clustering together, indicating the virus was already circulating within the seal population and representing what scientists now describe as a canid rabies Cape fur seal cycle. The NICD’s August 2025 briefing document records that between May 2024 and January 2025, a total of 39 cases were confirmed from the Western Cape and a further 16 from the Northern Cape province. In June 2025, the first case was confirmed in the Eastern Cape. Critically, DALRRD’s October 2024 report also documents that the outbreak is not new: Retrospective testing of stored samples from Cape fur seal deaths dating back to 2021 returned some positive results, indicating the virus has been present longer. Coastal manager Gregg Oelofse’s statistics from the field suggest a more specific timeline. The city first recorded a marked change in seal aggression in September 2021, beginning with an incident at Miller’s Point in the Western Cape. In the previous two decades, his team logged only two comparable cases. Within months of September 2021, however, reports of aggressive seals were coming in from across the peninsula, Oelofse said. Surfers showed photographs of bitten surfboards, swimmers recalled being chased from the water. At the time, rabies was not considered, since the disease had never before been recorded in a marine mammal. The working theory that first emerged was domoic acid poisoning from harmful algal blooms. That theory did not hold up. The earliest confirmed positive case of rabies came from retrospective testing of a brain sample taken from a seal removed from Kommetjie, in the Western Cape, after it had bitten an elderly woman walking on the beach in early July 2022. The seal died en route to the Cape of Good Hope’s Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals. At the time, no one suspected rabies, Oelofse said. It was only in April 2024, when four seals were tested and three came back positive, that the city and its scientific partners recognized rabies was established in the population. Retrospective testing of stored samples, including the 2022 Kommetjie case, confirming the earlier presence of the disease and its link with aggressive attacks. Will rabies spread further in seals? Now researchers are trying to better understand how the disease in Cape fur seals will spread. To understand where this is going, science needs a number. Specifically, it needs what epidemiologists call the basic reproductive number, R, the average number of new infections generated by a single infected individual in a susceptible population. The logic is if the R number falls below one, then each infected animal generates less than one new case on average. The chain of transmission then weakens, and the disease eventually burns out. But if the number is above one, each infection spawns more than one new case and the epidemic grows. Johann Kotze, a freelance veterinarian and epidemiologist who presented his modelling findings at the July 2025 Cape Town workshop, has been constructing an epidemiological model specifically for this outbreak. His preliminary results offer the first quantitative foothold on the question. The spread observed so far is slow, he said, rather than an explosively growing outbreak. This is consistent with a very low R number, Kotze said: “Only a very low R number would produce what we are seeing. Higher values would have grown much quicker, much faster.” His current estimate puts the R number below 1.1 and possibly below 1.05. For further context, an R number of 1.05 means that on average, every 100 infected seals would generate 105 new cases. The current estimate is above one, which means the virus is sustaining itself and slowly expanding. Experts at the July 2025 workshop put it plainly: Unlike the rapid, large-scale die-offs seen with other diseases, rabies may burn slowly through populations, flaring unpredictably. It is a slow burn, not an explosion. The question for scientists, then, is whether the pattern remains this way. On the ground in Cape Town, according to Oelofse, the pattern appears to be holding. “It’s kind of like it goes dead quiet for a while and then suddenly we’ll have three rabid seals in Kommetjie that came from Duiker Island,” he said. “And then it’ll go quiet for a while and then we’ll suddenly have one or two in Simon’s Town that came from Seal Island.” On another occasion, his colleague was called out to Big Bay twice in a single day for two separate rabid seals, he added. The pattern matches what scientists predicted at the outset: Small flare-ups, clusters of disease positive animals in one location, then nothing; and then a flare-up elsewhere. Researchers still have a lot more to learn about the disease. To refine estimates further, scientific models need answers to difficult questions: How long does an infected seal remain contagious? What is the incubation period? What is the natural turnover rate of the seal population, which incorporates not only their lifespan but also how often new pups are born? How do these factors interact with the virus’s transmission rates? With marine mammals that live across hundreds of kilometer of remote coastline, gathering that data is difficult. The DALRRD October 2024 report recognizes this challenge. Vast stretches of the Northern Cape coastline are sparsely populated and some areas are restricted due to mining activities, so collecting data in those areas is constrained, it said. Oelofse said Cape Town itself has no reliable measure of how many seals are infected at any given time across remote stretches of coastline such as Elands Bay, where seals are plentiful but monitoring is minimal. “We don’t really have a good idea of how present rabies is in the seal population,” he said. “It doesn’t appear to be rampant, but we don’t really know.” Kotze’s modelling so far points to two divergent future paths — rabies fades, or it increases. On the first path, the outbreak may dissipate because the virus encounters barriers like geographical gaps between colonies that limit its spread. The outbreak plateaus and the disease does not become entrenched permanently and gradually fades. This would mirror what happens with other viruses that enter a new host species but ultimately fail to sustain themselves. But the fear is that the current situation will follow the second scenario and that this is the cusp of something larger. On this second path, the disease has a slow beginning. The current case counts could suggest not a plateau but a prologue. Given enough time and enough seals across colonies stretching from the Western Cape to Angola, the virus will spread. Even worse in this scenario, seals then infect other marine mammals and the disease spreads to other species. The difference between these two futures turns largely on how Cape fur seals reproduce. Seals reproduce slowly. This may ultimately limit the virus’s ability to sustain itself; or it means the seal population cannot recover quickly if rabies infections do expand significantly. “The only thing that stops an epidemic of this kind is when you reach a point where the number of new infections equals the number of new births in the population, what we call an endemic state,” Kotze said. The past and the future for rabies and South Africa’s fur seals Cape fur seals have likely become more vulnerable to rabies and other diseases because of ecological pressures. Mass seal deaths in 2021 and 2022, in which thousands of seals died along South Africa’s West Coast, were investigated and attributed primarily to malnutrition. Scientists have pointed to chronically stressed animals living in warming oceans. Available prey has become less reliable. Coastal real estate development has added stress, limiting some of the territory they prefer for their colonies. Harmful algal blooms or red tides happen more often and are larger, affecting seal behavior and cognition. The aggressive encounters that alarmed beachgoers from 2021 onwards were initially attributed to these blooms. Researchers also point to another detail that may point to where the disease is heading: When the seal attacked Debbie Hendrikz on Fish Hoek beach, she did not contract rabies, unlike when a diseased dog bites a person. The NICD notes that to date, there are no known cases where seals transmitted the disease to people. Oelofse, who has tracked the toll of seal attacks on humans closely, called this the most interesting open question in the entire outbreak: Why has rabies not spread from infected seals to the humans they have bitten? His team has gone back and retrospectively contacted people known to have been bitten by confirmed rabid seals — none have developed rabies. Tour guides operating in the area around Duiker Island reported being bitten repeatedly by seals behaving strangely and were not treated for rabies. None became infected. “I think it might be related to the high salt content in the seal’s mouth,” Oelofse said. “It might be diluting the virus. We know salt water has a bit of an antiseptic effect. I don’t know.” Kotze cautioned against speculating on this question, “There is no proof as to why those bites would not have infected the human.” The leading theories involve reduced viral load in the saliva of marine-exposed animals, salt water degrading the virus, and the immediate wound-washing that ocean immersion provides. These theories remain unproven, he said. For now, government officials are cautious. The NICD strongly advises anyone bitten, scratched or licked by a seal suspected of having rabies to wash the wound thoroughly with soap and water and seek immediate treatment at the nearest clinic or hospital. Rabies post-exposure prophylaxis is effective in preventing infection if applied urgently and appropriately. The NICD further advises that those at frequent or continual risk of exposure, such as swimmers, divers, surfers or people working with seals, should discuss pre-exposure prophylaxis with their healthcare provider. The government has considered other efforts to limit the spread of the disease in seals. Mass vaccination of the entire seal population — an approach that has helped control rabies in European foxes — was assessed and declared not feasible by the National Department of Agriculture in its October 2024 report. A key factor cited for the decision is that seal colonies are often inaccessible. But the July 2025 workshop produced a more targeted recommendation: Vaccination trials should begin for seals that frequently interact with humans. The effectiveness of rabies vaccines in marine mammals remains unproven, but experts concluded that the trials pose minimal risk and may prove to provide protection. In August 2024, the world’s first ever rabies vaccination trial in marine mammals took place at Kalk Bay harbor in Cape Town, conducted by the Two Oceans Aquarium Foundation in collaboration with scientific and government partners. The city has since stepped back from continuing to vaccinate the Kalk Bay harbor seal colony. The high turnover of individual seals moving between that colony and Seal Island, approximately 12 km (7.6 mi) east, makes sustained coverage difficult to achieve. A renewed, smaller effort at Gordon’s Bay harbor is being considered, Oelofse said. Authorities have started and continue to vaccinate Southern elephant seals (Mirounga leonina) on the Cape coast. Even though they are a different species and the virus has not been identified in them, officials fear they may contract the disease from fights with Cape fur seals. The concern driving this effort is that elephant seals can travel far: One tagged elephant seal vaccinated in Cape Town later turned up on Marion Island, deep in the Southern Ocean, demonstrating how far and fast the disease could travel if it took hold in a more mobile, far-ranging species. Between 15 and 20 elephant seals have been tagged and vaccinated to date, with booster shots given when possible.
