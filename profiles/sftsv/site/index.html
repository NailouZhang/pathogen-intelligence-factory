Persistent viral carriage of severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus in canine: A potential canine-to-human transmission event in a family cluster

Authors: Jiang-Ping Ren, Shiping Gu, Ying Liu, Yu-Long Yang, Xu-Guang Shi, Xiao-Fu Luo, Jun-Wei Li, Lei Zhu, Sen Li, Hui-Min Yao
Original abstract (English)
Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is a tick-borne viral disease with a substantial case-fatality rate. Here, we reported a family cluster of SFTS cases in Southeast China. The index case was a 57-year-old man, whose son and wife subsequently developed SFTS-compatible symptoms at two and three days after his onset, respectively. All three cases were laboratory-confirmed for SFTS, and retrospectively reported close care to a sick golden retriever prior to symptom onset. In contrast, the other two family members without such canine exposure remained serologically and clinically uninfected. All three cases denied a tick-biting history before illness onset. Subsequent testing confirmed SFTS virus (SFTSV) infection in the golden retriever, and SFTSV was also detected in a tick collected from the dog. Longitudinal viral screening of serial canine samples revealed persistent SFTSV shedding: viral positivity was sustained for 23 days in nasal swabs and urethral swabs, 27 days in serum samples, and at least 30 days in rectal swabs following its symptom onset. Genomic analysis showed near-identical SFTSV sequences from the index case and the infected dog, with identities of 99.94% for the small segment, 100% for the medium segment, and 99.98% for the large segment. This study reveals prolonged SFTSV shedding in infected canine, suggests a plausible risk of canine-to-human SFTSV transmission, and highlights the potential role of dogs in the spread and evolution of SFTSV.

LPC(16:0) and prothrombin time as mortality predictors in severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome

Authors: Qiao-Yue Wei, Fa-Xue Zhang, Ruo-Bing Liu, Wei Liu, Hao Li
Original abstract (English)
ABSTRACT Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is an emerging tick-borne viral disease with a high fatality rate. However, the impact of the SFTS virus (SFTSV) on host lipid metabolism and its relationship with the prognosis of patients remain unclear. To determine the lipid metabolism profile of SFTSV-infected and identify prognostic biomarkers, targeted lipidomics analysis was conducted on SFTSV-infected Huh7 cells and serum samples from 71 subjects (including 15 healthy controls, 35 SFTS survivors, and 21 SFTS deceased patients) in this study. Our findings revealed that glycerophospholipid metabolism was the most significantly disrupted pathway in both SFTSV-infected Huh7 cells and SFTS patients, with lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) species, including LPC(16:1), LPC(18:1), LPC(16:0), and LPC(18:0), showing decreased levels. Together with clinical parameters, prothrombin time (PT) and LPC(16:0) were further identified as prognostic factors, and the combined model incorporating these two markers predicted mortality with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.937. These findings provide a foundation for mortality prediction and mechanistic understanding of SFTS. IMPORTANCE Effective prognostic tools and targeted therapies for SFTS remain urgently needed, as current clinical management relies largely on supportive care and lacks reliable indicators for early risk stratification. This study enhances our understanding of lipid alterations following SFTSV infection, opens potential avenues for metabolism-targeted intervention, and may aid in assessing disease progression and guiding appropriate clinical interventions.

Original abstract (English)

Renal dysfunction and coagulation abnormalities are closely related to disease severity. Original abstract (English)

These findings suggest that effective prevention should consider human activity patterns and the role of small mammals in sustaining pathogen circulation, while a One Health approach integrating humans, animals, and vectors can guide the development of more targeted control strategies.

Original abstract (English)
Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is an emerging tick‐borne zoonosis in East Asia, yet the contribution of animals to virus maintenance and spillover remains incompletely characterized. This review synthesizes current evidence of SFTS virus (SFTSV) infection across domestic livestock, companion animals, wildlife, and captive species, focusing on host range, geographic distribution, diagnostics, and reports of viral RNA and virus isolation. Available data indicate that SFTSV exposure and infection occur across a broad range of animal hosts, supporting a multi-host ecological framework rather than a simple single-reservoir interpretation. Domestic ruminants frequently exhibit high seroprevalence and may function as sentinels of enzootic activity, while occasional virus isolation from cattle demonstrates that viable virus can be recovered from naturally infected livestock, although this does not establish reservoir or amplification competence. Companion animals are considered a potential interface for interspecies transmission, as some studies have documented severe clinical disease, high viral loads, and cat-associated human transmission events supported by varying levels of epidemiological and genomic evidence. Studies in wildlife further document exposure and natural infection across diverse taxa, although their specific roles in viral maintenance remain unresolved. Interpretation of these findings depends heavily on the diagnostic modality: serology primarily indicates prior exposure, RNA detection supports current or recent infection, and virus isolation demonstrates that viable virus was present in the sampled material. Integrating animal infection data within an ecological framework reveals major knowledge gaps related to reservoir competence, infectious shedding, phylogenetic connections among animal, tick, and human cases, and implications for prevention. The review underscores the need for One Health surveillance that incorporates clinical data from companion animals and applies evidence‐based interpretation to improve recognition of animal–human interfaces associated with potential spillover risk.
