Intestinal infection and fecal shedding of infectious SFTS virus reveal a potential fecal–oral transmission route

Authors: Gang Xu, Xiaohan Liu, Xiaokun Li, Jie Xu, Keyu Wang, Xiaofei Song, Wannian Wei, Hailin Tang, Zhihao Xu, Jianhui Zhou
Original abstract (English)
Abstract Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is an emerging tick‐borne viral disease with a case fatality rate of 7.8% and no approved therapy. Gastrointestinal symptoms are frequently reported in patients, yet whether the intestinal tract serves as a site of viral infection or contributes to transmission has remained unclear. To address this, we combined clinical cohort analysis with mouse models and human intestinal organoids. In a multicenter cohort of 752 patients, 62.9% presented with gastrointestinal symptoms that correlated with serum viral load. Fecal calprotectin was elevated in the majority of tested samples, pointing to frequent intestinal inflammation during SFTS. In mice, the virus infected enterocytes and intestinal stem cells, causing epithelial injury, barrier disruption and diarrhea. These cellular and histopathological findings were recapitulated in human intestinal organoids, which also showed inflammatory reprogramming of the epithelium. Notably, replication‐competent virus could be isolated from the feces of infected mice and from patients with acute SFTS. Together, these observations indicate that SFTSV targets the intestinal epithelium and that infectious virus shed in feces may enable a fecal–oral transmission route. This adds to the growing evidence for non‐vector‐borne transmission and carries implications for infection control and public health surveillance.

The study is limited by its reliance on observational data and animal models, but the evidence is strong due to the replication of findings across multiple models and the correlation with clinical data.

A review of ticks and tick-borne diseases in Southeast Asia: identifying priorities, hurdles, and opportunities for health and knowledge gain.

Authors: Ernest Jm Teo, Mackenzie L Kwak, Nora G Cleary, Van Lun Low, Babi Kyi Soe, Michael E von Fricken, Andrew G Letizia, Keita Matsuno, Kimberly M Fornace, Benoit Malleret
Original abstract (English)
Ticks and tick-borne diseases (TBDs) in Southeast Asia are poorly understood, confounding surveillance, and mitigation opportunities. Herein, we conducted a survey of experts (n = 25) to identify tick species and TBDs of greatest concern to Southeast Asia, 'drivers' which may alter their impacts, and research hurdles. These experts identified Dermacentor auratus, Haemaphysalis longicornis, Rhipicephalus linnaei, Amblyomma testudinarium sensu lato, Ixodes granulatus, Ixodes persulcatus, and Rhipicephalus microplus as ticks of greatest concern; and rickettsioses, severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus, borreliosis, Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever, anaplasmosis, tick-borne encephalitis, and novel tick-borne viruses as TBDs of greatest concern. Land-use change was expected to have the largest impact on ticks and TBDs, followed by climate change, invasive species, and drug resistance. The three biggest research hurdles were lack of funding, lack of awareness, and lack of taxonomic expertise. Addressing these near-term priorities and hurdles while harmonizing research efforts and surveillance within, and between countries may help lay the groundwork for future research and surveillance efforts in the region, and may also enhance the region's preparedness, ultimately leading to better health outcomes for the region.

Scope and question
The review focuses on ticks and tick-borne diseases (TBDs) in Southeast Asia, with a specific emphasis on Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus (SFTSV). The central question is to identify research priorities, hurdles, and opportunities for improving health outcomes and knowledge in the region related to SFTSV.
Evidence base and method
The review was conducted through a survey of 25 experts to identify tick species and TBDs of greatest concern in Southeast Asia, including SFTSV. The methodology involved expert opinion to determine the most impactful drivers and research challenges.
Consensus and conclusions
Experts identified SFTSV as a significant TBD of concern in Southeast Asia. They also highlighted that land-use change is expected to have the largest impact on ticks and TBDs, followed by climate change, invasive species, and drug resistance. These findings suggest that SFTSV is a critical focus for understanding TBD dynamics in the region.
Controversies and gaps
The review notes that ticks and TBDs in Southeast Asia are poorly understood, which complicates surveillance and mitigation efforts. There is a lack of comprehensive data on the distribution, ecology, and transmission of SFTSV, as well as limited awareness and taxonomic expertise related to the virus.
Research and practice implications
The review emphasizes the need to address research and surveillance gaps related to SFTSV. Priorities include increasing funding, raising awareness, and improving taxonomic expertise to better understand and manage the virus. Harmonizing regional efforts could enhance preparedness and lead to improved health outcomes.

新闻简报Seoul is expanding tick surveillance in parks and walking trails as autumn approaches, following the first Severe Fever with Thrombocytopenia Syndrome (SFTS) death of the year on Jeju Island on August 28, 2026. The Seoul Research Institute of Public Health and Environment is monitoring high-risk green spaces, including Han River parks, ecological reserves, hiking paths, barefoot walking zones, and dog parks, from April through October. As of August, 480 ticks have been collected with no SFTS virus detected, and 108 animals tested between April and late July showed no positives. SFTS, transmitted by infected hard ticks like the Asian bush tick, has a fatality rate of 12 to 30 percent due to the absence of targeted treatments or vaccines. Health officials advise using repellents, wearing long sleeves, and checking for ticks after outdoor activities. The agency also conducts annual animal surveillance tests to monitor potential viral reservoirs.

By Lee Kyung-min Published Aug 28, 2026 2:07 pm KST gettyimagesbank Seoul is stepping up surveillance of disease-carrying ticks in parks and along walking trails as autumn approaches, following Korea's first Severe Fever with Thrombocytopenia Syndrome (SFTS) death of the year, reported on Jeju Island on Aug. 5. From April through October, the Seoul Research Institute of Public Health and Environment is conducting intensive monitoring across high-risk green spaces. Field researchers are targeting Han River parks, ecological reserves, popular hiking paths, barefoot walking zones and dog parks where dense vegetation and human activity intersect. The institute has collected approximately 480 ticks through August, with laboratory tests confirming zero cases of the SFTS virus among the gathered specimens so far. First identified in East Asia, SFTS is a viral infection transmitted through the bite of an infected hard tick, primarily the Asian bush tick (Haemaphysalis longicornis). The illness carries a high fatality rate — often between 12 and 30 percent — due to the absence of targeted antiviral treatments or preventive vaccines. Symptoms typically include high fever, muscle aches, nausea, vomiting and diarrhea, alongside a sharp drop in blood platelets and white blood cells that can cause severe complications in vulnerable patients. Because hard ticks are remarkably small — typically measuring 1 to 9 millimeters — and feed on blood at every stage of development, they are often difficult to spot. Health officials advise anyone who discovers an attached tick to carefully extract it using fine-tipped tweezers, pulling straight up without crushing the body, or to visit a clinic. Patients who develop fever or gastrointestinal symptoms within a 14-day incubation period following a bite should seek immediate medical care.
