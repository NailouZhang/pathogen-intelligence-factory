Phylogeographic epidemiology of Dabie bandavirus in East Asia: divergent transmission networks and genotype‐linked clinical severity.

Authors: Le-Tian Fang, Jiluo Liu, Jimin Sun, Ping Li, Zhongfan Wang, Hong-Xia Ni, Ling Ye, Yi-Wei Shi, Xin Yang, Han-Zheng Mu
Original abstract (English)
BACKGROUND: Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS), caused by Dabie bandavirus (SFTSV), exhibits geographically decoupled incidence and fatality patterns across East Asia. We aimed to elucidate the distinct ecological drivers and phylogeographic dynamics underlying this inland-coastal epidemiological divergence. METHODS: Integrating 1820 high-quality global genomes of SFTSV with well-characterized clinical cohorts (936 patients) and nationwide surveillance data (27,457 cases) from China, we constructed a comprehensive analytical framework. Ecological modeling, Bayesian phylogeography, and genotype-phenotype association analyses were employed to trace the evolutionary trajectories and clinical implications of the virus. RESULTS: A pronounced "inland-high-incidence vs. coastal-high-fatality" pattern of SFTS was identified. The incidence of SFTS exhibited divergent sensitivities to meteorological factors; inland transmission was sensitive to thermal fluctuations, whereas coastal dynamics were constrained by a sunshine threshold (> 200 h/month). In contrast, spatial divergence in clinical severity correlated with the distribution of regional viral genetic structures. Inland regions mainly co-circulated genotypes A, C, and D, while coastal regions were dominated by genotype B. Zhejiang province was identified as a genetic hub with significantly higher recombination frequencies than inland regions (11.0% vs. Bayesian phylogeographic inference indicated frequent lineage exchange of Zhejiang province in China with the Republic of Korea and Japan. Clinically, genotypes B and D were associated with elevated mortality in coastal and inland regions, respectively, suggesting that the severe coastal phenotype is shaped by its genotype B-dominated structure. Additionally, the RdRp-N828S mutation emerged as a robust molecular correlate of fatal outcomes, warranting further functional validation. CONCLUSIONS: Divergent meteorological factors and plausible maritime transmission networks may underlie the geographically decoupled epidemiology of SFTS. These findings highlight that risk assessment must extend beyond incidence alone and provide a phylogeographically informed framework for targeted surveillance and genotype-specific interventions in high-risk hotspots.

Original abstract (English)
Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus (SFTSV), officially designated as Dabie bandavirus by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV), is an emerging zoonotic pathogen belonging to the order Bunyavirales and the family Phenuiviridae. First isolated and identified in 2010 from the serum of patients with severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) in China, SFTSV has since been reported in multiple provinces across China, as well as in South Korea, Japan, Vietnam, the United Arab Emirates (including Dubai), and Pakistan. With a case fatality rate of up to 30%, SFTSV poses a serious threat to public health and carries the potential for broader global spread. In nature, SFTSV is primarily transmitted through tick bites, although direct human-to-human or animal-to-human transmission can also occur through exposure to the blood, respiratory secretions, or other bodily fluids of infected individuals or animals. The pathogenic mechanisms of SFTSV are complex and multifaceted, involving the coordinated actions of multiple viral proteins, host epigenetic regulation, and virus–vector interactions. In this review, we take tick bites as the starting point to describe how SFTSV achieves cross-species transmission through ticks, summarize the viral life cycle, including internalization, replication, assembly, and egress, and discuss the antagonistic interplay between the viral life cycle and host defense responses, with the aim of providing a solid theoretical foundation for understanding the infection and pathogenesis of SFTSV.

A newly identified tick-borne virus may help explain why some patients in China develop symptoms resembling a dangerous hemorrhagic fever even though tests for the expected virus come back negative. Researchers identified the virus as Asian longhorned tick nairovirus, or ALTNV. Among 3,163 patients tested in areas where tick-borne viruses circulate, 10.4% showed evidence of ALTNV infection. Among patients who tested negative for Dabie bandavirus, or DBV, 15.1% tested positive for ALTNV. DBV causes severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome, or SFTS, a tick-borne disease found across Asia. Thrombocytopenia means a person has an abnormally low number of platelets, the blood cells that help with clotting. The disease has a reported fatality rate of 10% to 30%, and the World Health Organization lists DBV as a priority for research. Negative DBV tests reveal another virus Yet about 30% of patients with signs and symptoms suggesting DBV infection test negative for the virus. That unexplained group prompted researchers led by the State Key Laboratory of Pathogen and Biosecurity in Beijing to search for another possible cause. The team collected samples from people who had been bitten by ticks and sought care at sentinel hospitals in parts of China where tick-borne viruses circulate. Using RNA sequencing and viral isolation, they identified ALTNV and determined that it belongs to a group of viruses known as orthonairoviruses within the Nairoviridae family. Genetic analysis showed that ALTNV shared less than 80% amino acid similarity with other species in the same genus, supporting its identification as a distinct virus. The researchers then tested whether ALTNV could reproduce in the laboratory. Serum from patients that contained ALTNV RNA produced viral growth in cultured cells. Immunofluorescence testing detected ALTNV viral antigens, while electron microscopy revealed features characteristic of orthonairoviruses. The virus was also able to replicate in several types of cells. Coinfection brought more severe outcomes The illnesses seen in patients differed depending on whether ALTNV occurred alone or together with DBV. Among patients infected only with ALTNV, fatigue was the most common symptom, affecting 84%. Gastrointestinal problems occurred in 80%, while 38% developed thrombocytopenia and 36% had elevated aminotransferase levels, which can indicate inflammation or injury involving the liver or other tissues. All patients with ALTNV infection alone recovered completely. The picture was more concerning among people infected with both ALTNV and DBV. Among 38 coinfected patients, 61% developed respiratory symptoms, compared with 38% of patients infected with DBV alone. Kidney impairment occurred in 84% of coinfected patients, compared with 66% of those with DBV alone. Seven of the 38 patients with both viruses died, representing 18.4% of the coinfected group. Asian longhorned ticks carried ALTNV The researchers also investigated how widely ALTNV was circulating among ticks. Laboratory experiments also showed that this tick species could transmit ALTNV to mice, providing evidence that it can carry and spread the virus. A shared tick complicates diagnosis That overlap could complicate diagnosis. ALTNV and DBV circulate in the same regions, share a tick vector, and can produce overlapping symptoms, making it difficult to determine which infection a patient has based on symptoms alone. “The cocirculation of ALTNV and DBV in the same areas, coupled with their shared tick vector, underscores the need for improved differential diagnosis between infections with these viruses in regions where SFTS is endemic,” the authors concluded. The virus, called Asian longhorned tick nairovirus (ALTNV), was identified in patients who had been bitten by ticks and developed symptoms including fever, fatigue and gastrointestinal problems. The discovery, published in the New England Journal of Medicine, is another reminder of how much scientists continue to learn about the viruses and other germs that ticks can carry. What Is ALTNV? ALTNV is a newly identified virus in the orthonairovirus group. Researchers discovered it while investigating patients in China who had been bitten by ticks and developed fever and other symptoms but who were negative for all currently known viruses. The researchers tested 3,163 patients and found evidence of ALTNV infection in about 10 percent of them. Among the patients who had ALTNV alone, the most common symptoms included fatigue, digestive problems, fever and low platelet counts, also known as thrombocytopenia. All 56 patients with ALTNV alone recovered. Some patients were infected with both ALTNV and Dabie bandavirus (DBV), the virus that causes severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome, or SFTS (an illness unique to China). Seven of the 38 patients with both infections died. The researchers found that people with both viruses tended to have more severe illness than those infected with ALTNV alone. What Is SFTS? Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome, or SFTS, is a serious illness caused by Dabie bandavirus. This illness is not associated with any other ticks except the Asian Long Horned tick. This illness is only found in China. The name describes two of its main features: a high fever and a low platelet count. Platelets are blood cells that help the body form clots. SFTS can cause serious complications and can be fatal. Reported fatality rates in areas where the disease occurs have been about 10 percent to 30 percent. Importantly, that mortality rate refers to SFTS caused by Dabie bandavirus, not ALTNV alone. In the new study, all patients who had ALTNV alone recovered. The deaths occurred among patients who were infected with both ALTNV and Dabie bandavirus. What Does This Mean for the United States? There is currently no evidence that ALTNV is causing human illness in the United States. The virus was identified in patients in China. This is important information for travelers, those going to China and those returning from trips to China. Please inform your medical provider of your itinerary as these details will help them to deliver the best care to you. However, the tick associated with ALTNV, the Asian longhorned tick (Haemaphysalis longicornis), is already present in parts of the United States, including New York. The presence of the tick does not mean that ALTNV is circulating here. The United States has many other tick-borne diseases to be aware of. In New York, these include: Lyme Disease Anaplasmosis Ehrlichiosis Babesiosis Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever Bourbon Virus STARI Alpha-Gal Syndrome Ticks Carry Many Different Germs One important takeaway from this discovery is that ticks can carry many different viruses, bacteria and parasites, and scientists continue to improve their ability to identify them. Many tick-borne illnesses can cause similar symptoms, such as fever, fatigue, headache and muscle aches. Understanding which infections are present in a particular region, along with a person’s history of tick exposure, can help healthcare providers determine what testing and treatment may be appropriate. How to Protect Yourself From Tick Bites The best way to prevent tick-borne illness is to prevent tick bites. When spending time outdoors: Use tick repellent. Follow the product directions and choose a repellent appropriate for your age and activity. Cover up. Wear long pants and consider tucking pants into socks when walking through areas where ticks may be present. Check for ticks. Check yourself and your children after spending time outdoors. Look carefully around the scalp and hairline, behind the ears and knees, around the waist and in other areas where ticks can hide. Check pets, too. Dogs and other pets can carry ticks into the home. Remove ticks promptly. If you find an attached tick, remove it as soon as possible with fine-tipped tweezers. If you develop a fever, unusual fatigue, rash or other symptoms after a tick bite or spending time in an area where ticks are common, contact your healthcare provider. Many tick-borne illnesses can look similar to one another in the early stages, so your symptoms and recent exposure are important information for your physician. The Bottom Line The discovery of ALTNV does not mean this virus is circulating in the United States. It does show that scientists are continuing to discover new germs carried by ticks and improve the ways they identify them. For families, the advice remains simple: prevent tick bites, check for ticks after spending time outdoors and remove attached ticks promptly. New Tick Virus Found in China

News Brief
Scientists in China have identified a new tick-borne virus, named Asian longhorned tick nairovirus (ALTNV), which can cause fever, fatigue, and digestive problems. The discovery, reported in the New England Journal of Medicine, was made by researchers at the State Key Laboratory of Pathogen and Biosecurity in Beijing. Among 3,163 patients examined, 10.4% showed evidence of ALTNV infection, with 15.1% of those who tested negative for Dabie bandavirus (DBV) being found to have ALTNV. The virus was detected in about 1.3% of ticks tested, with the highest rate in Asian longhorned ticks. While the study suggests that coinfections with ALTNV and DBV may lead to more severe outcomes, it does not confirm the virus's full risk or prevalence. Further research is needed to understand its impact on human health and its role in tick-borne disease transmission. The findings highlight the importance of surveillance and better diagnostic tools to distinguish between similar infections.

Scientists in China have identified a previously unknown virus carried by ticks that can make people sick with fever, tiredness and stomach problems. The discovery may help explain why some patients who appear to have a known tick-borne disease repeatedly test negative for it. The new virus has been named Asian longhorned tick nairovirus, or ALTNV. Researchers led by scientists at the State Key Laboratory of Pathogen and Biosecurity in Beijing reported the discovery in the New England Journal of Medicine. Their work suggests the virus is circulating across a surprisingly wide area of China. Ticks are small parasites that feed on the blood of animals and people. During feeding, some species can pass viruses, bacteria and other disease-causing organisms into the bloodstream. Tick-borne diseases are a growing public health concern in many parts of the world. The researchers began investigating patients with symptoms similar to severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome, often shortened to SFTS. This serious illness is caused by Dabie bandavirus, or DBV, and occurs in several Asian countries. It can cause high fever, extreme tiredness and dangerously low levels of platelets, the blood cells that help stop bleeding. DBV infection can be severe, with previous estimates suggesting that roughly 10% to 30% of diagnosed patients may die. Yet doctors had noticed a puzzle. About 30% of patients with signs that looked like the disease tested negative for the virus, raising the possibility that another infection was producing similar symptoms. To investigate, scientists collected samples from people who had experienced tick bites and were treated at hospitals in areas where tick-borne viruses are common. They examined genetic material in the samples and isolated the newly discovered virus. Further laboratory work confirmed that ALTNV could reproduce inside several types of cells. The scale of the testing was substantial. Among 3,163 patients examined, 10.4% showed evidence of ALTNV infection based on either viral genetic material or antibodies produced by the immune system. Among patients who had tested negative for DBV, 15.1% showed evidence of the new virus. People infected only with ALTNV commonly experienced fatigue, which affected 84% of cases. About 80% developed digestive problems, while 38% had low platelet levels. Around 36% had higher levels of certain enzymes in their blood that can rise when the liver or other tissues are irritated or injured. Encouragingly, the patients identified as having ALTNV alone recovered completely in this study. That does not yet establish how dangerous the virus may be in every group of people, however. Newly discovered infections often require years of monitoring before scientists understand their full range of symptoms and risks. The researchers also found people infected with both ALTNV and DBV at the same time. There were 38 such patients, and their illnesses appeared more serious in some respects than infections involving DBV alone. Respiratory symptoms and signs of kidney problems were more common in the patients with both viruses. Seven of the 38 people with both infections died, representing 18.4% of that group. The study cannot by itself prove that ALTNV made the DBV infections more deadly, because the number of coinfected patients was small and other differences could have affected their outcomes. Still, the finding gives researchers an important reason to investigate coinfection more closely. The scientists then looked for the virus in ticks themselves. They tested 47,428 ticks collected from 15 Chinese provinces and detected ALTNV genetic material in about 1.3% of them. The highest rate was found in the Asian longhorned tick, Haemaphysalis longicornis, at about 1.8%. This tick species is widespread in parts of Asia and has also appeared in other regions of the world. In laboratory experiments, the researchers showed that Asian longhorned ticks could transmit ALTNV to mice. This provides evidence that the tick can act as a carrier of the virus, although transmission patterns in people still need further study. The discovery has practical importance because ALTNV and DBV can circulate in the same places and can be carried by the same tick species. Patients may also develop overlapping symptoms, making it difficult for doctors to know which virus is responsible based on symptoms alone. Better diagnostic tests could help doctors distinguish between the infections. The study is strong because researchers combined patient testing, virus isolation, genetic analysis, laboratory experiments and a very large survey of ticks. Together, these different forms of evidence make a convincing case that ALTNV is a genuine tick-borne virus infecting people. The work also offers a possible explanation for some previously unexplained illnesses that resembled DBV infection. Important questions remain. Researchers need to determine how common ALTNV infection is outside the areas studied, whether it can cause severe disease on its own, which people are most vulnerable and whether other animals help maintain the virus in nature. Scientists will also need to watch for the virus in other countries where Asian longhorned ticks are found. For now, the discovery does not suggest a new global emergency. Instead, it shows how much remains unknown about viruses carried by ticks and why surveillance matters. Identifying ALTNV should make it easier to study unexplained fever after tick bites and could eventually help doctors provide more accurate diagnoses. (Griffin Dill/University of Maine via AP) Chinese researchers, working with U.S. collaborators, have identified a previously unknown tick-borne virus that appears to be responsible for hundreds of previously unexplained fever cases in China. The new pathogen, named Asian longhorned tick nairovirus (ALTNV), belongs to the genus Orthonairovirus in the Nairoviridae family. The findings were published in The New England Journal of Medicine on Sept. 2. The University of Minnesota’s Center for Infectious Disease Research and Policy (CIDRAP) also reported on the discovery. ALTNV infection is associated with a flu-like illness marked by fever, fatigue, gastrointestinal symptoms, and abnormal laboratory results. The researchers also found evidence that co-infection with ALTNV and Dabie bandavirus (DBV) can lead to more serious illness. How Virus Was Found The discovery began with a medical mystery. In some parts of China, more than 30 percent of patients with symptoms resembling severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) test negative for DBV, the virus known to cause the disease. SFTS is a tick-borne illness that can cause high fever and dangerously low platelet counts. Its reported case-fatality rate is between 10 and 30 percent. The unexplained cases suggested that another pathogen might be circulating. Researchers collected samples from patients with fever and a history of tick exposure at sentinel hospitals in areas of China where tick-borne diseases are common. They used RNA sequencing and virus-isolation techniques to identify the pathogen. Genetic analysis placed ALTNV in the Orthonairovirus genus. Its amino acid sequence was less than 80.04 percent identical to those of other known species in the genus, supporting its classification as a distinct virus. The researchers tested 3,163 patients with fever and a history of tick exposure. Overall, 10.4 percent tested positive for ALTNV based on detection of viral RNA or antibodies. Among patients who had tested negative for DBV, 15.1 percent were positive for ALTNV. Patients infected only with ALTNV generally developed an influenza-like illness. Fatigue was reported in 84 percent of patients, gastrointestinal symptoms in 80 percent, low platelet counts in 38 percent, and elevated liver enzymes in 36 percent. All patients with ALTNV alone recovered without lasting complications. The picture was different among patients infected with both viruses. Of 38 patients with ALTNV and DBV coinfection, seven—18.4 percent—died. Coinfected patients also had higher rates of respiratory symptoms and impaired kidney function than patients infected with DBV alone. The Tick Behind the Virus The researchers also tested 47,428 ticks collected in 15 Chinese provinces. ALTNV RNA was detected in 1.29 percent of them, with the highest detection rate found in Haemaphysalis longicornis, commonly known as the Asian longhorned tick, at 1.75 percent. Laboratory experiments showed that H. longicornis can transmit ALTNV to mice. Infected mice developed tissue changes and produced antibodies against the virus. The findings provide evidence that ALTNV is a newly discovered emerging tick-borne virus associated with human illness. The researchers said the fact that ALTNV and DBV circulate in the same areas and share the same tick vector highlights the need for better testing to distinguish between the two infections. Chinese Military Scientists Included The study was led by seven researchers from Beijing's State Key Laboratory of Pathogen and Biosecurity, which is under the administration of Academy of Military Medical Sciences (AMMS). Two other authors are listed as directly affiliated with the AMMS, China's top-level military research institution. U.S. collaborators included Ellen Owen of the University of Washington and Jonathan E. Owen of Emory University. Potential Implications for US According to the Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service of the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA), the Asian longhorned tick has been found across much of the eastern United States, from New York south to Georgia and west to Missouri. The invasive species can heavily infest livestock and poses a potential threat to animal and human health by transmitting pathogens. However, the USDA currently has no evidence that ALTNV is circulating in U.S. tick populations or causing human infections in the United States
