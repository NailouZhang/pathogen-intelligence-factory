马尔堡病毒在卢旺达康复者中的持续存在及临床并发症:一项纵向研究方案。

Marburg virus persistence and clinical complications among recovered persons in Rwanda: a longitudinal study protocol.
作者： Jcs Ngabonziza, Eric Seruyange, Nouh Saad Mohamed, Polina Brangel, Albert Tuyishime, Mary J Choi, Christine Dahlke, Eric Remera, James Kagame, Marrigje J Kreuger
摘要中文翻译INTRODUCTION: 马尔堡病毒 (MARV) 引起一种潜在高度致命的出血热疾病,威胁全球卫生安全,目前对病毒持续存在、康复者长期临床结局以及保护性免疫方面的知识仍有限。现有的协议和技术指南为安全样本采集、临床监测和暴发反应提供了框架,但在非洲环境中的系统性研究方法却很少。本方案描述了在卢旺达2024年暴发后开展的马尔堡病毒(MARV)合作研究(PREMAV)。PREMAV是一项纵向研究,旨在调查康复者体液中MARV的持续存在,描述相关临床并发症,识别与病毒持续存在相关的因素,分类保护性免疫应答,并在病毒持续存在期间发现基因组变异。METHODS AND ANALYSIS: 本方案为一项前瞻性队列研究研究的协议,纳入了在卢旺达幸存马尔堡病毒病(MVD)的个体、具有天然抗MARV抗体的个体、密切接触者和低风险对照组。参与者将在康复后24个月内进行血、尿、精液及其他相关样本的连续采集,并结合持续的临床评估,实验室分析将包括分子检测、血清学分析、免疫学检测和病毒基因组特征分析。统计分析将整合各种数据集以确定相关性。ETHICS AND DISSEMINATION: 本研究将遵循赫尔辛基宣言,并获得卢旺达国家伦理委员会的批准。所有参与者将获得书面知情同意,对弱势群体将采取额外的保护措施。通过去标识化和安全的数据存储确保保密性。所有生物样本将按照国家和国际生物安全标准进行处理。将提供心理社会支持,以应对幸存者的污名和心理健康需求。本研究将产生关于MARV持续存在、临床后遗症和风险因素的关键证据,为幸存者护理指南、传播缓解策略以及未来暴发的准备提供信息。研究结果将通过同行评审的出版物、会议和针对国家及全球利益相关者的政策简报进行传播。去标识化的数据和基因组序列将通过安全平台和公共数据库进行整理和共享,符合伦理和监管要求。

Marburg virus persistence and clinical complications among recovered persons in Rwanda: a longitudinal study protocol.

Authors: Jcs Ngabonziza, Eric Seruyange, Nouh Saad Mohamed, Polina Brangel, Albert Tuyishime, Mary J Choi, Christine Dahlke, Eric Remera, James Kagame, Marrigje J Kreuger
Original abstract (English)
INTRODUCTION: Marburg virus (MARV) causes a potentially highly lethal haemorrhagic fever disease that threatens global health security, and knowledge of viral persistence, long-term clinical outcomes among recovered persons and protective immunity remain limited. Existing protocols and technical guidelines provide a framework for safe sample collection, clinical monitoring and outbreak response, yet systematic approaches to investigate these aspects in African settings are scarce. This protocol describes the Partnership Research on MARV (PREMAV) following the 2024 outbreak in Rwanda. PREMAV is a longitudinal study that was initiated to investigate the persistence of MARV in body fluids of recovered persons, characterises associated clinical complications, identifies factors associated with viral persistence, classifies the protective immune response and discovers genomic variants during viral persistence. METHODS AND ANALYSIS: This is a protocol for a prospective cohort study that enrolled individuals who survived Marburg virus disease (MVD), individuals with natural antibodies for MARV, close contacts and low risk controls in Rwanda. Participants will undergo serial sampling of blood, urine, semen and other relevant specimens over 24 months post-recovery. This will be combined with continuous clinical assessments, and laboratory analyses will include molecular detection, serological profiling, immunological assays and genomic viral characterisation. Statistical analysis will integrate the various datasets to determine associations. ETHICS AND DISSEMINATION: This study will adhere to the Declaration of Helsinki and obtain approval from the Rwanda National Ethics Committee. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants with additional safeguards for vulnerable groups. Confidentiality will be ensured through de-identification and secure data storage. All biological samples will be handled in compliance with national and international biosafety standards. Psychosocial support will be provided to address stigma and mental health needs among survivors. The study will generate critical evidence on MARV persistence, clinical sequelae and risk factors, informing survivor care guidelines, transmission mitigation strategies and future outbreak preparedness. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, conferences and policy briefs targeting national and global stakeholders. De-identified data and genomic sequences will be curated and shared through secure platforms and public repositories in line with ethical and regulatory requirements.

Question and background
The study aimed to address the limited knowledge of Marburg virus (MARV) persistence, long-term clinical outcomes among recovered persons, and protective immunity, given the potential threat to global health security and the scarcity of systematic approaches in African settings.
Design and population
A longitudinal study protocol was developed to investigate MARV persistence in body fluids of recovered persons, characterise associated clinical complications, identify factors associated with viral persistence, classify the protective immune response, and discover genomic variants during viral persistence in Rwanda.
Core methods
The study will enrol individuals who survived Marburg virus disease (MVD), individuals with natural antibodies for MARV, close contacts, and low-risk controls. Participants will undergo serial sampling of blood, urine, semen, and other relevant specimens over 24 months post-recovery, with continuous clinical assessments and laboratory analyses including molecular detection, serological profiling, immunological assays, and genomic viral characterisation.
Main results
The study aims to generate critical evidence on MARV persistence, clinical sequelae, and risk factors, which will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, conferences, and policy briefs targeting national and global stakeholders.
Interpretation and novelty
The findings will inform survivor care guidelines, transmission mitigation strategies, and future outbreak preparedness, contributing novel insights into the persistence and clinical outcomes of MARV infection.
Scientific and public-health significance
The study will provide essential evidence to improve survivor care, mitigate transmission risks, and enhance preparedness for future outbreaks, with implications for surveillance, diagnosis, and treatment of Marburg virus disease.
Limitations and evidence strength
The study's limitations include the potential for selection bias due to enrolment of survivors and close contacts, and the generalisability may be limited to the Rwandan context. The evidence strength is moderate due to the prospective cohort design and comprehensive sampling and analysis protocols.

Strengthening diagnostic capacity for viral hemorrhagic fevers in Forest Guinea: advances in Lassa fever case detection

Authors: Fara Raymond Koundouno, Youssouf Sidibé, Saa Lucien Millimono, Kékoura Ifono, Julia Hinzmann, Hugo Soubrier, Karifa Kourouma, Tamba Elie Millimouno, Fernand M’Bemba Tolno, Faya Moriba Kamano
Original abstract (English)
Viral hemorrhagic fevers (VHFs) remain a major public health threat in resource-limited settings. The 2014-2016 Ebola epidemic exposed critical gaps in laboratory preparedness and diagnostic capacity in most-at-risk countries, including Guinea. In order to implement sustainable in-country laboratory capacity for early detection and rapid response to VHF outbreaks, a decentralized diagnostics laboratory strengthening program started in the forested region of Guinea in Guéckédou (2017) and N'Zérékoré (2021). Between 2017 and 2024, a total of 4683 samples from suspected VHF cases were tested across the two laboratories. Of those, 47 (1%) were identified as positive for VHFs by molecular testing, including the 2021 Ebola virus disease resurgence and Marburg virus disease emergence samples, for which the laboratories supported rapid outbreak detection and response. Notably, 30 Lassa fever cases were laboratory-confirmed with a case fatality rate of 67.9%. Of those, 50.0% and 27.6% of the cases originating from the Guéckédou and N'Zérékoré prefecture, respectively. Prior to this program, Lassa fever detection in Guinea remained anecdotic. One case of yellow fever and one of dengue were also identified by molecular assays. The establishment of locally embedded laboratory capacity enhanced VHFs preparedness, surveillance, and response in Guinea, supporting Lassa fever epidemiology and health-system resilience.

Question and background
The study aimed to strengthen diagnostic capacity for viral hemorrhagic fevers (VHFs) in resource-limited settings, addressing gaps exposed by the 2014-2016 Ebola epidemic in Guinea.
Design and population
A decentralized diagnostics laboratory strengthening program was implemented in Guéckédou and N'Zérékoré, Guinea, from 2017 to 2024, testing 4683 samples from suspected VHF cases.
Core methods
Samples were tested using molecular methods, identifying 47 positive cases, including Lassa fever and Ebola virus disease, with a case fatality rate of 67.9%.
Main results
Of the 4683 samples, 47 (1%) were positive for VHFs, including 30 Lassa fever cases, with a case fatality rate of 67.9%.
Interpretation and novelty
The study confirmed the importance of locally embedded laboratory capacity in enhancing VHFs preparedness, surveillance, and response in Guinea, supporting Lassa fever epidemiology and health-system resilience.
Scientific and public-health significance
The findings highlight the need for sustained investment in laboratory capacity to improve early detection and rapid response to VHF outbreaks in resource-limited settings.
Limitations and evidence strength
The study's limitations include the potential for sampling bias and the need for larger sample sizes to generalize findings, assessed as moderate evidence strength.

A Scientist Bled to Death for Two Weeks. The Virus He Left Behind Is Sitting in His Lab.

News Brief
A Soviet virologist, Nikolai Ustinov, died after accidentally infecting himself with Marburg virus while working in the BSL‐4 lab at VECTOR in Koltsovo, Siberia. The incident, recounted in a Frontline documentary and interviews with former bioweapons scientists, describes a two‐week hemorrhagic illness marked by extensive bleeding. Post‐mortem analysis revealed that the virus had mutated into a more virulent strain, dubbed “Variant U,” which Soviet researchers later weaponized and allegedly stored as a powdered bioweapon. Although the scientist’s death was the only reported case, the continued presence of the variant at the Russian facility raises lingering biosafety concerns. No current outbreak or secondary infections are noted, and the account is historical, based on documentary and interview sources rather than active investigation.
Original source text (English)
4 min read Key Points A Soviet scientist died agonizingly from Marburg virus after accidentally infecting himself during weaponization research at a Siberian biolab. The virus mutated into a deadlier strain inside the infected researcher's body, which Soviet bioweapons scientists then weaponized into powder for potential biological attacks. The weaponized Marburg variant remains stored at a Russian research facility today, raising ongoing concerns about accidental or intentional release decades after the Cold War. Many stories and secrets were lost in the ash heap of the Soviet Union’s collapse. But one fact is not in doubt: Dr. Nikolai Ustinov died painfully. “I can say it was a terrible death,” said Dr. Ken Alibek, formerly Dr. Kanatjan Alibekov, in a 1998 Frontline documentary . “He was dying for about two weeks. It was blood from everywhere, from nose, from eyes, mouth, ears.” Dr. Ustinov had become infected with a disease for which there was no cure. From the moment he contracted Marburg virus (MARV), a member of the Filoviridae family with symptoms similar to its cousin Ebola, his fate was sealed. But while Marburg infections typically come from an exchange of bodily fluids with an infected person, or contact with an Egyptian fruit bat, the manner in which Dr. Ustinov came to be infected is something stranger and far more sinister. Dr. Nikolai Ustinov worked at the State Research Center of Virology and Biotechnology, also known as VECTOR, conducting research at their maximum-security BSL-4 lab in Koltsovo, Siberia. Ustinov was researching the deadly Marburg virus, but not with the intention to find a cure; his work to better understand the disease was with an aim toward weaponizing it. There’s some disagreement as to just how Ustinov contracted Marburg himself. While it’s agreed that an accident occurred in the course of his daily research activity—purposefully infecting guinea pigs with the horrific bleeding disease via syringe—two stories have emerged for how it happened. The aforementioned Frontline report alludes to a second scientist being in the lab, whose syringe-wielding hand slipped and jabbed the finger of Ustinov, who was holding the next small, furry, soon-to-be viral victim. But Annie Jacobsen’s Biological War suggests that Ustinov made the error entirely himself, accidentally injecting his own finger instead of the guinea pig. Once Ustinov realized what had happened, Jacobsen recounts, he took action: “Dr. Ustinov quickly left the lab through one of Vector’s airlocks, took a chemical shower to decontaminate the biohazard suit he was wearing, and used an emergency telephone to call his boss. His supervisor instructed him to go to Vector’s biocontainment hospital, also equipped with submarine-like airlocks.” Ustinov’s wife Yevgenia could only see her husband through a window, and could only speak to him via a telephone, up to and including their final goodbye. She was spared, however, seeing the extent of what the deadly virus was doing to him; his colleagues, according to Frontline , “wrapped him in sheets to his chin during her visits.” But Ustinov understood that his colleagues did need to know the details of what the virus was doing to him. They were viral researchers, after all, and his infection offered a singular opportunity to advance their knowledge. “With clinical detachment,” the U.S. Naval War College recounts in an article on the event, “he documented his own decline in a journal with bloodspotted pages.” Following his death, a team of biohazard suit-adorned doctors went to work conducting an autopsy on Ustinov. They extracted his spleen and liver and removed his blood via syringe. What they discovered was that, while the virus was slowly killing Dr. Ustinov, it was also evolving inside him. “It had transformed,” Jacobsen writes, “into a far more virulent germ than the original strain of Marburg virus Dr. Ustinov had been working with when he accidentally stuck himself with that syringe.” Ustinov’s remains were placed in a “zinc-lined coffin,” and the new strain that had developed in his body was named “Variant U” in his honor. In the Frontline special, Dr. Alibek was asked about what became of “Variant U” after Ustinov died: “INTERVIEWER: What did you do with it? KEN ALIBEK: A virus that was isolated from his organs was used for manufacturing new variants for biological weapons. INTERVIEWER: So it would be more virulent? KEN ALIBEK: Yes, it was more virulent.” Jacobsen writes that by early 1988, the scientists in the USSR’s bioweapons division, Биопрепарат (Biopreparat), began work to mass produce “Variant U.” “They dried it into powder to create a dust to one day be covertly spread around enemy territory and inhaled as part of a biological weapons attack.” Of course, the Marburg-U bioweapon was never unleashed by the Soviet Union before its collapse. In April of 1992, Russian President Boris Yeltsin called for “the termination of research on offensive biological weapons, the dismantlement of experimental technological lines for the production of biological agents and the closure of biological weapons testing facilities.” But that didn’t mean the supply of powdered Variant U was suddenly swept away. “To this day,” Jacobsen concludes, “it is understood to be housed at Vector.” Whether it will ever make its way out has yet to be seen.
