多重泛丝状病毒检测平台的性能与跨地理和资源环境的可重复性评估

Multiplex pan-filovirus assay performance and reproducibility across varied geographical and resource settings
作者： Olivia A. Smith, Sydney Merritt, Jean Paul Kompany, N M VAN DER HOFF, Teri Ann S. Wong, Varney Kamara, Merly Tambu, Megan Halbrook, Jason Kindrachuk, Angelica L. Barrall
摘要中文翻译
查看研究七要素
问题与背景
本研究旨在评估多重泛 丝状病毒 测定的性能和重现性,该测定是一种很有前景的工具,可在血清监测和 疫苗 反应评估中检测对多个 丝状病毒 靶点(包括 马尔堡病毒)的体液免疫。
设计与对象
本研究涉及在两个独立实验室——夏威夷大学火奴鲁鲁分校(UH)和刚果民主共和国(DRC)金沙萨的国立生物医学研究院(INRB)——对来自DRC扬布库的46份样本(包括埃博拉病毒病(EVD)幸存者及其密切接触者)进行检测。此外,在DRC共检测了858份样本,时间点为接种预防性EVD 疫苗(ERVEBO)前后。
核心方法
通过评估泛 丝状病毒 MIA 的精确度和抗原辨别力来评估其性能。该测定在两个独立的实验室地点进行测试,并进行纵向分析以检测 疫苗接种 后抗体反应性的变化。
主要结果
结果显示实验室间差异较低,所有丝状病毒靶标的实验内和实验间变异系数均低于预设阈值。纵向分析显示,疫苗接种后EBOV GP反应性增加,而针对非疫苗 丝状病毒抗原的反应性保持稳定。
解释与创新
研究结果表明,该泛丝状病毒 MIA在不同实验室环境下可产生一致且可重复的结果,凸显其在血清学研究和疫苗应答监测中的潜在应用价值。
科研与公卫意义
结果表明,该检测平台可成为血清学监测和评估针对马尔堡病毒的疫苗抗体应答的有价值工具,有助于在受影响地区改善公共卫生应对措施。
局限与证据强度
本研究的证据较为充分,结果显示实验室间差异低且相关性高。然而,分析仅限于单一地理位置,可能影响结果的普遍性。

Multiplex pan-filovirus assay performance and reproducibility across varied geographical and resource settings

Authors: Olivia A. Smith, Sydney Merritt, Jean Paul Kompany, N M VAN DER HOFF, Teri Ann S. Wong, Varney Kamara, Merly Tambu, Megan Halbrook, Jason Kindrachuk, Angelica L. Barrall
Original abstract (English)
ABSTRACT Multiplex bead-based immunoassays (MIAs) are promising tools for simultaneously detecting humoral immunity to multiple targets, potentially playing a crucial role in serosurveillance and vaccine response assessments. However, evaluation of assay performance is paramount prior to widespread use. This study presents a performance evaluation of a pan-filovirus MIA through characterization of the analytical range for the EBOV glycoprotein (GP) target and assessments of assay precision and antigen discrimination. The precision of the MIA was evaluated by comparing the detection of anti-filovirus antibodies at two independent laboratory sites: the University of Hawai’i, Honolulu (UH), and the Institut National de Recherche Biomédicale (INRB) in Kinshasa, Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC). Forty-six samples from Yambuku, DRC, including Ebola virus Disease (EVD) survivors and close contacts, were tested at both sites. Additionally, 858 samples were tested in DRC before and after vaccination with a prophylactic EVD vaccine, ERVEBO. Results demonstrated low variability between laboratories, with intra-assay and inter-laboratory coefficients of variation below predefined thresholds for all filovirus targets included in the multiplex panel. Analyte correlations between sites were high (r 2 =0.86-0.92). Longitudinal analysis detected increased EBOV GP reactivity following vaccination, while reactivity to non-vaccine filovirus antigens remained stable, consistent with minimal cross-reactivity in a vaccinated cohort. These findings suggest that this pan-filovirus MIA produces reproducible results across distinct laboratory settings and may serve as a useful tool for comparative serologic investigations, serosurveillance, and evaluation of EBOV vaccine-associated antibody responses. IMPORTANCE STATEMENT This study investigates the functionality and intra-laboratory consistency of a novel multiplex bead-based immunoassay with pan-filovirus targets. As part of the evaluation process, the feasibility of using the assay in resource-limited settings was demonstrated in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. This assay holds significant promise as a tool for detecting filovirus-specific antibody responses. By leveraging its multiplex capabilities, it may be used for widespread serosurveillance of high-consequence pathogens, including the pan-filovirus antigens such as Ebolavirus and Marburg virus already incorporated in the assay, as well as other targets of interest.

This study aimed to evaluate the performance and reproducibility of a multiplex pan-filovirus assay, which is a promising tool for detecting humoral immunity to multiple filovirus targets, including Marburg virus, in serosurveillance and vaccine response assessments.
Design and population
The study involved testing 46 samples from Yambuku, DRC, including Ebola virus Disease survivors and close contacts, at two independent laboratory sites: the University of Hawai’i, Honolulu (UH), and the Institut National de Recherche Biomédicale (INRB) in Kinshasa, DRC. Additionally, 858 samples were tested in DRC before and after vaccination with the prophylactic EVD vaccine, ERVEBO.
Core methods
The performance of the pan-filovirus MIA was assessed by evaluating its precision and antigen discrimination. The assay was tested at two independent laboratory sites, and longitudinal analysis was conducted to detect changes in antibody reactivity following vaccination.
Main results
Results showed low variability between laboratories, with intra-assay and inter-laboratory coefficients of variation below predefined thresholds for all filovirus targets. Longitudinal analysis detected increased EBOV GP reactivity after vaccination, while reactivity to non-vaccine filovirus antigens remained stable.
Interpretation and novelty
The findings indicate that the pan-filovirus MIA produces consistent and reproducible results across different laboratory settings, highlighting its potential utility for serologic investigations and vaccine response monitoring.
Scientific and public-health significance
The results suggest that this assay could be a valuable tool for serosurveillance and evaluating antibody responses to vaccines targeting Marburg virus, contributing to improved public health responses in affected regions.
Limitations and evidence strength
The study's evidence is strong, with results demonstrating low variability and high correlations between sites. However, the analysis is limited to a single geographic location, which may affect generalizability.

东非丝状病毒(马尔堡病毒病与苏丹病毒病)的叙事综述:加强区域疾病监测的经验教训

A narrative review of filovirus outbreaks in East Africa: lessons for strengthening regional disease surveillance.
作者： Adewunmi Akingbola, Abiodun Adegbesan, Kolade Adegoke, Favour Peters, Joshua Alabi, Olajumoke Adewole, Petra Mariaria, Adegbesan Abiodun Christopher
摘要中文翻译坦桑尼亚马尔堡病毒病(MVD)与乌干达苏丹病毒病(SVD)的近期暴发,揭示了东非地区丝状病毒(丝状病毒病)威胁的日益增长。这些高致死性病毒的病例死亡率超过50%,因早期检测延迟、监测网络薄弱及跨境协调不足,持续对区域卫生系统构成挑战。坦桑尼亚卡盖拉地区(重要过境枢纽)的暴发凸显了国际传播风险,而乌干达在应对苏丹病毒方面的困境进一步凸显了区域传染病监测的迫切需求。本综述探讨了流行病学趋势、暴发(疫情应对)挑战及区域合作在缓解未来丝状病毒(丝状病毒病)威胁中的作用。文中总结了与马尔堡病毒病及苏丹病毒病相关的关键流行病学特征、传播(传播动态)及应对时间线,为当前区域公共卫生威胁提供数据驱动的背景支撑。通过分析过往及正在进行的暴发,本文提出了加强疾病监测、提升跨境协调及改善快速响应机制的策略。统一的区域应对措施对于防止丝状病毒(丝状病毒病)疫情升级为全球公共卫生危机至关重要。

本综述聚焦东非地区丝状病毒(丝状病毒病)威胁的增长,重点分析坦桑尼亚马尔堡病毒病(MVD)与乌干达苏丹病毒病(SVD)的暴发。核心问题是如何加强区域疾病监测以减轻这些病毒未来暴发的风险。

本综述为东非地区丝状病毒(丝状病毒病)暴发的可用数据之叙事综合。未报告具体数据库、检索日期或荟萃分析方法。证据来源于病例报告、疫情摘要及马尔堡病毒病与苏丹病毒病的公共卫生应对记录。

综述得出结论:坦桑尼亚马尔堡病毒病与乌干达苏丹病毒病暴发,显示区域卫生系统在应对丝状病毒(丝状病毒病)时的脆弱性。这些暴发的特点为高病例死亡率及早期检测与跨境协调方面的挑战。
争议与缺口
综述指出,这些暴发凸显了疾病监测与应对机制中的显著缺口。目前对这些病毒的传播(传播动态)与地理扩散的数据有限,且当前公共卫生策略的有效性仍不明确。
科研与实践启示
综述强调需改善疾病监测、加强跨境协调及完善快速响应机制,以应对丝状病毒(丝状病毒病)暴发。同时建议加强公共卫生基础设施建设并提升区域防范能力。

A narrative review of filovirus outbreaks in East Africa: lessons for strengthening regional disease surveillance.

Authors: Adewunmi Akingbola, Abiodun Adegbesan, Kolade Adegoke, Favour Peters, Joshua Alabi, Olajumoke Adewole, Petra Mariaria, Adegbesan Abiodun Christopher
Original abstract (English)
The recent outbreaks of Marburg virus disease (MVD) in Tanzania and Sudan virus disease (SVD) in Uganda reveal the growing threat of filoviruses in East Africa. These highly lethal viruses, with case fatality rates exceeding 50%, continue to challenge regional health systems due to delayed detection, weak surveillance networks, and limited cross-border coordination. The outbreak in Tanzania's Kagera region, a major transit hub, highlights the risk of international spread, while Uganda's struggle with Sudan virus further emphasizes the urgent need for regional infectious disease surveillance. This review paper explores the epidemiological trends, challenges in outbreak response, and the role of regional cooperation in mitigating future filovirus threats. It summarizes key epidemiological features, transmission dynamics, and response timelines associated with both outbreaks to provide data-driven context to the current regional public health threat. By analyzing past and ongoing outbreaks, we propose strategies to strengthen disease surveillance, enhance cross-border coordination, and improve rapid response mechanisms. A unified regional approach is critical to preventing filovirus epidemics from escalating into global public health crises.
View review five-element analysis
Scope and question
This review focuses on the growing threat of filoviruses in East Africa, specifically examining Marburg virus disease (MVD) outbreaks in Tanzania and Sudan virus disease (SVD) in Uganda. The central question is how to strengthen regional disease surveillance to mitigate future outbreaks of these viruses.
Evidence base and method
The review is a narrative synthesis of available data on filovirus outbreaks in East Africa. It does not report specific databases, search dates, or meta-analysis methods. The evidence is derived from case reports, outbreak summaries, and public health responses to MVD and SVD.
Consensus and conclusions
The review concludes that Marburg virus disease outbreaks in Tanzania and Sudan virus disease in Uganda demonstrate the vulnerability of regional health systems to filoviruses. These outbreaks are characterized by high case fatality rates and challenges in early detection and cross-border coordination.
Controversies and gaps
The review notes that the outbreaks highlight significant gaps in surveillance and response mechanisms. There is limited data on the transmission dynamics and geographic spread of these viruses, and the effectiveness of current public health strategies remains unclear.
Research and practice implications
The review emphasizes the need for improved disease surveillance, enhanced cross-border coordination, and rapid response mechanisms to address filovirus outbreaks. It also suggests the importance of strengthening public health infrastructure and increasing regional preparedness.

At a glance CDC began working in Uganda in 1991 and established a country office in 2000. CDC works with the Ministry of Health (MOH) and other partners to strengthen sustainable public health systems, reduce the burden of HIV and malaria, expand access to life-saving vaccines, and respond quickly to disease outbreaks and other health emergencies. CDC’s work aims to protect the health of Americans and public health around the world. Accomplishments Supported population and case-based surveillance to generate up-to-date data to drive evidence-based HIV, TB, and other health programs and policies. Supported over 85 laboratories in attaining international accreditation—up from only 7 in 2015. Strengthened Uganda workforce capacity through training of 878 fellows from the Field Epidemiology Training Program (FETP) and 5 Lab Leadership Fellows. Responded to more than 651 outbreak events, including outbreaks of monkeypox, Ebola, Marburg virus disease, Rift Valley fever, Crimean Congo hemorrhagic fever, yellow fever, COVID-19, influenza, measles, cholera, typhoid, meningitis, and anthrax. Supported MOH in reaching more than 9,000 children with catch-up vaccinations in targeted districts and linked 90% of identified zero-dose children to immunization services. Provided life-saving HIV treatment to nearly 800,000 people living with HIV (with 97% achieving viral suppression) and supported more than 860,000 pregnant women with HIV testing services—linking 99% of those diagnosed to antiretroviral treatment to prevent mother-to-child transmission in 2024. Uganda’s third Population-based HIV Impact Assessment, launched in 2025, was fully implemented by Ugandan institutions—marking a major milestone in national ownership—and newly includes data on select noncommunicable diseases to support integrated health planning and accelerate progress toward ending the HIV epidemic. Global health security Renowned for its expertise in disease detection, outbreak investigation, laboratory systems and emergency response, CDC works with the MOH and local partners to support activities aligned to the Global Health Security Agenda (GHSA) with a focus on strengthening the country’s public health systems across the following core areas: Laboratory systems strengthening Since 2003, CDC has supported MOH to build a strong national laboratory network and expand diagnostic capacity. Today, this network delivers efficient, quality-assured diagnostic and surveillance services that guide patient care, strengthen surveillance, and support outbreak response. Established in 2011, Uganda’s national integrated sample transport and referral network now moves more than 4 million samples annually for HIV and TB testing, disease monitoring, and outbreak response. In 2014, MOH launched centralized testing, which has grown from just 8 hubs to 100 hubs and six standby mobile units to expand timely access to testing across the country. Additionally, the CDC-supported National Equipment Calibration Laboratory (NECL) has ensured uninterrupted testing by certifying more than 1,000 biosafety cabinets and calibrating a wide range of laboratory instruments across Uganda’s diagnostic network. These efforts keep equipment accurate and reliable, strengthen infectious disease detection that protects U.S. health security, and safeguard laboratory staff and the environment. Workforce development In 2015, CDC collaborated with MOH and Makerere University School of Public Health to establish FETP to build skills in disease detection, data use, and outbreak response across all levels of the health system. The program, which trains public health professionals at national (advanced), regional (intermediate), and district (frontline) levels, is the only FETP in Africa that enrolls post–master’s degree fellows. Building on the success of FETP, CDC collaborated with the Uganda National Institute of Public Health to launch the complementary Laboratory Leadership Program to strengthen laboratory management and leadership capacity. FETP trainees and alumni continue to play a vital role in Uganda’s outbreak investigations and response, including during recent Ebola and monkeypox outbreaks. Emergency response In 2013, CDC supported MOH to establish Uganda’s Public Health Emergency Operations Center (PHEOC) to coordinate preparedness, response, and recovery from public health emergencies. Over the past decade, the national PHEOC has managed more than 70 major public health emergencies (of international concern). To strengthen response capacity at the source, CDC supported MOH in establishing six of the 13 regional PHEOCs, all of which receive ongoing technical support from CDC. During the COVID-19 pandemic and recent Ebola and monkeypox responses, CDC and partners supported MOH to contain disease spread and strengthen emergency operations, cross-border surveillance, vaccination, laboratory, and workforce capacity. Since 2018, CDC has also supported disease surveillance in 45 border districts and 66 points of entry, enabling rapid detection and containment of major outbreaks, including Ebola (2019, 2022, and 2025)—all contained within 90 days with no cross-border spread—cholera (2024), and monkeypox (2025). Integrated Health Information/Data Systems CDC supports Uganda’s MOH to strengthen national health information systems through digital solutions that promote integration, sustainability, and government ownership. With CDC support, the Ministry established a National Digital Health Data Warehouse to consolidate data across programs, provide real-time analytics, and support evidence-based decisions to improve patient care, surveillance, and overall health system performance. Aug 12, 2026 HIV and TB As a key implementer of the U.S. President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR), CDC plays an essential role in the fight against HIV and TB. With unmatched scientific and technical knowledge and long-standing relationships with ministries of health, CDC is uniquely positioned to advance HIV, TB, and other global health security activities that keep Americans safe at home and abroad. Through PEPFAR, CDC provides critical support to Uganda's public health infrastructure, improving the country's ability to prevent, detect, and respond to HIV, TB, and other infectious diseases and minimizing their risk from entering the U.S. Spotlight Uganda is one of five countries conducting the TB Guidance for Adaptable Patient-Centered Service (TB GAPS) operational research study. The research aims to identify better ways to screen, diagnose, and prevent TB in children and adults For Public Health HIV and TB Overview: Uganda Malaria Malaria remains a major health challenge in Uganda, causing 37% of hospital admissions. CDC has supported MOH and partners to implement malaria prevention and control activities, including improving diagnosis and treatment, preventing malaria during pregnancy, and promoting community awareness. Since 2006, collective efforts to distribute 107,000 mosquito nets, spray 750,000 homes (protecting 2.7 million people), and provide 1.4 million rapid tests have helped to reduce child mortality by 53% and lower malaria prevalence among children under five from 42% to 9% (2019 Uganda Malaria Indicator Survey). Immunization Working through MOH’s Uganda National Expanded Program on Immunization (UNEPI), CDC helps to strengthen Uganda’s immunization systems that protect children and communities from vaccine-preventable diseases. Through the Global Immunization Division, CDC supports routine vaccination, new vaccine introductions, and rapid outbreak response for diseases such as polio, measles, and Ebola. CDC also supports health worker training, data use, and community outreach to expand vaccine access, reach children who have missed routine vaccines, and respond quickly to outbreaks before they spread. Uganda recently introduced the R21 malaria vaccine, marking a major step in reducing childhood illness and death. CDC supported the Ministry of Health in the vaccine’s rollout. CDC also played a key role in introducing the MVA-BN monkeypox vaccine by providing technical support, data to guide vaccination strategies, last-mile distribution, and real-time monitoring—with close to 200,000 high-risk persons already vaccinated. Success story spotlight Uganda Defeats Ebola With Its Fastest Response Ever Healthcare workers in the field reviewing forms and recording data. When Uganda confirmed its eighth Ebola outbreak on January 30, 2025, the Ministry of Health (MOH) projected it could last more than 200 days. The outbreak ended in 87 days, and all ten treated patients survived—marking Uganda's highest survival rate to date. The outbreak, caused by Sudan virus, reported 14 cases and 4 deaths. Those low numbers reflect decades of preparation, and a coordinated, immediate response led by CDC in partnership with the Ugandan MOH. CDC has been Uganda's trusted public health partner since 1991. When Ebola was confirmed in 2025, strong public health systems were already in place. A CDC-supported Public Health Emergency Operations Centre activated immediately. Field Epidemiology Training Program fellows were deployed to identify and monitor 600 high-risk contacts, and laboratory networks processed over 2,500 samples—returning results within 12 hours of symptom onset. The response was fast and comprehensive. CDC trained infection prevention control leads across more than 1,000 healthcare facilities. Real-time data dashboards helped health officials make decisions as the outbreak spread to new districts. At Entebbe International Airport, staff screened over 500,000 travelers in just eight weeks, preventing cross-border spread and reducing the risk of Ebola reaching the United States. Epidemiologists modeled transmission chains to support surveillance through the outbreaks' final days. The improvements from past responses are clear. During Uganda's 2000 Ebola outbreak, the virus killed 224 people. By comparison, in 2025, despite the outbreak occurring in a densely populated urban area with higher transmission risk, only 4 people lost their lives. Decades of CDC support helped Uganda build the expertise, infrastructure, and readiness needed to respond swiftly stopping Ebola at its source and protecting communities in Uganda and the United States. John Mfungo Kiki & Miles Productions 20 August 2026 During the Marburg Virus Disease (MVD) outbreak in early 2025, nurse Aloysia Emmanuel Bakuza knew that one of the most effective tools for preventing infection was also one of the simplest: water. Working at Biharamulo District Hospital, the district's designated treatment facility during the outbreak, Aloysia and her colleagues depended on water every day for handwashing, cleaning and disinfection, among the critical measures for protecting patients, caregivers and health workers from infection. Yet the hospital relied on an unreliable water authority supply that was available only about three days each week. "With a disease like Marburg, appropriate hygiene practice is not optional," Aloysia recalls. "Every hand washed, every surface cleaned and every piece of protective equipment washed contributes to preventing infections. Water is at the centre of everything we do." At the time, the hospital depended on an unreliable water supply. According to district authorities, water was available for only about three days each week. With just 18,000 litres of storage capacity, the hospital often struggled to meet the needs of its 168 beds, 45 health workers, hundreds of patients and caregivers, and an average of 205 deliveries each month. “We needed water constantly to wash our hands, clean wards, disinfect surfaces and maintain a safe environment for patients,” says Aloysia. “Whenever the supply was interrupted, many routine activities became more difficult. During the outbreak, that was a serious concern.” The hospital had toilets, handwashing stations and other sanitation facilities in place, but without a reliable water supply, they could not always be used as intended. For health workers like Aloysia, the shortages made even the most basic infection prevention and control measures more challenging at a time when they were needed most. Kiki & Miles Productions A 50,000-litre water tank funded by the Republic of Korea through UNICEF strengthens reliable water access at Biharamulo District Hospital. Kiki & Miles Productions A pump house for the Republic of Korea- and UNICEF-supported borehole system at Biharamulo Hospital. To address this challenge and strengthen the hospital's resilience, UNICEF, with funding from the Republic of Korea, partnered with the Government of Tanzania and its technical partner, Sanitation and Water Action (SAWA), to improve water, sanitation, and hygiene services at the facility. The support included drilling a 105-metre borehole, installing a high-capacity pump, constructing a 50,000-litre elevated water storage tank, laying 738 metres of water distribution pipelines, and installing handwashing stations in key service areas, including the hospital's main entrances. Today, reliable water is available throughout the hospital, strengthening infection prevention and control, environmental cleaning, and patient care. "Now when I come to work, I know water will be available when we need it," says Aloysia. "That gives us confidence. We can maintain hygiene standards consistently and focus on caring for patients." "You only walk a few steps and find water," she says. "When water is there, you have hope." We are much better prepared than before.” At Biharamulo District Hospital, turning on a tap is no longer a question of chance. It is a reminder that one of healthcare's simplest necessities can also be one of its strongest defences. By investing in reliable water, sanitation and hygiene services, the Government of Tanzania, with support from UNICEF and the Republic of Korea, has helped strengthen safer care today while building a more resilient health system for the emergencies of tomorrow.
