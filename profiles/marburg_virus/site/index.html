Question and background
Marburg virus (MARV) is an extremely virulent pathogen for which effective preventive and therapeutic strategies remain limited, highlighting the urgent demand for a safe and efficient vaccine.
Design and population
An in silico study was conducted to design a novel multi-epitope subunit vaccine candidate against Marburg virus, focusing on immunoinformatics-based approaches to predict and assemble epitopes.
Core methods
The vaccine construct was designed using GGGS, GPGPG, and KK linkers to assemble cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL), helper T lymphocyte (HTL), and B-cell epitopes, respectively. It was further enhanced with the 50S ribosomal protein L7/L12 as an adjuvant and a PADRE sequence for broad CD4+ T-cell activation.
Main results
Molecular docking studies revealed strong and stable interactions between the vaccine and immune receptors, as reflected by a favourable HDOCK score (− 315.21) and a high confidence value (0.9646). Normal mode analysis via iMODS demonstrated appropriate molecular flexibility, indicated by a low eigen value (3.61×10–6). Immune simulations revealed strong humoral and cellular immune responses, marked by increased levels of IgG and IgM, cytokine secretion (IFN-γ, IL-2, TNF-α), and the development of memory cells.
Interpretation and novelty
The study interprets the in silico results as promising for the development of a multi-epitope vaccine candidate against Marburg virus, with novel insights into its immunogenic potential and structural stability.
Scientific and public-health significance
The findings provide a foundation for further experimental validation of the vaccine's immunogenic effectiveness and protective capability, which could inform future vaccine development and public health strategies against Marburg virus.
Limitations and evidence strength
The study is limited to in silico analysis, which requires experimental validation to confirm immunogenic effectiveness and protective capability. The evidence strength is moderate due to the reliance on computational models without empirical data.

Original abstract (English)
This work models the infection dynamics of the Marburg Virus Disease using the SEIQR model. It is a five-compartment model. We investigated the non-negativity of solutions, invariant region and boundedness, equilibrium points, basic reproduction number, sensitivity, equilibrium points’ stability, and bifurcation of the model. For all time and positive initial conditions, we observed that the model has non-negative solutions. The invariant region of the model is established, revealing the population size is bounded. The sensitivity analysis shows that the contact rate, the probability of effective contact and the rate at which exposed individuals become infectious have positive sensitivity indices, while the quarantine rates for the exposed and infectious classes, the death rates, and the recovery rates for the exposed and infectious classes have negative sensitivity indices. We established that the non-negativity of the residual vector , resulting from the difference between the rate of change of infected individuals and the Metzler matrix, stops the existence of several endemic equilibria for . Thus, no critical value exists, and there is an extension of local stability of the disease-free equilibrium to global stability on the entire domain. The bifurcation analysis reveals a forward bifurcation and the bifurcation plot confirms that for effective management and eradication the disease, the basic reproduction number must satisfy , where is a critical value. The mathematical analysis also reveals that the disease-free equilibrium is globally asymptotically stable. The effects of some sensitive parameters on the infectious class are shown via numerical simulations.

Pseudovirus Nanoparticles Displaying the Receptor-Binding Glycoprotein of Marburg Virus as Potential Vaccine Candidates

Authors: Ming Xia, Frank S. Vago, Ming Tan
Original abstract (English)
Background: The lack of a licensed vaccine against the deadly Marburg virus (MARV) calls for the development of a novel and effective vaccine strategy. Methods: We report here our development of three pseudovirus nanoparticles (PVNPs), each displaying a MARV-neutralizing glycoprotein (GP) segment of different lengths, as MARV vaccine candidates. These PVNPs, designated S-GP278, S-GP377, and S-GP486, were generated using our proprietary norovirus shell nanoparticle (SNP) platform. The PVNPs were evaluated for their production, self-assembly, morphology, structural properties, and immunogenicity. Results: Our data demonstrated that the three recombinant S-GP proteins, S-GP278, S-GP377, and S-GP486, can be produced at low cost using an E. coli expression system. The purified recombinant S-GP proteins self-assembled into PVNPs with high efficacy. Structural modeling of the S-GP1 PVNPs highlighted their key architectural features, consisting of an icosahedral inner shell and multiple trimeric GP structures displayed on the surface. Immunization of mice with the S-GP PVNPs elicited high titers of GP-specific antibodies, including IgG and IgA. Original abstract (English)
Rwanda’s health security can only be strengthened through integrated One Health approaches that link human, animal, and environmental health. Emerging health threats such as the Marburg Virus highlight the urgency, yet fragmented systems, weak coordination, and behavioral barriers continue to constrain resilience. Thus, we assessed behavioral, social, and systemic determinants influencing community resilience, outbreak preparedness, and response, using the Health Belief Model (HBM) and the Social Ecological Model (SEM) to generate actionable evidence for strengthening One Health integration, health system performance, and risk communication strategies for effective disease prevention and outbreak mitigation in Rwanda. We had six In-depth Interviews (IDIs) per district and two Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) per district. Participants were selected from different socioeconomic class, occupation selected rotated across districts, ensuring inclusivity, while data collection was carried out in October 2025. We conducted weekly debriefing meetings, which enabled us to familiarize ourselves with the data. Using Dedoose for data coding, results were presented according to themes, while indicating areas of convergence. We received ethical approval for this study from the Rwanda National Ethics Committee (IRB00001497 of IORG0001100). These diseases are Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever (CCHF), Lassa fever and Marburg virus disease (MVD). The Communicable Diseases Agency (CDA) said it has been monitoring them for some time “as all three diseases are viral haemorrhagic fevers that can cause severe illness and have the potential for human-to-human transmission”. The diseases could have fatality rates of up to 50%, according to the World Health Organization (WHO). They will be added to the Infectious Diseases Act’s (IDA) list of infectious diseases, following the amendment of IDA and its related regulations in September by the Ministry of Health (MOH). This means that all medical practitioners such as doctors, as well as clinical laboratories, are legally required to notify the authorities of all confirmed as well as suspected cases. Although the risk posed by these diseases to Singapore remains low, the latest moves will ensure the nation is prepared to respond when necessary, said a CDA spokeswoman in response to queries from The Straits Times. The notification of cases will support early detection and a timely public health response, which could include measures such as isolation of cases and activation of contact tracing, she added. The WHO website states that there is currently no specific treatment or vaccine approved for any of the diseases, though there is ongoing vaccine development for Lassa fever and MVD. Diseases with high fatality rates The WHO information showed that fatality rates of the three diseases could go as high as 50%. CCHF can cause severe viral haemorrhagic fever outbreaks, with a fatality rate of up to 40%. It is transmitted primarily through tick bites, contact with infected livestock, as well as close contact with the blood, secretions or other bodily fluids of infected people. There is sudden onset of symptoms, such as fever, muscle ache, dizziness, sore eyes and photophobia (sensitivity to light), as well as sharp mood swings and confusion. Severely ill patients may experience rapid kidney deterioration, sudden liver failure or pulmonary failure. Lassa fever has an overall fatality rate of 1%, rising to at least 15% for those hospitalised with severe illness. It is more concerning for pregnant women as foetal death rates can go beyond 80% in the third trimester and maternal death rates can exceed 30%. The virus is mainly transmitted through food or items contaminated with rodent urine or faeces, and possibly human to human. About 80% of those infected have no or mild symptoms such as sore throat, muscle pain, nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea. Severe cases may suffer from facial swelling, fluid in the lung cavity, bleeding from the mouth, nose, vagina or gastrointestinal tract, and low blood pressure. Even after recovery, up to 25% of patients reported sudden hearing loss, and most of them never regained their hearing. UK Commits £33.2 Million to Eight Vaccine Projects Targeting Ebola, Marburg, Plague and Other Epidemic Threats - Global Biodefense
新闻简报The UK government has committed £33.2 million to eight vaccine projects targeting Marburg virus and other epidemic threats, as announced by the Department of Health and Social Care, the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA), and Innovate UK on October 8, 2026. The funding aims to accelerate vaccine development and deployment for diseases with epidemic potential, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. This initiative is part of the UK Vaccine Network, which addresses market failures by supporting research, clinical trials, and technologies for emerging pathogens. The UK has previously invested £134 million in its first phase between 2016 and 2022, with further funding planned for 2023 to 2029. The program also includes efforts to develop vaccines for other diseases such as Ebola, plague, Lassa fever, Nipah virus, chikungunya, and Mayaro virus. Richard Pebody, UKHSA’s Director of Epidemic and Emerging Infections, emphasized the importance of preparedness, citing the ongoing Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and a suspected pneumonic plague case in Russia as reminders of persistent infectious disease risks. The funding will not support pathogens with existing licensed vaccines or advanced candidates, nor SARS-CoV-2 or influenza.

Close Menu Facebook X (Twitter) Instagram Facebook Instagram Threads Subscribe Featured Ebola Outbreak Avian Influenza Measles Resurgence Vaccine News Events Advertisement Medical Countermeasures UK Commits £33.2 Million to Eight Vaccine Projects Targeting Ebola, Marburg, Plague and Other Epidemic Threats Funding through the UK Vaccine Network aims to speed development and deployment of vaccines for diseases with epidemic potential, with a focus on low- and middle-income countries By Global Biodefense StaffOctober 18, 2026 Background of compilation image credit: Shutterstock, modified Share Facebook LinkedIn Reddit Email The UK government has announced £33.2 million to fund eight vaccine projects against some of the world’s deadliest infectious diseases. The awards, announced October 8, 2026 by the Department of Health and Social Care, the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) and Innovate UK, target Ebola, plague, Marburg virus, Lassa fever, Nipah virus, chikungunya and Mayaro virus. The money will advance promising vaccine candidates, clinical trials and technologies intended to make vaccine design and manufacture faster. It will also build the research, clinical trial and technical capacity needed to develop, test and deploy vaccines quickly when a new threat emerges, and to make those vaccines affordable and accessible in the countries at greatest risk. Richard Pebody, UKHSA’s Director of Epidemic and Emerging Infections, cited the ongoing Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo as a reminder that emerging diseases “are not a distant threat,” and said effective vaccines and the manufacturing capability to produce them at speed are essential alongside detection and response. The announcement also referenced a suspected pneumonic plague case linked to a laboratory worker’s death in Russia’s Irkutsk region, with nearly 200 contacts placed under precautionary medical observation, as another sign that serious infectious disease risks persist. The awards are delivered through the UK Vaccine Network, an expert advisory group which brings together industry, academia and funders to guide government investment. Its premise is a market failure: outbreaks of some of the deadliest diseases occur intermittently and often in poor countries, so there is little commercial incentive for industry to develop vaccines against them. The UK committed £134 million to its first phase between 2016 and 2022, with further investment committed for 2023 to 2029. Government officials note the programme previously supported development of the Oxford/AstraZeneca COVID-19 vaccine. The network maintains a priority pathogen list covering 12 families of viruses and bacteria, including Lassa fever virus, MERS, Marburg and Sudan ebolavirus, Nipah virus, Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever, Rift Valley fever, chikungunya, Q fever and plague. It will not fund work on pathogens that already have licensed vaccines or advanced candidates, nor on SARS-CoV-2 or influenza. It also supports vaccine technology development suited to responding to novel pathogens, the so-called “disease X” scenario. Health Minister Chris McDonald said investing before a crisis hits helps protect the public and ensure the UK is ready to respond to future threats. Whitty said the aim is to prevent some of the most dangerous infectious diseases in the world today.
