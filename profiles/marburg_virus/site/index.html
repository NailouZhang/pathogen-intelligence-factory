Original abstract (English)
In November 2025, Ethiopia reported its first outbreak of Marburg virus disease, with a case fatality rate of 64%. Here we report the analysis of seven near-complete Marburg virus genomes from seven early cases. The outbreak lineage belonged to the MARV.A.1 clade, which is closely related to genomes from historical human cases and bats. A high nucleotide identity across the seven genomes suggests human-to-human transmission after a zoonotic spillover event. These findings highlight the importance of genomic surveillance for outbreak detection, response and preparedness. Authors: André Filipe Pastor, Shruti Shirsathe, Santiago Vidal-Freire, Natalia M. Ferressini-Gerpe, Jonathan C. Guito, Rachel Somavarapu, Juan Angel Patiño-Galindo, Elaina Giannetti, Maya Tagliavia, Heng Pan
Original abstract (English)
Bats tolerate viruses that cause severe disease in humans. Egyptian rousette bats, the natural reservoirs of Marburg virus (MARV), sustain asymptomatic infection, whereas humans develop severe and fatal disease. To define the basis of this divergence, we establish a comparative framework using primary and immortalized Egyptian rousette and human cells exposed to double-stranded RNA and interferon (IFN). Bat cells upregulate antiviral effectors while limiting inflammatory amplifiers, a pattern further supported by analysis of independent in vivo MARV infection datasets. In contrast, human cells activate antiviral programs and immune hyperactivation in response to MARV. Integrating transcriptomic and proteomic analyses, we identify species-specific immune programs encompassing differential IFN-stimulated gene regulation, apoptotic control, and cytokine signaling. Our findings establish an integrative framework for understanding species-specific antiviral responses associated with MARV reservoir biology, identify pathways linked to immunopathogenesis in humans, and provide a foundation for future studies of host responses to filovirus infection.

Quantifying the extinction probability of Marburg virus disease outbreaks: A continuous-time Markov chain model integrating a multi-type branching process

Authors: Ester Damiano, Jacob Ismael Irunde, Maranya Makuru Mayengo, Ester Damiano Damiano, Jacob I. Irunde
Original abstract (English)
Understanding the extinction dynamics of Marburg virus disease is crucial, particularly during the early stages of an outbreak when stochastic effects strongly influence disease outcomes. Although deterministic models have provided valuable insights into virus transmission dynamics, they cannot adequately capture random transmission events or quantify extinction probabilities, particularly when outbreaks begin with few infected cases. This study develops a continuous-time Markov chain stochastic model derived from a deterministic multi-host transmission model. The model incorporates a multi-type branching process, in which distinct infectious types are tracked according to their contributions to secondary infections, enabling estimation of disease extinction probabilities during the early outbreak phase. The extinction analysis shows that outbreaks initiated by exposed humans have a higher probability of extinction (21.6%) compared with those initiated by infectious humans (13.7%), whereas outbreaks initiated by infected bats have an extinction probability of only 0.36%, highlighting their role as important reservoirs sustaining virus transmission. Furthermore, the results demonstrate that reducing human-to-human transmission, decreasing viral shedding, and increasing environmental viral decay significantly enhance the likelihood of disease extinction. The proposed framework provides a quantitative tool for assessing Marburg virus disease outbreak risk and valuable insights for designing early interventions for human transmission, environmental contamination, and cross-species interactions.

Authors: Marcus Ifeanyi Ossaiugbo, Innocent Adinebo Katule, Mgbomene Chukunalu, Chukunalu Mgbomene
Original abstract (English)
Marburg virus disease (MVD) is a zoonotic viral haemorrhagic fever that presents continuing challenges for outbreak prevention and control. This study develops a deterministic compartmental model to examine transmission among humans, bats and monkeys, together with the contribution of environmental viral contamination. The human population is represented by susceptible, exposed, infectious, quarantined and recovered compartments, while susceptible and infected classes are considered for the animal hosts. The model incorporates direct and indirect transmission, quarantine, recovery, loss of immunity, and environmental viral shedding and decay. Non-negativity, boundedness, equilibria and reproduction-number thresholds are analysed. Latin hypercube sampling and sensitivity indices are used to examine parameter effects, while an optimal-control formulation evaluates quarantine and environmental interventions alongside behavioural responses. Under the assumed baseline parameters, model simulations indicate that environment-to-human exposure accounts for at least 99.3% of the human force of infection over the 50-day period. Environmental controls alone reduce the simulated infectious peak from 6.03 to 1.15 and the cumulative infectious burden by 85%. The combined intervention scenario produces the lowest reported objective value, although its infection trajectory is similar to that of environmental control alone. Increasing quarantine rates reduces simulated infectious peaks, whereas social distancing has limited influence in the environmentally dominated baseline scenario. These results underscore the importance of assessing multiple transmission routes when selecting interventions, while recognising that the findings depend on model structure and assumed parameter values.

The effectiveness of risk communication and community engagement (RCCE) depends on the population’s baseline knowledge, perceptions, and the sources of information. Therefore, this study aimed to assess community knowledge, perceptions, and associated factors of Marburg virus disease in the South Ethiopia Region. Quantitative data were collected using interviewer-administered questionnaires with the Kobo Toolbox data collection software. For qualitative data, focus group discussions were conducted. Health education delivered in multiple channels contributed to increased knowledge, whereas official recognition corrected early misconceptions and changed attitudes. Targeted interventions that focus on lower educational status and wealth, advanced age, and sources of information are essential. Strengthening risk communication and community engagement via trusted platforms is crucial. Strategies that utilize social media for information dissemination, empower housewives, and build positive perceptions are highly recommended.

Original source text (English)
Abstract In November 2025, Ethiopia reported its first outbreak of Marburg virus disease, with a case fatality rate of 64%. Here we report the analysis of seven near-complete Marburg virus genomes from seven early cases. The outbreak lineage belonged to the MARV.A.1 clade, which is closely related to genomes from historical human cases and bats. A high nucleotide identity across the seven genomes suggests human-to-human transmission after a zoonotic spillover event. These findings highlight the importance of genomic surveillance for outbreak detection, response and preparedness. Similar content being viewed by others Main Marburg virus disease (MVD) is a severe and highly lethal zoonotic infection with case fatality rates ranging from 23% to 90%1,2. It is caused by the orthomarburg viruses (Orthomarburgvirus marburgense), Marburg virus (MARV) and Ravn virus (RAVV), which belong to the Filoviridae family and are closely related to ebolaviruses, which share similar clinical features and transmission routes. Human infection typically follows zoonotic exposure in caves or mines that are inhabited by the fruit bat Rousettus aegyptiacus, with subsequent spread through direct contact with contaminated bodily fluids. The MARV genome is a single-stranded negative-sense RNA of approximately 19 kb encoding seven proteins, including nucleoprotein (NP), viral protein (VP)35, VP40, VP30, VP24, glycoprotein (GP) and the large polymerase protein (L). Since 2008, MARV has caused most documented MVD outbreaks globally and has been detected in all countries where MVD occurs, whereas RAVV has only been reported in Uganda, Kenya and the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC)1. As of March 2025, 608 confirmed cases and 453 deaths have been recorded across 19 outbreaks, predominantly in Africa3. The largest outbreak occurred in Angola in 2004–2005, resulting in 227 deaths among 252 cases. Uganda has experienced multiple outbreaks, often linked to miners exposed to bat colonies, while Kenya and the DRC reported outbreaks in the 1980s and early 2000s. More recent outbreaks in Guinea (2021), Ghana (2022), Equatorial Guinea (2023), Tanzania (2023 and 2025) and Rwanda (2024) highlight MARV’s expanding geographic footprint across East and West Africa2,3. Genomic analyses consistently show limited within-outbreak diversity, with most outbreaks arising from single zoonotic spillover events followed by human-to-human transmission1,2. Recent outbreaks in Rwanda and Tanzania involved lineages closely related to Ugandan bat-derived clades, suggesting long-term viral persistence in bat reservoirs with periodic spillover4,5. The first recorded MARV outbreak in Ethiopia was detected on 14 November 2025 following a cluster of haemorrhagic fever cases in Jinka, Ari Zone, South Ethiopia Regional State (the town, administrative zone and region, respectively)6 (Fig. 1a). Within 48 h, MVD was confirmed in suspected cases by reverse transcription PCR (RT–PCR) at the Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI), with subsequent rapid implementation of a comprehensive emergency response by the government, which included contact tracing, isolation, expanded diagnostic capacity, community engagement and the deployment of antiviral drugs and vaccines. By the end of the outbreak in December 2025, 4,006 individuals were investigated, and 155 suspected cases were identified. Overall, 14 were confirmed, 9 of whom had died (case fatality rate: 64%). Three confirmed cases originating from Jinka were later detected in Hawassa, Dasenech and Malle, all with documented epidemiological links to the initial cluster. Most patients were young male adults (8 out 14 male) (Extended Data Table 1). Genomic characterization was undertaken to investigate viral evolution and transmission dynamics. Out of 14 RT–PCR-confirmed specimens, 7 samples had sufficient material and underwent whole-genome sequencing. Seven near-complete genomes were generated with ≥30× coverage that enabled confident consensus calling for >90% of the genome, applying a majority consensus rule to a minimum of ten unique reads (Extended Data Table 2). The GC content ranged from 40.2% to 41.0%. To contextualize the Ethiopian outbreak, the seven genomes were analysed along with all publicly available complete orthomarburgvirus sequences from GenBank (n = 103). Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic analysis placed all Ethiopian sequences within a single monophyletic lineage nested in the MARV.A.1 clade (Fig. 1b). To investigate the temporal origins of the 2025 Ethiopian outbreak lineage, we performed a global molecular clock analysis using the Bayesian Evolutionary Analysis Sampling Trees (BEAST). Bayesian phylogenetic analyses converged successfully, with all estimated parameters achieving effective sample size (ESS) values above the recommended threshold of 200 (Supplementary Table 1), indicating that the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) adequately sampled the parameter space. The global MARV phylogeny exhibited a detectable temporal signal (R2 = 0.56, 1.75 × 10−4 substitutions per site per year, P < 0.001) (Extended Data Fig. 1). The Ethiopian lineage was most closely related to the human MARV isolate JN408064 (‘Leiden’ strain), isolated from a Dutch patient infected in 2008 after visiting Python Cave in Uganda7 (Fig. 1c). The estimated time to the most recent common ancestor (tMRCA) of the Ethiopian lineage and JN408064 was approximately September 1994 (95% highest posterior density (HPD) January 1993–April 1996). The lineage also clustered near the MARV sequences from the DRC (1999) and Uganda (2012)8,9. The tMRCA of the Ethiopian lineage and its closest bat-derived isolates (JX458853 and JX458858) was approximately September 1990 (95% HPD April 1987–February 1994). These bat isolates were sampled in 2008–2009 from Python Cave8. The JN408064 strain itself is closely related to the EF446132 strain responsible for the initial Marburg outbreak (1967), which also originated in Uganda10. Overall, the Ethiopian lineage exhibited extremely limited diversity, with 0–6 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) between genomes (Extended Data Table 3). A total of 148 lineage-defining substitutions were identified, including 110 within coding DNA sequence (CDS) regions (28 non-synonymous and 82 synonymous) and 38 in intergenic regions (Fig. 2, Extended Data Table 4 and Supplementary Table 2). Most genes exhibited low non-synonymous to synonymous substitution rate ratios (dN/dS, ω) across the viral genome (Extended Data Table 5). The GP gene was the major hotspot of divergence, containing 13 fixed non-synonymous substitutions. These included Q205K in the receptor-binding domain and 11 substitutions in or near the mucin-like domain (K222N, D250N, A284T, S291P, P295L, N310D, G317D, H336R, T343I, K465E and S485P). The N501H substitution was identified in the membrane fusion region. VP35 contained a single non-synonymous substitution (S193A). The L gene contained nine substitutions (A115V, I359V, I1643T, Q1787R, P1796S, D1806N, R2091K, K2156Q and V2278A), representing the second-highest level of non-synonymous substitutions. NP and VP genes showed minimal diversity, reflecting strong functional constraint. Global MARV genomes exhibit similar patterns with low diversity in NP and VP regions and higher diversity in GP and L genes (Extended Data Fig. 2). Epidemiological investigations support that the Ethiopian outbreak originated from a single zoonotic spillover event. The earliest cases most likely contracted the infection from an index patient, who probably died from viral haemorrhagic fever in October 2025. Retrospective outbreak investigations, including the sampling of bats and fruit bats in surrounding areas, suggested that the index case had prior exposure to these animals. Between 12 and 24 November, MARV spread among close contacts of the early cases. The findings reported here represent only a fraction of the likely transmission chain, as several suspected MVD cases died before PCR confirmation, and only seven complete genomes were recovered. Indeed, this limited genomic dataset restricts our ability to robustly determine whether the genomic transmission patterns fully support the observed epidemiological links. The Ethiopian lineage’s high conservation mirrors patterns observed in recent Rwanda and Tanzania outbreaks4,5. By contrast, the 1998–2000 DRC outbreak involved multiple independent spillovers and distinct co-circulating clades11. The close relationship between the Ethiopian genomes and historical MARV.A.1 sequences suggests long-term persistence of this clade in East and Central Africa, probably maintained in bat reservoirs with occasional spillover2,8. Given the capacity of Rousettus aegyptiacus for long-distance migration, these bats may facilitate the spread of MARV across regions, including the potential establishment of endemicity in southwestern Ethiopia. The estimated ~30-year divergence of the Ethiopian lineage from its closest relatives suggests either recent introduction from neighbouring countries or cryptic circulation within Ethiopian bat populations over decades. Distinguishing between these scenarios requires expanded longitudinal surveillance. Filovirus persistence in survivors is a recognized concern, and its RNA has been detected in immune-privileged sites, including the testes12,13. MARV persistence has been reported in a Kenyan survivor 2 months post-recovery13. Although sexual transmission has not been confirmed, persistence may contribute to sporadic re-emergence. Nonetheless, most MARV outbreaks arise from new zoonotic spillovers associated with human–animal interface activities1,2. The Ethiopian outbreak exhibited only 28 non-synonymous SNPs, reflecting a highly conserved genome. All genomes showed a dN/dS < 1, consistent with strong functional constraint14. Although purifying selection is more readily detectable in ebolaviruses owing to deeper sampling and longer transmission chains, MARV’s limited diversity and sparse genomic data reduce power to observe similar patterns15. The slower evolutionary rate observed in the Ethiopian outbreak suggests that these isolates may represent stable targets for therapeutics and vaccines. Indeed, these genomic insights informed the implementation of investigational interventions, including remdesivir and Sabin’s cAd3-MARV vaccine, which was administered to patients and healthcare professionals as both therapeutic and prophylactic treatments during the outbreak period. Genomic characterization of MARV in Ethiopia reveals a lineage closely related to East African clades and probably originating from a single zoonotic spillover event. Genomic epidemiology provided actionable insights into evolutionary stability, potential sources of infection and intervention strategies. However, the findings should be interpreted with caution. The broad and partially overlapping HPD intervals observed throughout the time-scaled phylogenetic analysis might reflect several inherent limitations. These include the small sample size, short outbreak duration (~6 weeks), sparse global representation of MARV.A.1 sequences and low sequence divergence. Strengthening regional sequencing capacity and integrating genomic surveillance within a One Health framework will be essential for timely detection and containment of filovirus and other outbreaks. The Ethiopian event underscores MARV’s ability to emerge in previously unrecognized ecological niches and reinforces the need for sustained preparedness against emerging pathogens. A narrative review published in the journal Discover Viruses on 9 August 2026 synthesises what is currently known about the disease’s epidemiology, virology, pathogenesis, diagnostics and the investigational countermeasures now being evaluated in clinics and laboratories. The review, led by Amit Bansal and colleagues, draws on peer-reviewed literature published up to 13 February 2026, searched in MEDLINE and Embase, and deliberately excludes preprints and unpublished data. Marburg virus disease is a severe and frequently fatal zoonotic haemorrhagic fever caused by two genetically distinct viruses, Marburg virus and Ravn virus, both members of the Filoviridae family and the genus Orthomarburgvirus. Genomic analyses show the two viruses differ by up to 21.3 percent in nucleotide sequence, yet they cause clinically indistinguishable illness. Case fatality rates vary widely, from 24 to 88 percent, reflecting ascertainment bias, outbreak size, access to supportive care and the completeness of surveillance. Globally, more than 600 cases have been recorded since the virus was first identified in August 1967, when laboratory workers in Marburg and Frankfurt in Germany fell ill after handling African green monkeys, with an average case fatality rate of around 50 percent. The review’s authors caution that the case fatality rate is difficult to interpret as a measure of care quality: in a small outbreak where the only detected case is discovered because the patient died, the rate would be 100 percent, a statistical artifact of limited sample size, delayed detection and reporting bias. In larger outbreaks, the rate often falls as a broader spectrum of disease severity is captured. Notable outbreaks include the Democratic Republic of the Congo from 1998 to 2000, Angola in 2005, Equatorial Guinea and Tanzania in 2023, Rwanda in 2024, and Tanzania and Ethiopia in 2025. The 2004–2005 outbreak in Uíge, Angola, disproportionately affected young children, and the intensive care those children required likely catalysed nosocomial and community amplification. Many filovirus outbreaks have been characterised by truncated transmission chains with limited onward propagation, which contrasts sharply with the sustained, widespread community transmission seen during the 2013–2016 West African Ebola epidemic. That difference matters for research: the prolonged Ebola footprint was what made it possible to design and implement robust clinical trials of therapeutics and vaccines, an opportunity Marburg has rarely afforded. Discover more Check Symptoms Study Biology Read Med Journals Track Outbreaks At the molecular level, Marburg virus is a filamentous, enveloped, single-stranded negative-sense RNA virus whose main structural proteins are the glycoprotein, the large polymerase, VP24, VP30, VP35, VP40 and the nucleoprotein. The replication cycle begins when the glycoprotein mediates attachment to host cell receptors, with entry reported to involve macropinocytosis, although clathrin-mediated or alternative pathways may dominate depending on cell type. Once inside endosomes, the GP1 subunit is cleaved by endosomal proteases, an essential step before binding to the Niemann-Pick C1 cholesterol transporter, the principal entry receptor. The GP2 subunit then drives pH-dependent fusion between viral and endosomal membranes, releasing the nucleocapsid into the cytosol, where the genome is transcribed and replicated before new virions bud from the plasma membrane, often at filopodia, in a process driven primarily by VP40. The virus also dismantles the early immune response. VP35 and VP40 likely suppress type I interferon production or signalling, while VP24, unlike its Ebola counterpart, does not block STAT1 nuclear import and is instead associated with modulating Keap1-dependent stress pathways. Infected dendritic cells may fail to mature properly, impairing antigen presentation and cytokine production, and early lymphocyte depletion together with pro-inflammatory cytokine release can potentially contribute to cytokine storm and septic shock. Transmission from animals to humans is suspected to occur through Egyptian fruit bats as the reservoir, with monkeys serving as spillover hosts in laboratory-associated outbreaks, and the virus spreading via the saliva, urine and faeces of infected animals. Once people are infected, they transmit the virus through body fluids including saliva, blood, semen and breast milk, and through contaminated objects such as clothes, bedding and needles. Viremia increases throughout the course of disease, leading to increased shedding, and spread is often associated with caring for the sick and with burial preparations. By day 3 after symptom onset, patients frequently develop profuse watery diarrhoea, abdominal pain, cramping, nausea and vomiting, and a non-pruritic maculopapular rash, most prominent on the trunk, may appear between days 2 and 7. From around day 5, haemorrhagic manifestations may develop, though not in all patients, including haematemesis, melaena, epistaxis, gingival and vaginal bleeding, and haemorrhage at venepuncture sites. Neurological involvement can include confusion, irritability, aggression, delirium, encephalitis and seizures. Marked biochemical alterations accompany the disease, including elevated liver enzymes, increased creatinine and profound lymphocyte depletion, and later stages may lead to multi-organ failure and death. Viral persistence in immune-privileged sites such as the testes and eyes can lead to orchitis and uveitis during convalescence, sexual transmission is possible months after infection, and recent longitudinal studies have identified persistent cardiometabolic and haematological alterations, such as elevated blood pressure, reticulocytosis and biochemical markers of hepatic and renal stress, even years after recovery. Diagnosis relies on laboratory confirmation because early symptoms are non-specific. RT-PCR is considered the gold standard for detecting Marburg virus RNA, typically targeting conserved genes such as NP, L and GP, with reported analytical sensitivities as low as 5 copies per reaction or 8.6 to 16 RNA copies per assay. ELISA targeting NP, VP40 and GP detects filovirus-specific IgM, visible as early as 2 to 4 days after symptom onset, and IgG, detectable from 8 to 10 days after onset and persisting for up to 2 years. Virus isolation requires a biosafety level 4 laboratory and is not used routinely. The review notes that diagnostic modalities remain limited in accessibility and scalability, particularly in resource-constrained settings, and that no WHO-prequalified and validated Marburg-specific antigen rapid diagnostic test exists for routine clinical deployment. With no approved vaccines or specific antiviral treatments, early intensive supportive care remains the cornerstone of management and was shown to significantly improve patient survival. Intravenous fluid resuscitation, electrolyte correction, analgesia, antipyretics, nutritional support and psychosocial care form the standard package, and follow-up of contacts for early detection, isolation, testing and rapid access to supportive care is key to outbreak management. On the therapeutic front, the monoclonal antibody MBP091, isolated from a Marburg survivor four years after recovery, binds the viral glycoprotein and prevents its interaction with the NPC1 receptor, and a Phase I trial showed it was well tolerated in healthy adult volunteers. Remdesivir, a nucleotide analogue prodrug that inhibits the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, showed promise in preclinical filovirus models, but human data specific to Marburg are limited, and a randomised controlled trial in the Democratic Republic of the Congo did not demonstrate a survival benefit for remdesivir in Ebola when compared against the monoclonal antibody ZMapp. The WHO-coordinated SOLIDARITY PARTNERS trial is now assessing these therapies for filoviral disease, randomising participants to receive MBP091 or no adjunctive therapy, and a 10-day course of remdesivir or no adjunctive therapy, with a host-directed intervention arm currently inactive. During Rwanda’s 2024 outbreak, frontline healthcare workers were offered both agents under expanded access and clinical trial protocols: remdesivir was administered intravenously to 52 patients, including all who were critically ill, and MBP091 was given to 10 patients, six under expanded access and four enrolled in the WHO-coordinated randomised trial. Early clinical observations indicated that treated patients showed signs of improvement, but the authors stress that because of non-randomisation other factors may have contributed to the differences seen between treated and untreated patients. Rwanda also marked the first time post-exposure prophylaxis was administered to a large number of contacts during a filovirus outbreak, with remdesivir given to 150 contacts; no outcome data have been reported for these individuals, and the lack of randomisation warrants caution in interpreting any effect. In preclinical work, the oral prodrug obeldesivir, given for 10 days starting one day after infection, did not prevent disease from developing but showed 80 percent protection against a lethal Marburg challenge in non-human primates, and its efficacy when given later than one day post-infection still needs study. On the vaccine front, no candidate has yet received regulatory approval, and the WHO Technical Advisory Group has prioritised four viral-vectored candidates encoding the Marburg surface glycoprotein, two based on vesicular stomatitis virus and two on non-replicating chimpanzee adenoviruses. An adenovirus-vectored vaccine developed by the Sabin Vaccine Institute has advanced furthest, demonstrating immunogenicity and safety in human trials, and a Phase I trial in the United States reported binding antibody responses in 95 percent of 40 healthy adult participants at one month, with 70 percent retaining elevated titres at one year. Phase II trials are underway in Uganda, Kenya and the United States, and Oxford University launched a first-in-human trial of its ChAdOx1 Marburg vaccine, which was given to 1,710 frontline workers and high-risk contacts during the 2024 Rwanda outbreak under emergency use authorisation in a phase 2 trial. DNA vaccines required three doses for peak antibody titres, with only 11 percent retaining antibodies at six months, while mRNA vaccines encoding the Marburg glycoprotein have shown promising results in animal models, including complete protection at low doses under specific experimental conditions, though human trials have not yet commenced. The review’s authors conclude that given the sporadic nature of outbreaks, targeted post-exposure measures may be more feasible than widespread pre-exposure immunisation, and that strengthening global preparedness will require integrated surveillance, rapid clinical trial frameworks and equitable access to vaccines and antivirals. Given the emergency situation, verbal consent was received from all patients included in the report, and all collected samples were anonymized to prevent the identification of any individual. The researchers were involved in all stages of the work, including study design, epidemiological investigation, community engagement, data collection, analysis and manuscript preparation. The samples were transported under standard operating procedures for infectious material to the EPHI in Addis Ababa. There, at the Integrated Pathogen Genomics and Bioinformatics Facility for Surveillance and Research, they were inactivated inside a fixed-film isolator using 100% ethanol at five times the sample volume. RNA was then extracted with a Qiagen kit inside a class III biosafety cabinet, following the manufacturer’s instructions, with lysing and purification buffers and a final elution step. The extracted RNA was screened by multiplex RT–PCR for dengue, Zika, Chikungunya, Yellow Fever, Ebola and Marburg viruses. Not all of these yielded usable sequence data: two genomes had less than 52% breadth of coverage and five samples were unavailable for processing, leaving seven samples that underwent whole-genome sequencing. Library preparation used the Illumina RNA Prep with Enrichment workflow with the Viral Surveillance Panel 2. In this workflow, RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA, enzymatically fragmented and amplified to add indexes and adaptor sequences. Library concentrations were measured with a Qubit 1× dsDNA High Sensitivity assay before enrichment, and target enrichment was performed by hybridization with capture probes from the Viral Surveillance Panel 2, followed by capture with Streptavidin Magnetic Beads 3, elution, amplification and purification with Illumina purification beads. The enriched libraries were normalized, pooled in equimolar concentrations and diluted to the appropriate loading concentration for sequencing. Two libraries were diluted with NovaSeq X series Pre-Load Buffer to a final concentration of 150 pM and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform using 2 × 150-bp paired-end reads. The remaining libraries were normalized to 10 pM and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform using 2 × 300-bp paired-end reads. All pooled libraries were denatured with freshly prepared 0.2 N sodium hydroxide before sequencing. These details matter because they document how a national public health laboratory, sequencing in-country, produced near-complete viral genomes during an active outbreak. The bioinformatic pipeline began with quality control and trimming of raw paired-end reads using fastp v0.23.4, removing low-quality bases (Phred below 20), adaptor sequences, poly-G/poly-X tails, low-complexity regions and reads shorter than 50 base pairs. Host-derived reads were removed using HOSTILE v2.0.2, which aligns reads against the human reference genome GRCh38.p13; only read pairs that did not align to the human genome were retained. Non-host reads were then mapped to the Marburg virus reference genome (accession NC_001608.3) using BWA-MEM v0.7.18 in short-read mode with default Illumina-optimized parameters. The reference is the complete genome of the Musoke strain, derived from the 1980 outbreak in Kenya, a 19,112-base-pair sequence first sequenced in 1992. BAM files were sorted and indexed with SAMtools v1.17, and coverage, alignment quality and duplication rates were assessed with Qualimap v2.2.2. Variants were called with iVar v1.3.1 combined with SAMtools mpileup, applying a minimum base quality of 20 and a variant frequency threshold of 0.03, generating per-sample consensus genomes with ambiguous positions coded as N. Consensus sequences were aligned with MAFFT v7.520 (L-INS-i). The final comparative dataset included 110 Marburg virus whole-genome sequences sampled between 1967 and 2025, spanning 13 countries: the DRC (n = 30), Rwanda (n = 18), Uganda (n = 17), Angola (n = 16), Ethiopia (n = 7), Sierra Leone (n = 6), Tanzania (n = 5), Kenya (n = 3), South Africa (n = 2), Ghana (n = 2), Guinea (n = 2), Germany (n = 1) and the Netherlands (n = 1). To assess whether the data carried a usable temporal signal, the team performed root-to-tip regression analysis with TempEst v1.5.3 on the maximum-likelihood phylogeny, optimizing the tree root to maximize the correlation between root-to-tip genetic distance and sampling date, using RAVV as a true outgroup that was pruned before the regression and restricting the analysis to global Marburg virus genomes, following a previously validated approach. Time-scaled phylogenies were inferred with BEAST v10.5.0 under the HKY nucleotide substitution model with empirical base frequencies, selected by Bayesian information criterion comparison in ModelFinder. A fixed local clock model was used: a fixed substitution rate of 1 × 10−3 substitutions per site per year was assumed for the defined outbreak clade, representing a plausible evolutionary rate for an RNA virus during the early stage of an outbreak, while the deeper evolutionary history was allowed to evolve under a lognormal relaxed clock prior centred on 5 × 10−4 substitutions per site per year, reflecting the lower rate expected outside the rapid outbreak phase. The demographic model was similarly two-phased. A coalescent Bayesian SkyGrid tree prior allowed effective population size to vary flexibly through time; the subtree from the Ethiopian lineage tMRCA to the present was modelled with an exponential growth coalescent prior, reflecting the rapid expansion expected during an outbreak, while the earlier, deeper history was modelled with a constant-population-size coalescent prior, assuming stable viral population dynamics before the emergence of the outbreak lineage. Two independent MCMC chains were run for 100 million states each, sampling every 10,000 steps, with the initial 10% discarded as burn-in. Convergence was evaluated visually in Tracer v1.7.2 and quantitatively through effective sample size calculations, with ESS values above 200 considered indicative of adequate mixing; all estimated parameters achieved ESS values above the recommended threshold. News Brief
IAVI, a global nonprofit biomedical research organization, has received an award from BARDA to transfer manufacturing of Marburg virus vaccine candidates. The vaccine, based on a recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus (rVSV) vector, has shown high protective efficacy in preclinical trials and is now in Phase 2 clinical trials. The WHO has prioritized this vaccine during the ongoing epidemic.
Original source text (English)
Affordable, accessible vaccines & antibodies We are a global nonprofit biomedical research organization that develops vaccines and antibodies for HIV, tuberculosis, and emerging infectious diseases. About us Our approach We take a full-circle approach to product development, from the grassroots level to the lab to legislative bodies. Mission To translate scientific discoveries into affordable, globally accessible public health solutions. Vision A world where all people have equitable access to innovative vaccines and therapeutics. Our health areas - HIV - Tuberculosis - Lassa virus - Marburg virus - Sudan virus - Bundibugyo virus HIV The challenge HIV/AIDS remains one of the deadliest infectious diseases, with 1.4 million people acquiring HIV in 2024. Given the complexities of HIV, traditional approaches to vaccine development have so far failed to result in a preventive vaccine. However, a vaccine is still needed to bring a true end to the HIV pandemic. Source: UNAIDS Our solution IAVI scientists and our collaborators are developing next-generation HIV vaccines to address the challenges of HIV vaccine design. Together, we have pioneered promising new vaccination strategies and the discovery of broadly neutralizing antibodies (bnAbs) against HIV. IAVI and our partners are developing HIV bnAbs to prevent HIV acquisition in infants. Tuberculosis The challenge Tuberculosis (TB) is the world’s leading infectious disease killer. In 2024, 10.7 million people fell ill with TB and 1.23 million people died of TB disease. The only available TB vaccine is the century-old Bacille Calmette-Guérin, or BCG. While this vaccine has efficacy in protecting against severe TB disease in infants and young children, it is largely ineffective in adolescents and adults, among whom most transmission and disease occurs. Source: WHO Our solution IAVI works across our global hubs with a diverse network of partners to advance promising TB vaccine candidates from discovery through clinical trials, and eventually, to post-licensure access. Our work extends to policy and advocacy initiatives that support TB vaccine development and access in regions where new vaccines are needed most. Lassa virus The challenge Lassa virus is an arenavirus that causes Lassa fever, an acute viral hemorrhagic illness endemic to West Africa that causes significant annual outbreaks of disease. Lassa fever is difficult to diagnose, and surveillance data is limited. Current estimates range from 300,000 to 500,000 cases and 5,000 related deaths each year. No licensed vaccines for Lassa fever exist. Source: WHO Our solution IAVI is developing a single-dose vaccine candidate for Lassa that is based on a recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus (rVSV) vector. This technology is similar to that underlying an approved Ebola Zaire virus vaccine. In 2024, IAVI’s Lassa vaccine candidate became the first Lassa vaccine to enter Phase 2 clinical trials, launched in three West African countries. This trial builds on a Phase 1 clinical trial that showed the candidate vaccine was well tolerated and immunogenic over a wide dose range. IAVI also conducts Lassa epidemiological studies, modeling, and community engagement to support future vaccine access. Marburg virus The challenge Marburg virus is a filovirus and the causative agent of Marburg virus disease (MVD), which has a case fatality rate of up to 88%. Marburg virus has the capacity to cause deadly outbreaks and is a potential bioterror threat. No vaccines or antiviral treatments are approved for MVD. A Marburg vaccine is urgently needed to respond to future outbreaks. Source: WHO Our solution IAVI is developing a vaccine candidate for Marburg that is based on a recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus (rVSV) vector. This technology is similar to that underlying an approved Ebola Zaire virus vaccine. Preclinical data demonstrates that IAVI’s Marburg vaccine candidate is highly protective in an animal model with one dose. Sudan virus The challenge Sudan virus (SUDV) is a filovirus and causes outbreaks of Ebola disease, most recently in 2025 in Uganda. Case fatality rates of Ebola disease caused by SUDV have varied from 41% to 100% in past outbreaks. The licensed vaccine for Ebola Zaire virus does not provide cross protection against the Sudan strain. A SUDV vaccine is urgently needed to prevent and respond to future outbreaks. Source: WHO Our solution IAVI is developing a vaccine candidate for SUDV that is based on a recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus (rVSV) vector similar to the technology underlying an approved Ebola Zaire virus vaccine. During an Ebola outbreak starting in January 2025 in Uganda, the WHO prioritized IAVI’s SUDV vaccine candidate for a ring vaccination trial and delivered the first vaccinations just four days after the outbreak was declared as part of a comprehensive public health response. Bundibugyo virus The challenge Bundibugyo virus (BDBV) is a filovirus that has caused only three known outbreaks to date. The outbreak that began in Democratic Republic of Congo in May 2026 quickly turned into an epidemic and was determined by the WHO to be a Public Health Emergency of International Concern. There are no licensed vaccines or specific treatments for BDBV. The case fatality rate of BDBV disease ranges between 30-50%. Our solution At the outset of the outbreak in May 2026, IAVI quickly brought a BDBV vaccine candidate in house. It is based on the same recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus (rVSV) vector as our other emerging infectious diseases vaccine candidates as well as the approved Ebola Zaire virus vaccine. The WHO has prioritized development of this vaccine during the epidemic as the most promising candidate in the pipeline.
