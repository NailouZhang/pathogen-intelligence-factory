Unraveling the Transmission Dynamics and Evolutionary Trajectory of Mpox in South Korea: 2022–2025 Outbreak

Authors: Min-Ji Lee, Chi-Hwan Choi, Sang Eun Lee, Jin-Won Kim, Gyuri Sim, Jeong Hyun Lee, Hwajung Yi, - Yoon-Seok Chung, 민지 이
Original abstract (English)
The 2022 monkeypox (mpox) outbreak, driven by clade IIb monkeypox virus (MPXV), was facilitated by human-to-human transmission, particularly within heavy-tailed sexual networks. APOBEC3-associated mutations have been a prominent feature of MPXV genomic evolution during the global outbreak. National genomic data were collected from clinical samples by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency to examine the transmission dynamics and molecular evolution of MPXV. Target enrichment-based next-generation sequencing was used to generate high-quality genomes. Mutation profiling was used to identify key genetic changes; clustering based on common variants provided insights into national transmission dynamics and molecular evolution. Analysis of mpox-positive samples revealed the linkage of most cases to sub-lineage C.1 and a few cases to C.1.1. Genomic clustering revealed an uneven distribution of cases, with a dominant cluster accounting for a substantial proportion of domestic transmission while most other cluster remained relatively limited in size, consistent with heterogeneous transmission patterns. APOBEC3-associated mutations progressively accumulated during the outbreak and were frequently observed in genomic regions associated with viral structural integrity and cell-to-cell transmission and host-modulation, indicating continued genomic diversification during human-to-human transmission. Together with the dominance of localized clusters, these findings suggest heterogeneous transmission patterns and the possible contribution of epidemiologically unrecognized transmission to the spread of MPXV in South Korea. These results underscore value of integrating genomic surveillance and targeted interventions to characterize transmission patterns and monitor viral genomic changes of potential biological or public health relevance.

Paediatric mpox during Sierra Leone’s 2024 to 2025 clade IIb outbreak: A national surveillance and outbreak-investigation study of 415 children

Authors: Eric Nzirakaindi Ikoona, Lucy Namulemo, Mary Magdalene Sinnah, Mohamed Alex Vandi, Foday Sahr
Original abstract (English)
BACKGROUND: Evidence on paediatric mpox during contemporary African clade IIb outbreaks remains limited. We described paediatric cases in Sierra Leone's 2024-2025 clade IIb G.1 outbreak by person, time, and place and examined clinical care, outcomes, vaccination records, and documented exposures. METHODOLOGY/PRINCIPAL FINDINGS: We conducted a retrospective descriptive study using the national District Health Information Software 2 (DHIS2) mpox database, supplemented by patient charts and outbreak-investigation forms. Among 5,466 cases with recorded age, 415 (7.6%) were children younger than 15 years; 188 (45.3%) were aged 0-4 years, 121 (29.2%) were aged 5-9 years, and 106 (25.5%) were aged 10-14 years. Of 415 paediatric cases, 411 were laboratory-confirmed. Outcome was recorded for 261 children; three died (1.1%, exact 95% CI 0.2 to 3.3). Care location was recorded for 285 children; 216 (75.8%) received hospital care for isolation, clinical management, or both. Three sporadic paediatric cases of mpox-like illness were reported from 2017 through 2024; notifications then rose rapidly from April 2025 and peaked in May. Western Area Urban accounted for 195/413 cases with known district; crude notified paediatric rates were highest in Western Area Urban, Koinadugu, and Falaba. Household or caregiving contact was the most common documented exposure among 246 classifiable investigation forms (141, 57.3%). Mpox vaccination status was recorded for 27/54 cases aged 12-14 years; six were vaccinated. CONCLUSIONS/SIGNIFICANCE: Children were not spared during Sierra Leone's clade IIb outbreak. Response plans should include children in case finding, contact follow-up, clinical care, and vaccination assessment. DHIS2 should capture epidemiologic links, clinical outcomes, vaccine eligibility and status, and potential exposure settings through standardised fields.

摘要中文翻译背景:近年来,猴痘病毒已成为重要的公共卫生问题,具有本地和国际传播的潜力。本研究旨在描述一个三代猴痘病毒集群,并对其流行病学特征进行分析。方法:2024年8月19日至23日,中国重庆市报告了一例三代猴痘病毒集群。开展了现场调查以识别高风险暴露和传播途径,并指导公共卫生应对措施。从病例中采集了皮疹样本、肛拭子、咽拭子和血液样本进行实时聚合酶链反应检测。进行了基因组测序以确定猴痘病毒(MPXV)的簇类。结果:该集群包括三名男性同性性行为者,年龄分别为40多岁、30多岁和20多岁。所有病例均表现为轻度症状,包括肛周或生殖器皮疹。病例B在病例A出现症状前四天通过无套性行为暴露于病例A。流行病学调查显示,2024年8月病例A→病例B→病例C的传播链可能通过无套性接触发生。病例A和病例C的样本成功进行了基因组测序,并被鉴定为猴痘病毒IIb簇,E.3谱系。结论:该三代猴痘病毒集群由猴痘病毒IIb簇引起,通过无套性接触传播。研究结果提示猴痘病毒可能存在无症状期的传播。健康教育和持续的监测对于猴痘病毒的预防和控制至关重要。

Original abstract (English)

Recent outbreaks of SARS-CoV-2, H5N1 avian influenza virus (AIV), monkeypox, and Zika virus underscore the unpredictability of viral emergence and the urgent need to understand host/pathogen interactions. Jump to Key Points Cases of clade Ib mpox have increased in the US over the past several months, and this increase is likely to continue, on the basis of the latest data from the CDC. In early September, the CDC reported more than 60 cases of clade I mpox across at least 20 states. As of the CDC’s October 5 update , this number had increased to 80. Most mpox cases in the US continue to occur in men who are gay or bisexual or men who have sex with men (MSM), and many cases involve individuals who have traveled from areas of mpox outbreaks in Central and Eastern Africa or parts of Western Europe where outbreaks have been reported. Consequently, the risk to the general public remains low, and the risk to MSM is low to moderate, according to the CDC. However, an increasing proportion of US cases have occurred among people with no recent international travel or known links to recent travelers, suggesting the virus is spreading domestically. The CDC noted on its website that these cases indicate “a likely transition toward sustained transmission in the United States,” with presumed domestic transmission reported in more than 10 jurisdictions. What’s Driving the Change? Two key elements are converging in mpox epidemiology, said Jason Zucker, MD, assistant professor of medicine in the Division of Infectious Diseases at Columbia University Irving Medical Center in New York City and the corresponding author of a 2024 review of mpox diagnosis and treatment published in JAMA . SUGGESTED FOR YOU First, the number of places from which individuals may acquire clade Ib is increasing. It has been spreading in Western Europe since approximately last fall; therefore, an exposure during travel to Spain or Netherlands matters as much as travel to Central Africa, he said. Second, the virus spreads quickly once it arises in a sexual network, as seen in the 2022 outbreak, said Zucker. Prior infection and vaccination make similar spikes unlikely even with the current increase in cases, he said. However, “we can still see spread between vulnerable individuals who come in close physical contact, and most people acquiring infection are still unvaccinated,” he added. Genomic data from the 2025 California cluster showed a single introduction leading to local spread, and the recent rise in cases is likely that pattern repeating in multiple places at once, said Zucker. “Mild or unrecognized illness may also play a role,” he noted. Clade Ib cases outside of Africa have mainly been mild, especially among those previously vaccinated, he said. “A person with a few lesions who is not asked about mpox may never get tested, and transmission continues undetected,” he explained. Management of patients with mpox has not changed, and the standard remains supportive care and pain control, with CDC consultation for individuals who are immunocompromised to explore experimental antivirals. Prevention is the most important factor, Zucker said. For at-risk patients who are eligible, use every office visit to offer and complete the two-dose JYNNEOS vaccine series, he advised. The right message for patients is that mpox is preventable and manageable, he added. “The clade Ib we are seeing has not presented the same historically; more than 300 cases have been diagnosed in high-income countries without a single death,” said Zucker. A study published in PLOS Neglected Tropical Diseases reveals that children younger than 15 years accounted for 7.6 percent of Sierra Leone’s clade IIb mpox outbreak, with 415 cases recorded. This proportion is significantly higher than the 1.3 percent seen during the 2022 global mpox outbreak, which was primarily driven by adult sexual networks. The research, conducted by Sierra Leone’s National Public Health Agency, analyzed data from the national District Health Information Software 2 database and patient charts. The outbreak was declared over in December 2025, with the analysis covering data frozen on 1 July 2026. The study highlights that household transmission played a major role in exposing young children, with 57.3 percent of the 246 classifiable outbreak-investigation forms indicating household or caregiving contact. The authors caution that geographic differences in paediatric rates may be overestimated due to potential underestimation of age denominators in northern districts. Three children died, resulting in a case fatality proportion of 1.1 percent, with delayed presentation and poor feeding or dehydration noted in the fatal cases. The authors stress the need for improved surveillance and standardized documentation to better understand the outbreak’s impact on children and ensure adequate care.

A retrospective national study published in PLOS Neglected Tropical Diseases has now documented 415 children younger than 15 years among 5,466 cases with recorded age, meaning children accounted for 7.6 percent of the outbreak’s known burden. The finding matters because the 2022 multicountry global outbreak, which was driven largely by sexual networks among adults, reported children and adolescents as only about 1.3 percent of cases. Sierra Leone’s experience shows that during a sustained African clade IIb epidemic, household transmission can pull young children into the epidemiological picture at rates several times higher than the world saw in 2022, and that surveillance systems built around adult-centered assumptions risk underestimating and under-serving this vulnerable group. The research team, led by investigators at Sierra Leone’s National Public Health Agency, assembled their cohort from the national District Health Information Software 2 database, the country’s integrated disease surveillance backbone, and then enriched it with patient charts and the original outbreak-investigation forms completed by district response teams. The analysis covered a database extract frozen on 1 July 2026, after the Ministry of Health declared the outbreak over in December 2025. Of the 415 paediatric cases, 411 were laboratory-confirmed, four were classified as probable, and none were clinician-diagnosed only. The age distribution was strikingly young: 188 children, or 45.3 percent, were aged 0 to 4 years, 121 were aged 5 to 9, and 106 were aged 10 to 14. The median paediatric age was just 5 years, and 230 of the 415 children, or 55.4 percent, were male. Discover more Buy Maps Track Outbreaks Shop African Art The temporal signal was equally telling. National surveillance summaries recorded only three sporadic paediatric cases of mpox-like illness between 2017 and 2024, a period when the disease was largely absent from the country. Notifications then rose sharply from April 2025 and peaked at 37 laboratory-confirmed paediatric cases in the epidemiological week ending 18 May 2025, before declining through June with a smaller resurgence in September and early October. Genomic analyses cited by the authors indicated that the clade IIb G.1 virus had been transmitting locally before public detection, which helps explain how an epidemic that appeared suddenly in adults had already seeded the household settings where young children are most exposed. The paediatric epidemic curve, in other words, was not an independent phenomenon but a mirror of the national outbreak, amplified through domestic contact networks. Yet crude notified paediatric rates told a more nuanced story: Western Area Urban led at 80.2 notified cases per 100,000 estimated children, followed by the northern districts of Koinadugu at 60.5 and Falaba at 30.0. Together, Koinadugu, Falaba, and Karene accounted for 20.1 percent of paediatric cases but only 1.6 percent of cases among people aged 15 and older, a disproportionate paediatric share that the authors flag as a priority for field investigation. They caution, however, that these rates applied a single national age projection to every district, so if northern populations are younger than assumed, their paediatric denominators were underestimated and the rates overestimated. District-specific age denominators and testing data are needed before geographic differences can be read as true incidence. Clinically, the picture was dominated by fever and rash, each documented in 346 of 415 children, or 83.4 percent, with arm and leg lesions the most commonly reported sites. Secondary bacterial skin infection was documented in 36 children, or 8.7 percent, and one child developed acute respiratory distress syndrome. Care location was recorded for 285 children, of whom 216, or 75.8 percent, received hospital care for isolation, clinical management, or both, while 69 were managed at home. The authors emphasize that hospital admission in this outbreak did not necessarily signal severe disease, because admission frequently served isolation and infection-control purposes in crowded households where home isolation was impractical. Among 162 retrieved patient charts for hospitalized children, the median length of stay was 6 days, with an interquartile range of 4 to 9 days, and admission indication was available in 119 charts: isolation alone in 48, clinical management plus isolation in 62, and clinical management alone in 9. The fatal cases shared a concerning pattern of delayed presentation, with onset-to-care intervals of 6, 15, and 27 days, and records described poor feeding or dehydration in two children, secondary bacterial skin infection in one, and moderate acute malnutrition in one. HIV status was unavailable for all three children who died, a gap that carries weight given a companion national analysis from the same team reporting fourfold or higher mpox mortality among people living with HIV during the outbreak. The authors argue that national records should distinguish admission purpose and standardize documentation of severity, treatment, complications, and recovery, because the emergency-era data could not fully separate the clinical course of mpox from the circumstances of delayed or inadequate care. The exposure analysis, drawn from 246 classifiable outbreak-investigation forms retrieved for 331 of the 415 children, pointed firmly toward the household. Household or caregiving contact, defined to include a household case, direct care, shared sleeping space, shared bedding or clothing, bathing, or feeding, was documented in 141 of 246 forms, or 57.3 percent. Another 48 children, or 19.5 percent, had several plausible exposures with no single predominant source, 39, or 15.9 percent, reported school, peer, or community contact, 12, or 4.9 percent, reported animal, wildlife, or bushmeat exposure, and 6, or 2.4 percent, reported health-care contact. Notably, eight of the twelve animal-related records came from Koinadugu or Falaba, hinting at rural exposure patterns that differ from the urban household transmission dominating Western Area Urban. The authors are careful to stress that these categories describe documented exposure histories, not proven routes of infection, and that the forms rarely captured linked-case identifiers, timing, or contact testing consistently enough to establish who infected whom. Vaccination data proved interpretable only for a narrow slice of the cohort. Sierra Leone’s response used the modified vaccinia Ankara-Bavarian Nordic vaccine, or MVA-BN, in a targeted single-dose campaign for selected groups aged 12 years and older, consistent with the World Health Organization’s prequalification of the vaccine for adolescents aged 12 to 17. Among the 54 paediatric cases aged 12 to 14, vaccination status was recorded for only 27, of whom six were vaccinated. Dates, doses, indications, eligibility, and timing relative to exposure or symptom onset were inconsistently documented, so the study could not estimate coverage, access, or effectiveness. The authors note that WHO’s 2024 position paper supports off-label MVA-BN vaccination of children during outbreaks when expected benefits exceed potential risks, and that by September 2026 European authorization for the vaccine, marketed as Imvanex, had extended to children aged 2 years and older on the strength of paediatric immunogenicity data. With 188 children younger than 5 in this cohort alone, they argue that Sierra Leone’s future guidance should define eligibility for exposed younger children under applicable national authorization, with prospective safety and effectiveness monitoring. The study also surfaced a sensitive clinical and child-protection dimension. After deduplicating surveillance fields, 55 of the 415 children had documented genital or perianal lesions, and the DHIS2 field labelled vaginal lesions was marked for 43 of 185 girls, most often among those aged 10 to 14. The authors are explicit that genital or perianal lesions do not identify the route of infection: plausible explanations include disseminated disease, transfer from another body site, close caregiving contact, contaminated materials, sexual contact, sexual abuse, or simple recording error, and the retrieved records could not distinguish among them. Encouragingly, of 43 records retrieved for these children, 26, or 60.5 percent, documented a child-protection assessment or referral decision, 9 involved referral to social-work or child-protection services, and 3 involved forensic or medico-legal assessment. The authors recommend that clinicians take an age-appropriate exposure history and seek child-protection or medico-legal assessment whenever the history or examination raises concern for abuse. The broader lesson is one of surveillance architecture. Sex was recorded for all 415 children and district for 99.5 percent, but care location was recorded for only 68.7 percent, final outcome for 62.9 percent, and HIV status for a mere 18.3 percent, while the national database lacked complete fields for lesion count, mucosal involvement, nutritional status, treatment, exposure route, household links, and recovery time. The authors call for DHIS2 to capture epidemiologic links, clinical outcomes, vaccine eligibility and status, and potential exposure settings through standardized fields, supported by detailed investigation forms, and for prospective household and community contact studies to define paediatric transmission pathways. Passive case finding likely missed mild infections and children who never reached care, and the emergency context limited record retrieval, so the true paediatric burden may be higher than reported. What the study establishes beyond doubt is that children were not spared in Sierra Leone’s clade IIb epidemic, and that mpox response plans, from case finding and contact follow-up to clinical care and vaccination assessment, must be designed with children explicitly in view rather than as an afterthought to adult-focused strategies. Hong Kong has recorded 21 cases of mpox since August, accounting for 70 per cent of this year’s total, prompting health authorities to urge people who engage in high-risk sexual contact to take protective measures and get vaccinated to reduce their risk of infection. The latest case, involving a 29-year-old man, brought the total number recorded in Hong Kong this year to 30, comprising 23 local cases and seven imported ones, the Centre for Health Protection (CHP) said on Saturday. Twenty-one cases, or 70 per cent of the total, were recorded since August. Epidemiological investigations showed that all 21 patients reported engaging in high-risk sexual behaviour during the incubation period, including having sex with male strangers or multiple partners, and having sex without a condom, the centre said. Genetic analysis of the virus also found that some cases had identical genetic sequences, suggesting they might have shared a common source of infection, with multiple transmission chains existing among the population of men who have sex with men. “The CHP once again urges individuals who have engaged in high-risk sexual contact, particularly with strangers, to remain vigilant and take appropriate personal protective measures, including receiving the mpox vaccination and avoiding high-risk sexual contact, so as to lower the risk of infection,” it said. The centre also noted that more than half of the patients diagnosed in recent months with mpox, formerly known as monkeypox, had used dating apps to find casual sexual partners. “In some cases, patients were unable to contact their newly met sexual partners, which hinders effective contact tracing and the implementation of corresponding prevention and control measures,” it said. It urged people to keep their sexual partners’ contact details to make contact tracing easier. The latest case under investigation involved a 29-year-old man who developed a papule – a small, solid, raised bump – on his buttock on September 28. He later developed rashes on his limbs and buttock, as well as a fever and swollen lymph nodes. He attended an outreach vaccination service organised by the centre on October 8. Staff noticed his symptoms and arranged for him to receive isolation treatment at Princess Margaret Hospital in Kwai Chung. His clinical specimen tested positive for the mpox virus. The patient was in a stable condition, the centre said. He had high-risk sexual contact with a man in Hong Kong during the incubation period, it added. The centre said it was continuing its epidemiological investigation of the case and would report it to the World Health Organization. Hong Kong has recorded 114 mpox cases – 90 local and 24 imported – since 2022, all involving male patients. Mpox is a zoonosis – an infectious disease that can be transmitted from animals to humans – caused by the monkeypox virus. Infection can occur through contact with the virus from infected animals or people, or contaminated materials. People can become infected through bites or scratches from certain wild animals, such as some species of primates, rodents and squirrels, or through direct contact with their body fluids. Human-to-human transmission is mainly through close contact with infected individuals, such as skin-to-skin, mouth-to-mouth, mouth-to-skin and sexual contact. The likelihood of transmission through respiratory droplets is relatively low. The Department of Health provides mpox vaccination services for high-risk groups, including people who engage in high-risk sexual practices, such as having multiple sexual partners, sex workers or people who have had a sexually transmitted infection in the past 12 months
