Willingness of Chinese medical students to work on the frontline during a potential mpox outbreak: a nationwide cross-sectional study.

Authors: Jiaxin Chen, Jie Ge, Yunfeng Han, Zhiping Xie, Honglong Li, Yan Zhao, Yuehui Jia
Original abstract (English)
BACKGROUND: The global mpox epidemic has been designated a public health emergency of international concern, placing substantial strain on public health systems worldwide. As a critical reserve workforce for outbreak response, medical students' willingness to serve on monkeypox (mpox) frontlines remains understudied, and evidence on their mpox-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices and their association with frontline work willingness is still limited. METHODS: We conducted an online nationwide cross-sectional survey among Chinese medical students from November 2023 to March 2024 using snowball sampling. We used multinomial logistic regression to identify independent influencing factors. RESULTS: We included a total of 4,098 participants. Overall, 77.06% were willing to perform frontline mpox work, 18.57% were hesitant, and 4.37% refused. Among participant characteristics, studying medicine at others' suggestion was associated with both higher hesitancy (odds ratio (OR) = 1.425; 95% confidence interval (CI) = 1.142-1.776) and refusal (OR = 1.635; 95% CI = 1.110-2.407). Greater mpox knowledge (OR = 0.973; 95% CI = 0.966-0.981) and higher vaccination-related behaviour scores (OR = 0.894; 95% CI = 0.882-0.907) were associated with lower hesitancy to work on the mpox frontline. Higher vaccination-related behaviour scores were also associated with lower refusal (OR = 0.883; 95% CI = 0.862-0.904). CONCLUSIONS: Most Chinese medical students demonstrated favourable willingness to support frontline mpox response. Medical education should strengthen training in mpox prevention and professional ethics. Public health policies should use risk-stratified deployment, provide adequate protection and psychological support, and establish targeted mobilisation mechanisms to improve the emergency response capacity of the medical student reserve workforce.

Although over 1,000 cases have been confirmed, gaps in case‐based surveillance could lead to under-ascertainment. Original abstract (English)
Abstract Background Uganda's Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response framework designates community-based surveillance (CBS) as a core pillar of outbreak detection, yet how this pillar functions during a live, stigmatised, cross-border outbreak remains poorly understood. This study qualitatively examined CBS during the 2024 Mpox Clade Ib outbreak in Uganda's Kasese and Amuru border districts, focusing on the enablers, constraints, and adaptive responses shaping surveillance performance. Methods A qualitative descriptive design was employed, with data collected between June and August 2025 through 19 key informant interviews and 8 focus group discussions, engaging 100 participants across the two districts. Participants included Health-sector personnel, implementing partner staff, Community Health Workers (CHWs), and community members. Data were analysed thematically. Results Three interrelated themes emerged. Institutional memory and pre-existing infrastructure from prior outbreaks (Ebola, COVID-19, and the 2022 Mpox outbreak) strengthened clinical suspicion, reactivated standing response structures, and sustained community trust and partner support. Structural and social barriers, including delayed disbursement of funds, fragile specimen transport, limited staff, fragmented coordination, and stigma linked to both the sexualised transmission narrative and the confusion of Mpox with locally recognised skin conditions, drove concealment and care seeking outside the formal system. Communities responded with improvised adaptive mechanisms, including informal self-diagnosis, peer-based referral networks among key populations, informal cross-border resource-sharing and integration of Mpox screening into existing platforms such as antenatal and HIV/ART clinics. Conclusions Strengthening CBS in Uganda's border districts requires reliable financing, sustainable specimen transport, adequate staffing, locally appropriate risk communication, and formalised cross-border collaboration as a deliberate component of preparedness in high-mobility border settings.

Authors: An-Ke Chen, Ning Fang, Menglin Ye, Yu-Xin Qiu, Yuan-Yuan Zhang, Jin-Qing Huang, Jin Ding, Chaoneng Ji, Ji-Xi Li
Original abstract (English)
Mpox, caused by monkeypox virus (MPXV), remains a public health concern, yet the structural basis of poxvirus DNA ligase function has been poorly defined despite strong genetic and biochemical evidence supporting its essential role in viral DNA replication and repair. Here, we report the crystal structure of MPXV DNA ligase (MpxvLig) in complex with nicked DNA at 2.04 Å resolution. MpxvLig adopts the canonical three-domain architecture of ATP-dependent DNA ligases, comprising an N-terminal DNA-binding domain (DBD), a nucleotidyltransferase domain, and a C-terminal oligonucleotide-binding fold domain (OBD), which together form a clamp-like configuration that embraces nicked DNA for catalysis. The structure captures MpxvLig in an active catalytic state corresponding to the adenylylation step, in which Lys233 forms a covalent ligase-AMP intermediate. Comparative structural and biochemical analyses with the catalytic core of human DNA ligase III reveal substantial differences in domain arrangement and DNA engagement, consistent with the stronger DNA-binding capacity and higher ligation activity of MpxvLig in vitro . Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) further shows that apo-MpxvLig adopts an extended flashlight-like conformation in solution. Notably, we identify a virus-specific small-molecule-binding pocket within the DNA-binding domain. Mitoxantrone binds directly to this pocket and inhibits MpxvLig activity at micromolar concentrations, while exerting minimal effects on the human ligase. Mutations in pocket-defining residues markedly attenuate this inhibition, supporting an on-target mechanism. Together, these findings establish the structural basis of poxvirus DNA ligation and provide a framework for the rational development of selective antiviral inhibitors targeting MpxvLig.

Original abstract (English)

Original abstract (English)
Mpox (monkeypox) poses disproportionate risks to pregnant women and children, yet evidence on immunisation and prevention in these groups remains limited. This scoping review mapped and synthesised existing evidence on vaccines and non-pharmaceutical preventive measures for mpox in pregnant women and children, to assess reported effectiveness, and to identify implementation barriers and facilitators. Peer-reviewed and Grey literature were searched in PubMed, Scopus, and Google for records published since 2008; databases were last searched on 19 September 2025. Screening and data extraction followed a pre-specified protocol and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) framework. Key themes included immunisation strategies, preventive measures, effectiveness, barriers, and facilitators. Forty-eight peer-reviewed articles and 16 Grey literature sources were included. Third-generation non-replicating vaccines (MVA-BN/JYNNEOS/Imvanex/IMVAMUNE) were consistently recommended for pregnant women and children because of their favourable safety profiles. Observational and programmatic estimates of vaccine effectiveness (single dose 36-86%; two doses up to approximately 89%) came almost entirely from adults, and no effectiveness estimate specific to pregnant women or children was identified. No source reported vaccine use by trimester, and paediatric data were limited to small studies. Ring vaccination and post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) were supported mainly by adult data. Major barriers included limited vaccine supply and cold-chain and logistical constraints in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), vaccine hesitancy driven by safety concerns, particularly in pregnancy, and the absence of randomised trials in pregnant women and children. Few sources were centred on Africa or other LMICs. Facilitators included international coordination, targeted surveillance, flexible delivery strategies such as intradermal dosing, and integration with maternal and child health services. Priorities include prospective safety and immunogenicity studies in pregnancy and childhood, equitable vaccine access in LMICs, and tailored risk communication to address hesitancy.

Original abstract (English)
Sierra Leone confirmed mpox on January 10, 2025, and declared an emergency three days later. National surveillance recorded 5,442 confirmed cases and 60 deaths (1.1%) through December 14. District teams should protect confidential care seeking, sustain rash recognition and sampling, and track result communication. They should support feasible isolation, plan sustained staffing, and connect specimens to national and regional sequencing. Notifications declined before the enhanced response phase, and concurrent interventions prevent separate effect estimates. The declared outbreak end does not exclude undetected transmission. A proposed district package links these lessons to responsible officers, indicators, review triggers, and resources for prospective evaluation.

The four Anchorage cases are among men who reported no travel outside of Alaska in the past 30 days, Mclaughlin said. That is a contrast with the previous Alaska cases, all of which were among people who had recent travel experiences. Up to now, travel had been implicated in the spread of mpox in Alaska. Officials are currently trying to track the source of infection, said McLaughlin and Dr. Randi Rollins, a U.S. Centers for Disease Control epidemiologist working with the Alaska Department of Health. The lack of reported out-of-state travel "does not necessarily establish that the monkeypox virus is circulating here in Alaska," Rollins said. "They may have had exposure to somebody who traveled to Alaska, and they may be the end of that transmission line at this point. We don't know." Mpox is characterized by lesions that appear on various parts of the body. It usually takes three to 17 days after infection for symptoms to emerge, Rollins said, and in the first phase, the symptoms are flu-like: "fever, chills, fatigue, headache, sore muscles, and even swollen lymph nodes." The lesions develop in the second phase, with bumps that are firm to the touch or sometimes tender, she said. Some people skip the early symptoms and do not have any signs of infection until the rash phase develops, she said. The sickness lasts a few weeks, and patients are considered to have recovered when all the lesions are gone. Until that last lesion is completely healed, "the person is still infectious and can transmit monkeypox, and that can typically take two to four weeks," she said. McLaughlin said the Alaska patients are isolating and recovering. Two of the reported cases were among men who are partners, but it is unknown whether there are any additional links between any of the patients, he said. The new outbreak of mpox in Anchorage fits a national pattern. After a lull that followed the 2022-23 global outbreak, cases are on the rise again in the United States. An uptick in infections started in March, the CDC reported. The virus circulating now is genetically different from the variety behind the global outbreak in 2022, which is called clade II. The currently circulating virus is called clade I. In Central Africa, the clade I variation had been more deadly than the clade II variation. But the current variation of the clade I virus appears to be not particularly deadly, according to health officials. The JYNNEOS vaccine , approved by the Food and Drug Administration in 2019, guards against both clades of mpox, as well as smallpox, a disease that historically killed much of the world's population but is now considered to have been eradicated. The vaccine, which is administered in two doses, is available through the Anchorage Health Department and other health providers. It is recommended for certain groups of people considered to be at higher risk for infections. Those include men who have sex with other men, anyone with exposure to another person suspected of having mpox, people who are traveling to parts of the world with mpox outbreaks and medical workers, such as those employed in labs or healthcare facilities, whose jobs put them at risk of being exposed to this and other pathogens. The vaccine is effective even for people already infected with the virus, reducing risks even for those who might develop the disease, Rollins said. For anyone who has been exposed, "it's recommended that you get that vaccine within 4 days of exposure," she said. While that time window is ideal, "you can get it up to about 14 days and still see effectiveness at reducing disease severity," she said. State immunization records show that since 2022, 851 people in Alaska have received a first dose of the JYNNEOS vaccine and 604 receiving a second dose, Rollins said. Like other pox diseases, they were characterized by skin lesions and some flu-like symptoms. "One patient did experience respiratory illness, fever, muscle aches, joint aches." Scientists say borealpox circulates among small mammals . So far, red-backed voles, which are plentiful across most of Alaska, have been identified as a major reservoir. Investigation of cases so far has focused on household pets that appear to have encountered infected voles or other small mammals. Unlike the case with mpox, there is no vaccine for borealpox. Mpox vaccine stock ‘will be deployed as needed’ as cases climb to 26 - Daily Maverick

News Brief
The article reports on the current mpox situation in South Africa, highlighting an uptick in cases since August 2026. As of 2026-10-27, 26 confirmed mpox cases have been reported, with the majority concentrated in the Western Cape. The outbreak is described as ongoing, with the NICD emphasizing the need for vigilance without causing unnecessary alarm. Seven individuals have been hospitalized, but no deaths have been reported this year. The health department is actively managing the outbreak through surveillance, contact tracing, and public awareness campaigns. Mpox vaccines, including MVA-BN, are being deployed as needed, with 2,365 doses administered between September 2025 and September 2026. There are still 3,865 doses available, set to expire by 31 August 2027. The department is reviewing procurement options for additional vaccine doses, depending on recommendations from the Incident Management Team and the National Advisory Group on Immunisation. The NICD is also focusing on early intervention and identifying additional cases to reduce the likelihood of further spread. The article notes that while mpox cases have been relatively low in South Africa, the current situation is considered an outbreak due to local transmission and the need for public health response.
Original source text (English)
Twenty of this year’s 26 confirmed mpox cases came after August, concentrated mostly within the Western Cape. Elri Voigt looks at how the virus has changed and how much of the donated vaccine is left. The viral illness mpox has caused several clusters of outbreaks across the world. In South Africa, sporadic cases have been detected, but an uptick since August has now brought the illness back into the spotlight. As of 27 October, 26 mpox cases have been confirmed in South Africa this year, Dr Jacqueline Weyer, Head of the Centre for Emerging Zoonotic and Parasitic Diseases at the National Institute for Communicable Diseases (NICD), told Spotlight. Of those 26 cases , 18 are in the Western Cape, three are in Gauteng, two are in the Eastern Cape, another two are in the Free State and one is in KwaZulu-Natal. The majority of cases, 20, have been detected since August this year. All but one of these cases were in men between the ages of 23 and 50 years. One case was in a woman aged 35 to 44. Seven people have been admitted to hospital , but no deaths have been reported so far this year. Mpox was first identified in humans in 1970, and was mostly observed in Central, East and West Africa . The Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) was particularly affected, with thousands of cases reported since 2005. Then in 2022, the virus spread rapidly to multiple countries for the first time. About 140 countries have reported mpox cases since 2022, according to the World Health Organization’s mpox dashboard , with more than 190,000 laboratory-confirmed cases reported and over 536 deaths to date. The overall number of mpox cases in South Africa has been low. Our back-of-the-envelope calculation puts the total confirmed number of mpox cases since 2022 at 71. The NICD mpox dashboard lists a total of 66 cases and three deaths between 2024 and 2026 . In 2023, according to Weyer, there were no reported cases of mpox in the country. There were five cases of mpox identified in 2022, according to information on the NICD’s page on mpox . While the number of mpox cases have been relatively low to date, the current situation can be referred to as an outbreak. Weyer told Spotlight an “outbreak” doesn’t necessarily mean a “large number” of people are affected. “Outbreaks may be large, small and confined, and for some conditions, a single case may be denoted as an outbreak.” In public health, she added, an outbreak “refers to the occurrence of cases of a disease in a particular place and period that warrants investigation and a public health response”. When asked if there is a risk for a larger mpox outbreak in the country, Weyer is cautious. “ We cannot rule out the possibility of further cases, particularly while investigations are ongoing and local transmission has occurred previously,” she said. “The risk of further spread depends on several factors, including how quickly cases are detected, the number and nature of contacts, the extent of transmission within affected networks, and the effectiveness of public health measures.” She added: “We have the opportunity to intervene early, identify additional cases and reduce the likelihood of further spread.” She said the NICD’s approach is to remain alert without creating unnecessary alarm. “We are continuing to strengthen surveillance, laboratory investigation, contact tracing, vaccination of eligible priority groups and public awareness,” she said. There is a limited number of donated mpox vaccines available in South Africa. Since there are currently no registered vaccines against mpox or other treatment options in the country, any vaccine or treatment used will need to be supplied via a Section 21 request, which allows for medicines that are not registered in South Africa to be brought in. Vaccines against smallpox – which is caused by a virus closely related to the mpox virus – can offer some protection against the illness. There are three vaccines that can be used against mpox – LC16m8, MVA-BN and ACAM2000 . The latter is only registered for limited use in the United States. Last year, the Africa Centres for Disease control and Prevention (Africa CDC) donated just over 10,000 doses of the vaccine MVA-BN (known by the brand names imvanex and JYNNEOS) to the national Department of Health. These doses have been used in vaccination efforts ever since, with a total of 2,365 doses administered between September 2025 and September 2026, health department spokesperson Foster Mohale told Spotlight. There are currently 3,865 doses still available for use, according to Mohale, which will expire on 31 August 2027. We asked the health department about the remaining 4,000 or so doses but are still awaiting a reply. When asked whether any additional mpox vaccines would be procured or donated, Mohale said: “Yes, the department is currently reviewing procurement options of additional mpox vaccine doses, dependent on the recommendation from [the] Incident Management Team and the National Advisory Group on Immunisation.” Mohale said vaccine doses are distributed to provinces as required, depending on “disease outbreak and response”. Vaccination for mpox is recommended for people who are at high risk of acquiring the virus and at risk of getting severe disease. It can also be considered as a form of post-exposure prophylaxis for people who have been in contact with someone who has mpox. An antiviral medicine called tecovirimat is occasionally used to treat mpox symptoms in severe cases. There is some limited stock of the drug available for use in South Africa, which Mohale said was sourced through the World Health Organization (WHO) and Africa CDC’s Access and Allocation Mechanism. There are still more than 40 containers of tecovirimat capsules available, Mohale said. But there are not currently any plans to procure more, although he added that this will be dependent on the recommendation from the Incident Management Team and the National Essential Medicines List Committee. He did not say how many treatment courses are in a container. In a 2025 national Department of Health mpox guideline document , the National Essential Medicines List Committee suggests that tecovirimat should only be used to treat those with severe disease who have been admitted to hospital. This is because the drug has not been shown to make any significant difference in most cases of mpox. Earlier this year, results from a phase 3 study published in the New England Journal of Medicine showed that there was “no evidence that tecovirimat therapy shortened the time to clinical resolution, reduced pain, or increased viral clearance among adults with clade II mpox”. At first, mpox cases in the country were traced back to international travel. Then in 2024 and 2025 we started seeing local transmission. Most people diagnosed with mpox in 2024 reported no recent international travel history, Weyer said, noting that “the epidemiological assessment indicated that local person-to-person transmission was occurring”. For the current situation, Weyer said local transmission is “implied”, noting that investigations are still ongoing. “This has been established by identifying contact links between cases, and identifying the same clade and sub-lineage of the mpox virus associated with the cases to date.” There were some individuals who were diagnosed with mpox this year who had recently been out of the country. One of the people had recently travelled to Spain , while another had travelled to Belgium. In two other cases international travellers came to South Africa already carrying the virus. The mpox virus has two clades, clade I and clade II, with several sub-clades. Professor Tulio de Oliveira, Director of the Centre for Epidemic Response and Innovation at Stellenbosch University, explained to Spotlight previously that clades are a classification system based on the genetic similarities between different strains of a virus. “So, what it means is that when we see a genetic change [in a virus] that’s really visible and that may have impacted it, normally we call it a different clade or genotype or variant,” he said at the time. For now, the two mpox sub-clades that are driving most cases in people are sub-clade Ib and sub-clade IIb. This year, cases of mpox in South Africa are a mix of the two sub-clades. The mpox dashboard shows that of the 26 cases , 19 were sub-clade Ib, four were sub-clade IIb, and three cases didn’t have sub-clade information available. In 2022, sub-clade IIb was driving the mpox outbreak, said Weyer. This changed by 2024 when a new strain of the mpox virus spread beyond the DRC. Sub-clade Ib was first identified in the DRC in 2023 and was behind a large outbreak of the illness in the country that year. This sub-clade spread from the DRC through international travel, and cases of sub- clade Ib were reported across the globe by 2024 . The first case of mpox where sequencing indicated sub-clade Ib in South Africa was detected in 2025, according to the NICD dashboard. A total of 15 mpox cases were reported that year, and sequencing information was available in all but one of the cases. Weyer said that at the moment there is not any evidence to indicate that there are additional mpox clades or sub-clades circulating in the country. Knowing which clade or sub-clade someone with mpox has doesn’t change how their illness is managed. Instead, it helps scientists track outbreaks. “Understanding the genetic character of the viruses assists us in tracking outbreaks, evolution of the viruses and identifying markers which may affect diagnostic test performance and potentially vaccine efficacy,” Weyer said. “The central priorities remain early diagnosis, appropriate clinical care, identifying and monitoring contacts, vaccination of eligible target groups, and communicating accurate information without stigmatising affected individuals or communities. The approach is the same regardless of the current genetic character of these viruses,” she added. Mpox is mainly spread through close contact with someone who has the virus or contact with material like bedding or clothing that has been in contact with someone with mpox. The illness can cause flu-like symptoms – like a headache, sore throat, fever and muscle aches – as well as a distinct and often painful rash. The rash can occur on someone’s palms or soles of their feet, face, mouth and throat and sometimes the genital areas. It starts as a flat sore, then becomes a blister which can be itchy or painful. As the rash heals, the lesions dry up, crust over and fall off. The virus has an incubation period of anything from five to 21 days – but usually it is around seven to 14 days. “Mpox can affect anyone who has close contact with an infected person. We should avoid assumptions about who can acquire the infection and ensure that people feel able to seek healthcare without fear of judgement or discrimination,” said Weyer. Mpox is normally diagnosed by swabbing the lesions from the rash and sending that for testing with a PCR (polymerase chain reaction) test that looks for the virus’ DNA. While the overall case fatality for mpox is low, typically below 1% , people who are immunocompromised (such as people living with uncontrolled HIV), pregnant women, and children, have a much higher risk of severe illness, complications and even death. In most cases, according to the mpox guideline document, symptoms can be managed at home, with rash-related pain being a particular challenge. Severe mpox cases require being admitted to hospital. Someone who has mpox is considered contagious until the rash has completely gone away, and the scabs have healed . Thus, people diagnosed with mpox are advised to self-isolate until their rash has gone away completely. “People who develop an unexplained rash, particularly when accompanied by fever, swollen lymph nodes or a history of possible exposure, should seek medical assessment,” Weyer advised. “They should avoid close physical contact with others while awaiting clinical guidance and testing. The public should understand that mpox is preventable and that early diagnosis and appropriate care are important in reducing transmission and complications.” DM This article was first published by Spotlight – health journalism in the public interest. First Mpox Genomes from Uruguay Reveal Multiple Introductions and Limited Spread - Bioengineer.org

News Brief
Researchers in Uruguay have sequenced and analyzed the first 17 monkeypox virus genomes from the 2022 global clade IIb epidemic, revealing multiple independent introductions of the virus into the country. These introductions were linked to travel from Brazil, Argentina, and Chile, with the virus lineages B.1, B.1.2, and B.1.6 identified. Despite these repeated introductions, local transmission chains were found to be short and geographically restricted, indicating that the limited spread of the outbreak was due to restricted transmission rather than fewer viral entries. The study, published in Virology Journal, highlights the importance of combining genomic surveillance with epidemiological data during emerging outbreaks. The research was conducted by a team of scientists and published on November 6, 2026, by Bioengineer.org, providing a comprehensive understanding of the outbreak dynamics in Uruguay.

Compared with sexual transmission, nonsexually acquired clade II mpox is associated with an increased risk for hospitalization, a higher prevalence of HIV, and lower vaccination rates. Patients with clade II mpox (previously monkeypox) resulting from nonsexual transmission are more frequently hospitalized and have a higher prevalence of HIV than those who report recent sexual contact, according to study results presented at IDWeek 2026, held from October 21 to 24, in Washington, DC. Researchers evaluated differences in demographic characteristics, clinical outcomes, and potential exposures among patients with sexually and nonsexually acquired mpox in California. The analysis included confirmed and probable clade II mpox cases reported to the California Department of Public Health between May 2022 and December 2024. Drawing on public health surveillance records, the researchers sorted cases into groups based on whether patients reported sexual activity in the 21 days before their symptoms began. Patients who denied sexual contact during this period were included in the nonsexual exposure group only if their initial lesions occurred outside the genital and perianal regions. Those reporting no sexual contact but presenting with lesions initially involving these areas were excluded from that group. More information is needed on the dynamics of which populations may be at risk for non-sexual mpox transmission for targeted messaging and strategies. Demographic and clinical characteristics were compared using proportions via the Wilson method. Statistical significance was assessed based on whether the CIs overlapped between groups. Of 6651 reported mpox cases, 5206 had sufficient information to determine sexual exposure classification. Among these patients, 561 (10.8%) were categorized as having nonsexually acquired mpox, while 4645 (89.2%) reported recent sexual contact. Hospitalization occurred in 12.5% of patients in the nonsexual exposure group compared with 4.7% in the sexual exposure group. The prevalence of HIV was also higher among patients classified as having nonsexually acquired mpox (53.3% vs 39.8%). The researchers reported differences in vaccination history and potential environmental exposure. Most patients in both groups were unvaccinated against mpox, although the proportion was higher among those in the nonsexual group (96.8% vs 87.9%). Receipt of 1 vaccine dose was reported in 2.3% vs 5.6% of patients, respectively, while 0.9% vs 6.5% had received 2 doses. Possible exposure through contaminated objects or surfaces was reported by 16.4% of patients in the nonsexual group compared with 10.0% of those in the sexual exposure group. Patients classified as having nonsexually acquired mpox also differed by age, sex at birth, and race and ethnicity. The proportion assigned female at birth was higher in the nonsexual group (4.6% vs 2.2%), as was the proportion younger than 18 years (2.3% vs 0.3%). Black patients accounted for 17.5% of nonsexual cases and 10.8% of sexual cases, while Hispanic or Latino patients represented 53.5% and 43.5%, respectively. White patients accounted for a smaller proportion of the nonsexual group than the sexual exposure group (18.5% vs 32.2%). Regarding sexual orientation, 20.0% of patients in the nonsexual group identified as heterosexual compared with 7.3% of those in the sexual exposure group. Conversely, 54.2% vs 73.7% identified as gay, lesbian, or same-gender loving. Patients declining to disclose their sexual orientation represented 5.9% and 5.4% of the respective groups. Geographic socioeconomic differences were identified using the Health Performance Index (HPI). Residence in an area within the lowest HPI quartile was more common among patients in the nonsexual group (38.5% vs 23.4%), whereas residence in the highest quartile was less common (14.5% vs 27.9%). All reported differences were considered statistically significant based on nonoverlapping 95% CIs. “More information is needed on the dynamics of which populations may be at risk for non-sexual mpox transmission for targeted messaging and strategies,” the researchers concluded. (NIAID via AP, File) A rare viral disease has emerged in an outbreak in Alaska’s largest city that appears to be expanding, state health officials report. There are now four new cases in Anchorage of mpox , a viral disease formerly called monkeypox that is spread through close contact and can be fatal. None of the patients reported traveling outside the state in the monthlong period before becoming infected. It is the highest number of annual mpox cases in Alaska since 2022, when five cases were diagnosed in the state amid a global outbreak . Between 2023 and 2025, there were only two Alaska cases. Three of the Anchorage cases emerged in October and were reported in late October through a state Department of Health alert intended to raise awareness in the public and among healthcare providers. The fourth case was reported to state officials Thursday, said Dr. Joe McLaughlin, Alaska’s state epidemiologist. The four Anchorage cases are among men who reported no travel outside of Alaska in the past 30 days, Mclaughlin said. That is a contrast with the previous Alaska cases, all of which were among people who had recent travel experiences. Up to now, travel had been implicated in the spread of mpox in Alaska. Officials are currently trying to track the source of infection, said McLaughlin and Dr. Randi Rollins, a U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention epidemiologist working with the Alaska Department of Health. The lack of reported out-of-state travel “does not necessarily establish that the monkeypox virus is circulating here in Alaska,” Rollins said. “They may have had exposure to somebody who traveled to Alaska, and they may be the end of that transmission line at this point. We don’t know.” Mpox is characterized by lesions that appear on various parts of the body. It usually takes three to 17 days after infection for symptoms to emerge, Rollins said, and in the first phase, the symptoms are flu-like: “fever, chills, fatigue, headache, sore muscles, and even swollen lymph nodes.” The lesions develop in the second phase, with bumps that are firm to the touch or sometimes tender, she said. Some people skip the early symptoms and do not have any signs of infection until the rash phase develops, she said. The sickness lasts a few weeks, and patients are considered to have recovered when all the lesions are gone. Until that last lesion is completely healed, “the person is still infectious and can transmit monkeypox, and that can typically take two to four weeks,” she said. McLaughlin said the Alaska patients are isolating and recovering. Two of the reported cases were among men who are partners, but it is unknown whether there are any additional links between any of the patients, he said. Increase in cases nationally The new outbreak of mpox in Anchorage fits a national pattern. After a lull that followed the 2022-23 global outbreak, cases are on the rise again in the United States. An uptick in infections started in March, the CDC reported. The virus circulating now is genetically different from the variety behind the global outbreak in 2022, which is called clade II. The currently circulating virus is called clade I. In Central Africa, the clade I variation had been more deadly than the clade II variation. But the current variation of the clade I virus appears to be not particularly deadly, according to health officials. Spread of the virus happens through close contact, including but not limited to sexual contact. Alaska health officials are encouraging anyone considered to be at risk of infection to get vaccinated. The JYNNEOS vaccine , approved by the Food and Drug Administration in 2019, guards against both clades of mpox, as well as smallpox, a disease that historically killed much of the world’s population but is now considered to have been eradicated. The vaccine, which is administered in two doses, is available through the Anchorage Health Department and other health providers. It is recommended for certain groups of people considered to be at higher risk for infections. Those include men who have sex with other men, anyone with exposure to another person suspected of having mpox, people who are traveling to parts of the world with mpox outbreaks and medical workers, such as those employed in labs or healthcare facilities, whose jobs put them at risk of being exposed to this and other pathogens. The vaccine is effective even for people already infected with the virus, reducing risks even for those who might develop the disease, Rollins said. For anyone who has been exposed, “it’s recommended that you get that vaccine within four days of exposure,” she said. While that time window is ideal, “you can get it up to about 14 days and still see effectiveness at reducing disease severity,” she said. State immunization records show that since 2022, 851 people in Alaska have received a first dose of the JYNNEOS vaccine and 604 receiving a second dose, Rollins said. The (Alain Jocard, Pool via AP, File) New borealpox cases reported While healthcare providers are being advised to watch for the resurgence of mpox, there is news about a rarer type of pox disease: borealpox. Two cases of borealpox , formerly known as Alaskapox, have been diagnosed this year, Rollins and McLaughlin said. That brings the world’s total number of diagnosed borealpox cases to nine. The disease, related to mpox, smallpox and other poxes, was first identified in 2015 in Fairbanks. The first six cases were found in Fairbanks and were considered to be mild. Like other pox diseases, they were characterized by skin lesions and some flu-like symptoms. However, in January of 2024, a Kenai Peninsula man died from borealpox after being infected by what scientists discovered was a genetically different strain of the virus. The patient was in his 60s and had an immune system weakened by treatment for cancer. This year’s two borealpox cases, like the previous Fairbanks-area cases, were mild, Rollins said. “It was the lesion that came to the attention of both patients, and they sought treatment for the lesion,” she said. “One patient did experience respiratory illness, fever, muscle aches, joint aches.” Scientists say borealpox circulates among small mammals . So far, red-backed voles, which are plentiful across most of Alaska, have been identified as a major source. Investigation of cases so far has focused on household pets that appear to have encountered infected voles or other small mammals. Unlike the case with mpox, there is no vaccine for borealpox. But some scientists are working on developing one, and some are investigating the effectiveness of certain antiviral medicines to treat infections. The review, which completed its final database searches on 19 September 2025, found that vaccine effectiveness estimates were largely based on adult populations, with no specific data for these vulnerable groups. It noted significant implementation barriers, including supply limitations, cold-chain and logistical constraints, and vaccine hesitancy driven by safety concerns, especially during pregnancy. The review also highlighted the absence of randomised controlled clinical trials in pregnant women and children, as well as limited geographic representation from low- and middle-income countries. Despite these challenges, the authors identified key facilitators such as international coordination, targeted surveillance, and integration of immunisation into routine maternal and child health services. The review underscores the urgent need for prospective studies in these populations, equitable vaccine access, and tailored risk communication to address hesitancy.

Four mpox cases reported in Stanislaus County Staff reports Turlock Journal Published: Nov 4, 2026, 8:02 AM STANISLAUS COUNTY — Stanislaus County public health officials are urging residents to be aware of mpox symptoms and prevention measures after four recent cases of Clade I mpox were reported in the county. According to the Stanislaus County Health Services Agency, two of the cases have been confirmed as Clade I by the California Department of Public Health, while confirmatory testing is underway for the remaining two cases. Health officials said three of the individuals reported no recent international travel. The travel history of the fourth individual remains under investigation. Historically, Clade I mpox cases in the United States have been linked to international travel. However, public health officials said more cases have been identified among people without recent international travel since August 2026, suggesting the virus may now be spreading within U.S. communities. "While we have identified four cases in our county, we want residents to know that the risk to the general public remains low," said Dr. Thea Papasozomenos, Stanislaus County public health officer. "Being aware of the symptoms, getting vaccinated if at risk of exposure to mpox, and seeking medical care when needed are important ways to protect yourself and prevent the spread of mpox," Papasozomenos said. Mpox is a viral infection that can cause a painful or itchy rash and flu-like symptoms. Symptoms can include a rash or sores that resemble pimples or blisters, fever, chills, swollen lymph nodes, headaches, muscle aches and fatigue. Some people may develop only a few sores without other symptoms. Public health officials said the virus spreads primarily through close personal contact with an infected person, including skin-to-skin contact, intimate or sexual contact, contact with infected sores or bodily fluids, or contaminated items such as bedding, towels and clothing. Officials emphasized that mpox does not spread easily through casual contact, such as passing someone in a store or working in the same office. Most people recover within two to four weeks, although more serious illness can occur. The JYNNEOS vaccine is recommended for people at increased risk of exposure to mpox. The vaccine is administered in two doses approximately four weeks apart and helps protect against infection and severe illness. Residents experiencing an unexplained rash or sores are encouraged to contact a healthcare provider about testing.
