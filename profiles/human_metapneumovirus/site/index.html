Computational prediction of a multi-epitope Human Metapneumovirus vaccine candidate through integrated reverse vaccinology and pan-genomic approaches

Authors: Nazia Fairooz Alam, Moumita Chakrabarty, Tanmoy Debnath, Mohammad Ashik Sheikh, Md. Imtiaz, Sadia Jannat Tauhida, Md. Nazmul Hasan, Md. Mohaimenul Islam Tareq
Original abstract (English)
Human metapneumovirus (HMPV) is a primary cause of global respiratory infections yet no approved vaccine currently exists. This study computationally predicts a multi-epitope vaccine candidate using a diverse dataset of 65 HMPV sequences spanning five continents. Following the screening of lead proteins for antigenicity and virulence, fifteen highly conserved MHC-I, MHC-II and B-cell epitopes were prioritized. These were integrated with a putative L7/L12 adjuvant using optimized AAY, GPGPG, and KK linkers to design three constructs (HMPV_V1–V3). Structural validation identified HMPV-V2 as the lead candidate that exhibits a Z-score of-5.24 and 87.7% of residues in favored Ramachandran regions indicating excellent stereochemical quality and structural stability. In silico docking indicated a strong predicted binding affinity between HMPV-V2 and the TLR4 receptor (energy: −969.2). Immune simulations predicted a robust adaptive response characterized by high IgG1 titers, memory B-cell maturation, and a Th1-dominant cytokine profile. Furthermore, molecular dynamics simulations suggested exceptional structural integrity for HMPV-V2, maintaining a low RMSD of 8.213 and RMSF of 0.737 throughout the simulation. Optimized in silico cloning into the pET28a (+) vector indicated a high potential for protein expression in E. While these findings provide a theoretically grounded blueprint for vaccine development, this study is entirely computational and lacks experimental validation. Further in vitro and in vivo testing is required to confirm the actual safety and immunogenicity of the proposed candidate.

Estimating cumulative incidence from partially missing time series of respiratory viral infections

Authors: Sudhir Venkatesan, Lisa J. White, Hyewon Nina Koo, John Dickerson, Weiming Hu, Jennifer Kuntz, Mark Schmidt, Sylvia Taylor, Carla Talarico
Original abstract (English)
BACKGROUND: Seasonal respiratory viruses, including respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and human metapneumovirus (hMPV), are major contributors to global respiratory infection burden. Estimating viral incidence using real-world data (RWD) is challenging as misalignment of data collection with seasonal circulation can lead to underestimated incidence and hinder inter-study comparisons. METHODS: We developed a regression-based approach to retrospectively correct partial-season time series with contiguous missing weeks at the start or end of a season. Weekly RSV clinical incidence (per 100,000) was extracted from U.S. RWD sources (Optum® CDM and PharMetrics Plus, adults ≥65 years; Kaiser Permanente Northwest [KPNW], all ages) for seasons 2018-19 to 2023-24 and aligned to RSV‐Net hospitalization surveillance. Weekly early- and late-season missingness scenarios were created (approximately 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% of weeks removed). Models were fit within each season using harmonic terms and the surveillance-aligned covariate. Uncertainty was quantified using moving block bootstrap 95% prediction intervals. Weekly accuracy was evaluated using RMSE and normalized RMSE, and seasonal burden reconstruction accuracy using cumulative percent error and signed difference. For hMPV, we evaluated an illustrative extension using Optum® CDM (adults ≥65 years; 2022-23 and 2023-24) and NREVSS test positivity; because positivity is bounded and depends on testing volume, we used a denominator-aware binomial/logit model (positives out of tests). RESULTS: For RSV, weekly prediction error increased with missingness level, while reconstructed seasonal burden generally remained close to observed totals in applicable scenarios. Early-season gaps were associated with larger cumulative errors than late-season gaps in Optum® CDM and PharMetrics Plus, whereas late-season median percent errors were closer to zero across missingness levels. Applicability was reduced primarily for higher early-season missingness in KPNW due to peak overlap (e.g., early 25% scenarios not applicable). For hMPV test positivity (illustrative; two seasons), weekly prediction RMSE was on the order of ~1-2 percentage points across missingness scenarios. CONCLUSION: Across multiple RSV seasons and data sources, reconstructed seasonal burden generally agreed with observed totals when missingness was confined to contiguous early- or late-season weeks and did not overlap peak activity. This approach provides a transparent method to improve the utility and comparability of partial-season respiratory virus time series. However, applicability is reduced when peak weeks are missing, and hMPV findings are illustrative given limited seasons and the use of test positivity.

Human Metapneumovirus Infection in an Elderly Patient Receiving Maintenance Hemodialysis with Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD): A Case Report

Authors: Antoine Zgheib
Original abstract (English)
Human metapneumovirus (hMPV) is an important cause of acute infections of the respiratory system affecting young children, older adults, and immunocompromised individuals. Patients suffering from chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) receiving maintenance hemodialysis represent a particularly vulnerable population, yet published reports describing hMPV infection in dialysis patients remain limited. A 70-year-old male with a background of COPD, hypertension, and diabetic nephropathy with a maintenance hemodialysis regimen presented with a four-day history of fever, cough with rhinorrhea, myalgias, and dyspnea. He was immunized against influenza, COVID-19, and RSV. Physical examination showed signs of mild hypoxemia with diffuse crackles and expiratory wheezing, without evidence of consolidation. The patient’s chest radiograph showed chronic obstructive changes. Triplex RT-PCR for influenza virus, RSV, and SARS-CoV-2 was negative. However, a multiplex RT-PCR respiratory panel identified hMPV. He had a good response to supportive care with oxygen supplementation, bronchodilator therapy, and continuation of his scheduled hemodialysis regimen, with subsequent discharge after five days. The above-mentioned case shows how crucial it is to consider the possible hMPV infection in differentiating the cause of respiratory complaints in dialysis patients. This infection, even being a relatively mild one, can have serious consequences from the point of view of infection control in dialysis units due to close contact between patients and their frequent use of common medical devices. This observation highlights the clinical significance of hMPV infections in hemodialysis patients.

Original abstract (English)
Acute respiratory infections (ARI) remain a major public health problem in Myanmar, a tropical Southeast Asian country, yet comprehensive molecular surveillance data are limited. We conducted outpatient surveillance during the 2024 rainy season and characterized pathogen distribution, seasonality, and viral genetic diversity. From June–November 2024, ARI outpatients were enrolled at two Yangon hospitals. Nasopharyngeal swabs were screened by rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) for influenza, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and SARS-CoV-2. RDT-positive specimens underwent confirmation by real-time PCR (RT-PCR). RDT-negative specimens were tested using the BioFire ® FilmArray ® Respiratory 2.1 Panel (BioFire RP). Influenza A(H1N1)pdm09, A(H3N2), and B/Victoria viruses with cycle threshold (Ct) < 32 underwent next-generation sequencing for hemagglutinin (HA) phylogenetics. Human metapneumovirus (hMPV)-positive specimens were sequenced targeting the partial F-gene for genotyping. Molecular Etiology and Mortality Risk Stratification of Non-SARS-CoV-2 Severe Acute Respiratory Infection in a Predominantly Pediatric Cohort: A Retrospective Study From a Tertiary Care Hospital in North India - Cureus

News Brief
This retrospective study from a tertiary care hospital in North India analyzed non-SARS-CoV-2 severe acute respiratory infections (SARI) in 117 hospitalized patients, including 103 children and 14 adults, admitted between September and December 2021. The study aimed to evaluate the etiology of SARI and the prognostic value of the ViVI Disease Severity Score (ViVI Score) in a predominantly pediatric cohort. Using multiplex PCR and conventional microbiological culture, respiratory pathogens were identified, with human respiratory syncytial virus (HRSV) A/B being predominant. The study highlights the importance of accurate pathogen identification and early risk stratification to improve patient outcomes and reduce unnecessary antibiotic use, especially in resource-limited settings like India. The findings suggest that integrating rapid molecular diagnostics with standardized severity assessment tools could enhance clinical decision-making and respiratory disease surveillance.
Original source text (English)
Abstract Background: Severe acute respiratory infections (SARI) remain a major cause of hospitalization and mortality, particularly among children and other vulnerable populations. Early identification of pathogens and disease severity is essential for clinical decision-making. This study evaluated the etiology of non-SARS-CoV-2 SARI and the prognostic utility of the ViVI Disease Severity Score (ViVI Score) in a predominantly pediatric cohort. Methods: In this retrospective study, 117 hospitalized SARS-CoV-2-negative SARI patients, including 103 children and 14 adults, admitted to a tertiary care hospital in North India between September and December 2021 were evaluated. Respiratory pathogens were identified using multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR), alongside blood and lower respiratory tract cultures. Serum C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), and D-dimer were measured. Mortality prediction was assessed using the ViVI Score and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Results: The median age was 2.0 years (interquartile range, 0.5-7.5 years). Multiplex PCR detected respiratory pathogens in 69.2% of patients, with human respiratory syncytial virus (HRSV) A/B predominating. Blood cultures were positive in 40/117 patients (34.2%), predominantly yielding methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus. Overall mortality was 20.5%, increasing to 60% among patients with culture-confirmed sepsis. Elevated CRP and PCT were observed in all septic patients. The ViVI Score showed excellent mortality prediction (area under the curve (AUC) 0.946). A cutoff of ≥30 yielded 83.3% sensitivity and 91.4% specificity and was strongly associated with mortality (OR 53.1, 95% CI 14.5-194.0; p<0.001). Conclusion: HRSV was the predominant respiratory pathogen, whereas bacterial sepsis was the principal determinant of mortality. Multiplex molecular diagnostics combined with standardized severity assessment may improve clinical triage, antimicrobial stewardship, and respiratory pathogen surveillance in resource-limited settings. Introduction Severe acute respiratory infections (SARI) pose a significant global health challenge, causing substantial morbidity and mortality, particularly among vulnerable populations. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines SARI as an acute respiratory illness with a history of fever or measured fever of ≥38°C and cough, with onset within the last 10 days, requiring hospitalization [1]. However, this syndromic definition does not provide information regarding the underlying etiological agent or the individual risk of adverse outcomes. SARI may result from a wide range of viral and bacterial pathogens, and conventional diagnostic methods may not always provide a timely or comprehensive etiological diagnosis. Before the COVID-19 pandemic, SARI detection was largely syndromic, relying on clinical presentation and limited diagnostic capacity [2]. The pandemic acted as a catalyst for strengthening surveillance systems and pathogen identification. In India, surveillance of epidemic-prone diseases is supported through the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme, including laboratory-based and information technology-enabled surveillance [3]. SARI constitutes a major cause of healthcare utilization and hospitalization and may require referral to secondary and tertiary healthcare facilities, particularly among young children, older adults, and individuals with underlying comorbidities. Family physicians are often the first point of contact for patients presenting with acute respiratory symptoms and therefore play a pivotal role in early recognition of severe disease, timely referral, initiation of appropriate management, and implementation of infection prevention measures [1,2]. Accurate identification of respiratory pathogens and early risk stratification are essential to improve patient outcomes while minimizing unnecessary antibiotic use [2]. In low- and middle-income countries such as India, access to specialized diagnostic services remains limited at primary-care facilities, with sample transport and referral mechanisms used to improve access to diagnostic testing [4]. Consequently, evidence on the etiological profile of SARI and validated clinical severity assessment tools is highly relevant for strengthening referral pathways, antimicrobial stewardship, and respiratory disease surveillance. Such evidence can assist family physicians and primary care providers in identifying patients requiring urgent referral, promoting rational antimicrobial use, and optimizing utilization of healthcare resources [2,4]. To standardize clinical assessment, various severity scoring systems have been introduced. One of the most widely recognized is the ViVI Disease Severity Score (ViVI Score), developed by the Vienna Vaccine Safety Initiative. This 22-item composite score quantifies the clinical severity of influenza-like illnesses and SARI across diverse patient populations [5]. It includes indicators of both uncomplicated and complicated disease, providing a standardized framework for clinical decision-making, disease severity assessment, and epidemiological comparison. The advent of multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) platforms has markedly advanced the diagnostic approach to SARI by enabling simultaneous detection of multiple respiratory pathogens from a single clinical specimen, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy and expediting clinical decision-making [6]. Integration of rapid molecular diagnostics with standardized severity assessment has the potential to improve clinical risk stratification while strengthening pathogen-specific surveillance and facilitating the integration of virological and clinical data for surveillance and public health applications [6,7]. Despite advances in molecular diagnostics and standardized severity assessment, evidence integrating pathogen identification with clinical risk stratification in non-SARS-CoV-2 SARI remains limited, particularly from resource-constrained settings. Addressing this gap may help strengthen clinical decision-making, antimicrobial stewardship, and respiratory infection surveillance across primary and tertiary healthcare settings. Materials & Methods Study design and setting This retrospective observational study analyzed laboratory and clinical data of patients admitted with severe acute respiratory syndrome during the defined period from September to December 2021, corresponding to the COVID-19 pandemic, at a tertiary care teaching hospital in North India. The data analysis was undertaken subsequently in the post-pandemic phase. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Vardhman Mahavir Medical College and Safdarjung Hospital, New Delhi, India (Approval Ref No. IEC/VMMC/SJH/Cert./05-2024/54, dated 23/07/2024). The primary objective was to establish an etiological profile of pathogens responsible for SARI other than SARS-CoV-2 among hospitalized patients, using a combination of multiplex PCR and conventional microbiological culture. Clinical and laboratory data were retrieved retrospectively from hospital records. The study population was predominantly pediatric, comprising 103 children and 14 adults. Sampling technique and patient selection Consecutive patients admitted during the study period with signs and symptoms of acute respiratory illness were screened for inclusion. Patients meeting the WHO SARI case definition, with fever ≥38°C and cough, with symptom onset within the last 10 days, requiring hospitalization, were included in the study [1]. The study cohort comprised predominantly pediatric patients, with 103 children and 14 adults. Pathogen detection and laboratory methods RT-PCR for SARS-CoV-2 Nasopharyngeal and/or oropharyngeal swabs were collected from all SARI patients in the hospital during that period and tested for SARS-CoV-2 RT-PCR. Patients who tested negative for SARS-CoV-2 were included in this study and were subjected to multiplex PCR. Testing was performed in a designated COVID-19 molecular diagnostic laboratory, following Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) guidelines [8] and using ICMR-National Institute of Virology-approved commercial RT-PCR kits. The test targeted specific viral genes (e.g., E, ORF) for detection of SARS-CoV-2 RNA. Multiplex PCR for Respiratory Pathogens Respiratory specimens (nasopharyngeal/throat swabs) were further subjected to multiplex PCR using the Fast-Track Diagnostics Respiratory Pathogens 21 Plus (Fast-Track Diagnostics, Junglinster, Luxembourg). This assay detects Chlamydia pneumoniae, Enterovirus, Haemophilus influenzae B, Human adenovirus, Human bocavirus, Human coronavirus 229E, Human coronavirus HKU1, Human coronavirus NL63, Human coronavirus OC43, Human metapneumoviruses A/B, Human parainfluenza virus 1-4, Human respiratory syncytial virus (HRSV) A/B, Human rhinovirus, Influenza A virus, Influenza A (H1N1) virus, Influenza B virus, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, and Streptococcus pneumoniae. Tests were conducted according to the manufacturer's protocol, including appropriate internal and external controls. Conventional Microbiological Culture Blood cultures were performed in all 117 enrolled patients using the BacT/ALERT® automated system (bioMérieux, France). Samples that flagged positive were subcultured on blood and MacConkey agar plates, followed by identification using standard microbiological techniques [9,10]. Patients with positive blood cultures were clinically evaluated and classified as having sepsis based on the treating clinical team's assessment and documented clinical findings. All 40 patients with positive blood cultures were clinically diagnosed with sepsis. The lower respiratory tract samples were processed using standard microbiological techniques [9,10]. Isolates were identified by morphology, Gram staining, and biochemical profiling. Biomarkers Serum C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), and D-dimer were measured on the Mispa i3 automated cartridge-based specific protein analyser (Agappe Diagnostics Ltd., Kerala, India). The platform uses a dual-channel system (rate-based nephelometry and turbidimetry/photometry) and prefilled single-use reagent cartridges with test-specific IC-card (smart card) calibration. Disease severity assessment The ViVI Score was used to assess disease severity based on 22 clinical parameters, with a total score ranging from 0 to 48 [11,12]. The scoring was weighted as follows: Disease Severity with Uncomplicated Symptoms (DSU): Weight=1×; Disease Severity with Complicated Symptoms (DSC): Weight=3×; Total Disease Severity Score=DSU + DSC. A schematic representation of the study methodology is shown in Figure 1. Statistical analysis Pathogen distributions and descriptive statistics (median, IQR) were calculated in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, United States), and odds ratios were estimated using Fisher’s exact test. Diagnostic performance of the ViVI Score for outcome classification was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis of ordinal rating data in JROCFIT v2.0.1 (John Eng; based on ROCFIT by Metz et al.) [13]: outcomes were coded as positive = survival (alive) and negative = death, ViVI scores were converted into an ordinal rating scale in which higher categories represented stronger evidence of the positive state, and a maximum-likelihood unequal-variance binormal ROC model was fitted to estimate the area under the curve (AUC) with its standard deviation; because ViVI is a severity index (higher scores indicate poorer prognosis), the ratings were oriented such that lower ViVI scores corresponded to stronger evidence of survival. Results A total of 117 patients were included in the study. The cohort was predominantly pediatric, comprising 103 children and 14 adults. The median age of the study population was 2.0 years (IQR 0.5-7.5 years). The male-to-female ratio was 1.81:1, indicating a male predominance. Pathogen detection profile from the multiplex PCR Out of the 117 patients, 81 (69.2%) were positive for at least one respiratory pathogen using multiplex PCR. Multiplex respiratory pathogen testing identified HRSV A/B as the most frequently detected pathogen (n=60), followed by Streptococcus pneumoniae (n=16), Staphylococcus aureus (n=11), human rhinovirus (n=8), and influenza B virus (n=7). Lower detection rates were observed for human parainfluenza virus type 4 (n=3), human adenovirus (n=2), human coronavirus OC43, human metapneumovirus A/B, human parainfluenza virus type 3, and human parechovirus (each n=1). No cases were detected for Chlamydophila pneumoniae, enterovirus, Haemophilus influenzae, human bocavirus, human coronaviruses 229E, HKU1, and NL63, human parainfluenza virus types 1 and 2, influenza A virus (including H1N1), or Mycoplasma pneumoniae. Conversely, 36 patients tested negative for all pathogens, indicating either non-infectious etiologies or undetected organisms outside the panel’s scope (Figure 2). Additionally, six cases of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) due to HRSV were documented in a subset of adult patients, along with HRSV positivity among 13 children with underlying hematological disorders. Pathogen detection profile from conventional cultures Blood cultures were positive in 40/117 patients (34.2%); five were adults, and 35 were children. Among the positive blood cultures, 18 yielded methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus, seven Escherichia coli, seven Klebsiella pneumoniae, seven Streptococcus pneumoniae, and one yielded both S. aureus and S. pneumoniae. Lower respiratory tract aerobic cultures were positive in 48 patients (five adults and 43 children), yielding S. pneumoniae in 15, E. coli in 12, confluent growth of K. pneumoniae in 10, and S. aureus in 10; one patient had both S. pneumoniae and S. aureus. Biomarker profile Elevated PCT (>0.5 ng/mL) was observed in 55/117 patients (47.0%), including all 40 patients with positive blood cultures. Markedly elevated CRP (>10 mg/dL) was observed in 101/117 patients (86.3%), including all 40 patients with positive blood cultures. Positive D-dimer, suggestive of a hypercoagulable state, was observed in 35 patients, of whom 30 had sepsis. ViVI Score distribution The median ViVI Score across all patients was 22. Distribution of ViVI scores varied based on pathogen and infection type. Among single infections, HRSV A/B was most frequent (n=38) with a median ViVI score of 20.5 (IQR 13.5). Other single pathogens were less common, including Staphylococcus aureus (n=5; median 20 (IQR 19)), Influenza B (n=4; median 20.5 (IQR 10.25)), Streptococcus pneumoniae (n=3; median 29 (IQR 6.5)), Human rhinovirus (n=2; median 35 (IQR 6)), and individual cases of adenovirus (median 37) and parainfluenza virus 4 (median 42). Among coinfections, HRSV + Streptococcus pneumoniae was the most common (n=10; median 21 (IQR 13.75)). Other HRSV coinfections included HRSV + rhinovirus (n=3; median 20 (IQR 4)), HRSV + Influenza B (n=2; median 32 (IQR 16)), and HRSV + Staphylococcus aureus (n=2; median 20 (IQR 3)), while the remaining coinfections occurred as single cases with variable ViVI scores (Table 1). Clinical outcomes and mortality The overall mortality rate in the cohort was 20.5% (24/117). Of particular concern was the subgroup of patients presenting with SARI and culture-confirmed sepsis (n=40), in which mortality rose dramatically to 60% (24/40). This suggests that progression to sepsis remains the strongest indicator of poor outcomes in SARI cases. Predictive utility of the ViVI Score A total of 117 cases were analyzed (93 survivors and 24 deaths). ROC analysis using JROCFIT demonstrated excellent discrimination between survivors and non-survivors (fitted AUC 0.946 (SD 0.022); empiric AUC 0.942) (Figure 3). The ROC curve lies close to the upper-left corner, indicating excellent discriminatory performance of the ViVI Score for predicting mortality across a range of decision thresholds (Figure 3). Using the ViVI Score as a mortality predictor and classifying death as the event, a low cutoff of ViVI ≥15 yielded 100% sensitivity for death (24/24) but low specificity (29.0%, 27/93) (TP=24, FN=0, TN=27, FP=66). In contrast, a high cutoff of ViVI ≥45 yielded 100% specificity (93/93) but low sensitivity (25.0%, 6/24) (TP=6, FN=18, TN=93, FP=0). The optimal threshold by Youden's J was ViVI ≥30, which provided balanced performance (sensitivity 83.3% (20/24) and specificity 91.4% (85/93); overall accuracy 89.7% (105/117)) (TP=20, FN=4, TN=85, FP=8). Patients with ViVI ≥30 had markedly increased odds of death compared with those with ViVI <30 (odds ratio 53.1, 95% CI 14.5-194.0; Fisher's exact test p=1.4 × 10−12). Discussion The present study provides a comprehensive etiological and clinical profile of non-SARS-CoV-2 SARI in a predominantly pediatric cohort, with simultaneous assessment of respiratory pathogens, bacterial culture results, inflammatory biomarkers, and disease severity. Multiplex PCR detected at least one respiratory pathogen in 69.2% of patients, while HRSV A/B was the predominant respiratory pathogen. Culture-confirmed bloodstream infection was associated with substantially higher mortality, and the ViVI Score demonstrated excellent discrimination for mortality. The 69.2% pathogen detection rate observed with multiplex PCR is consistent with previous studies demonstrating substantial pathogen detection among patients with severe respiratory infection. El Kholy et al. reported a high rate of viral detection and frequent co-detection in children with SARI, while Contier et al. demonstrated the diagnostic performance of multiplex PCR among critically ill immunocompromised patients [14,15]. Differences in age distribution, disease severity, sampling practices, seasonality, and the respiratory pathogen panels used may account for variations in diagnostic yield between studies.
