Viral etiology and predictors of hospitalization in adults younger than 65 years with laboratory-confirmed respiratory viral infections in the pre-COVID era

Authors: Benjamin Davido, Djillali Annane, Michelle Saliba, Martin Rottman, Christel Mamona, Paul Loubet, Benoit Lemarié
Original abstract (English)
Respiratory viral tract infections (VRTIs) are generally considered more severe in older adults, while their clinical impact in younger adults remains less well characterized. We conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study of adults aged 18–64 years with RT-PCR-confirmed influenza, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), human metapneumovirus (hMPV), or parainfluenza virus (PIV) infection during the 2016–2018 respiratory seasons. Among 172 patients, 110 had influenza, 31 RSV, 17 hMPV, and 14 PIV infections. Hospitalization occurred in 64.0% of patients overall, with rates of 53.6% for influenza, 77.4% for RSV, and 87.1% for hMPV/PIV infections. Chronic respiratory disease, neutrophilia, and hMPV/PIV infection were independently associated with hospitalization. Chronic respiratory disease and previous respiratory tract infection were independently associated with lower odds of short-term favorable outcome, whereas detectable eosinophils were associated with improved recovery. Ninety-day mortality was low across viral groups. Age was not independently associated with hospitalization or short-term recovery within this restricted age range. These findings highlight substantial morbidity associated with VRTIs in adults younger than 65 years and underscore the need for greater attention to this population, which remains less well characterized than older adults.

Variability across viral subgroups, particularly in the attachment (G) protein, can complicate universal design. Original abstract (English)
Introduction: Human Metapneumovirus is a significant respiratory pathogen causing infections across all age groups, particularly in young children, immunocompromised individuals, and the elderly. Despite its clinical relevance, there are currently no approved vaccines or specific antiviral treatments for Human Metapneumovirus, necessitating further research into therapeutic and preventive strategies. Methods: A systematic literature review was conducted using peer-reviewed journals, clinical trials, and recent studies focusing on Human Metapneumovirus infection. Data were extracted and analyzed to evaluate the effectiveness of antiviral therapies, monoclonal antibodies, immunomodulators, and vaccine candidates. Results: Studies indicate that Human Metapneumovirus primarily targets the respiratory epithelium, triggering immune responses that contribute to both viral clearance and disease pathogenesis. Investigational therapies, including fusion inhibitors and protease inhibitors, show promise but require further validation. Immunomodulatory agents and herbal remedies may offer supportive benefits, but their efficacy against Human Metapneumovirus remains unproven. Advances in vaccine development are ongoing, with several candidates in preclinical and early clinical stages. Discussion: HMPV continues to pose a substantial public health burden due to its seasonal outbreaks, clinical similarity to other respiratory viruses, and absence of specific therapies. The virus evades immune responses via multiple mechanisms, contributing to disease severity, particularly in vulnerable populations. Although supportive care is currently the mainstay, emerging antivirals and vaccines may offer future solutions. Improved diagnostic access, public awareness, and targeted research are essential for controlling HMPV infections. Conclusion: Human Metapneumovirus continues to pose a public health challenge due to the lack of targeted treatments and vaccines. Ongoing research into antiviral agents, monoclonal antibodies, and vaccines is critical for effective disease management. Further clinical trials are needed to establish safe and effective therapeutic options.

Human metapneumovirus pneumonia hits hardest in adults aged 80 and over - Bioengineer.org

News Brief
A study published on October 27, 2026, in BMC Infectious Diseases found that adults aged 80 years and older who develop pneumonia from human metapneumovirus (hMPV) face significantly higher hospitalization rates and more severe clinical and radiologic features compared to younger adults. The research, conducted at Ewha Womans University Seoul Hospital, analyzed 106 patients with hMPV infection and pneumonia diagnosed between September 2022 and March 2025. The older group (27 patients, 25.5% of the cohort) had a hospitalization rate of 96.3%, compared to 77.2% in the younger group (79 patients). Additionally, older patients showed more extensive disease, with 92.6% having multilobar involvement versus 74.7% in younger patients, and 22.2% showing pleural effusion versus 3.8%. Hypertension was the most common comorbidity, affecting 43.4% of patients, followed by malignancy and diabetes mellitus. The study highlights the substantial clinical burden of hMPV pneumonia in older adults and the importance of careful assessment of disease severity at presentation. However, the study is a retrospective single-center analysis, which only describes associations rather than causal effects. The ICU admission rate was numerically higher in the older group but did not reach statistical significance.

Human metapneumovirus is a significant viral pathogen associated with acute lower respiratory tract infections, particularly in elderly people, yet data on the clinical characteristics and outcomes of hMPV pneumonia among elderly people remain sparse. The researchers, led by Hyeon Hwa Kim and Yon Ju Ryu of Ewha Womans University Seoul Hospital, reviewed records from 106 adult patients with polymerase chain reaction-confirmed hMPV infection and radiologically confirmed pneumonia diagnosed at their hospital between September 2022 and March 2025. The cohort had a median age of 71 years, and 37.8% of the patients were male. Hypertension was the most common comorbidity, present in 43.4% of patients, followed by malignancy and diabetes mellitus, each affecting 24.5%. To examine how age shaped the disease course, the patients were divided into two groups: those younger than 80 years, who numbered 79 and made up 74.5% of the cohort, and those aged 80 years or older, who numbered 27 and made up 25.5%. The age-group comparisons revealed several significant differences. Patients aged 80 and older had a higher prevalence of hypertension, at 63.0% versus 36.7% in the younger group, and of dementia, at 22.2% versus 5.1%. Their chest imaging also showed more extensive disease, with multilobar involvement in 92.6% versus 74.7% and pleural effusion in 22.2% versus 3.8%. These differences in hypertension, dementia, multilobar involvement, and pleural effusion were all statistically significant. Co-infection was a notable feature of the cohort. Additional microorganisms were detected in 32.1% of patients, and bacterial organisms alone were more common than viral organisms alone, at 24.5% versus 5.7%. Haemophilus influenzae and Klebsiella pneumoniae were the most frequently detected bacteria among the co-pathogens. The overall hospitalization rate was 82.1%, and it was significantly higher among patients aged 80 and older, at 96.3% versus 77.2% in the younger group. Intensive care unit admission rates were numerically higher in the older group, at 25.9% versus 10.1%, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. Within the hospitalized subgroup, the researchers performed a multivariable analysis to identify factors linked to length of stay. Greater initial oxygen requirement was associated with longer hospitalization, indicating that the degree of respiratory support needed at presentation tracked with how long patients remained in hospital. The authors conclude that adults aged 80 years and older with hMPV pneumonia had higher hospitalization rates and more severe clinical and radiologic features in the age-group comparisons, and they highlight the substantial clinical burden of hMPV pneumonia in older adults and the importance of careful assessment of disease severity at presentation. As a retrospective analysis of patients at a single hospital, the study describes associations observed in one cohort rather than causal effects, and the ICU admission finding did not reach statistical significance. The findings nonetheless add to the limited evidence base on hMPV pneumonia in the very old, a group in which the virus appears to impose a heavier clinical burden. News Brief
A study conducted in Guangzhou, China, from July 2023 to January 2024 found that co-infections with viruses and bacteria, such as human parainfluenza virus and influenza virus, were linked to more severe clinical outcomes in children and adolescents with Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection. The research, published on October 26, 2026, analyzed 1,499 patients, with a mean age of 6.39 years and 50.7% male. Of these, 68% were hospitalized, and 62% of hospitalized patients had severe infections. The study noted that severe disease was more common in the youngest age group (0–5 years). Co-infections with human parainfluenza virus and influenza virus were detected in 7.5% and 7.2% of participants, respectively. However, the study did not establish causality, and its retrospective design limits the generalizability of the findings. The authors emphasized the need for age-adapted clinical management strategies for Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection, particularly in cases of specific co-infection patterns.

The study included 1,499 patients treated in Guangzhou, China, from July 2023 through January 2024. Participants had a mean age of 6.39 years, with a standard deviation of 3.49 years, and 50.7% were male. Most were hospitalized: 1,020 of 1,499 patients, or 68%. Among the hospitalized group, 630 patients, or 62%, had severe infection according to the study’s criteria. Severe disease was particularly associated with the youngest age group, aged 0–5 years. Human parainfluenza virus was the most common pathogen detected alongside M. pneumoniae, occurring in 7.5% of participants, followed by influenza virus in 7.2%. The temporal distributions of these co-infections differed. M. pneumoniae–human parainfluenza virus co-infection predominated from early July to late September 2023 and subsequently decreased, whereas M. pneumoniae–influenza virus co-infection remained common throughout the seven-month study period. These descriptive patterns show that the respiratory pathogens recorded alongside M. pneumoniae varied over time in the Guangzhou cohort. The retrospective observations do not establish the reasons for those changes. Patients entered the multivariable analyses if they had completed testing for all eight viral and five bacterial pathogens assessed in the study. The researchers used multivariable logistic regression to examine co-infection patterns in relation to severe clinical outcomes and negative binomial regression to assess hospital stay and fever duration. Hospitalized patients had higher overall rates of both viral and bacterial co-infection with M. pneumoniae than outpatients. The analysis was conducted at one center, and the retrospective design limits how broadly the findings can be generalized beyond the population and clinical setting studied in Guangzhou.
