多胺耗竭抑制小鼠和人类诺如病毒感染

Polyamine depletion inhibits murine and human norovirus infection
作者： Maryna Chaika, Heike Laschin, Marina Pekelis, Alexander Müller, Carmen Mirabelli, Sandra Niendorf, Christiane E. Wobus, Stefan Taube
摘要中文翻译未标注:诺如病毒是Caliciviridae科中的无包膜正链RNA病毒。人类诺如病毒(HNoVs)是病毒性胃肠疾病的主要病因。尽管其具有重要的临床意义,但针对诺如病毒感染的治疗策略仍然有限,部分原因是HNoV培养的困难以及对病毒-宿主相互作用的理解不足。多胺(PAs)是源自氨基酸代谢的小分子多阳离子代谢物,对多种细胞功能至关重要,并且是许多RNA病毒的重要宿主因子。然而,其在诺如病毒感染中的作用仍不清楚。在这里,我们证明PAs对HNoV和小鼠诺如病毒(MNV)的有产感染至关重要,突显了跨物种的保守且功能重要的依赖性。在MNV感染的细胞中进行的功能分析显示,多胺耗竭导致主要衣壳蛋白合成显著减少,并在第一次复制周期后病毒滴度降低,这与病毒诱导的细胞病变效应(CPE)的丧失同时发生。在多胺耗竭的细胞中,通过外源性补充精胺或精脒(多胺生物合成途径的代谢物)、胆固醇或PI3-激酶抑制剂可以恢复病毒滴度和CPE。除了MNV之外,多胺耗竭完全消除了人类肠道类器官中的HNoV感染,这种效应在添加外源性多胺后部分恢复。这些发现识别出多胺作为新型宿主依赖因子,促进诺如病毒感染,并强调多胺代谢作为针对HNoV感染的潜在治疗靶点。重要性:人类诺如病毒(HNoVs)是全球急性病毒性胃肠炎的主要病因,但由于缺乏小型动物模型和有效的体外系统来传播病毒,开发有效的抗病毒药物和治疗手段受到了阻碍。小鼠诺如病毒(MNV)与HNoV共享关键特征,并可作为研究诺如病毒生物学的有价值的替代模型。在这项研究中,我们确定了源自宿主的多胺(PAs)作为HNoV和MNV有产感染所必需的保守代谢物。多胺对诺如病毒感染具有多方面的影响,特别是在促进病毒诱导的细胞凋亡和促进病毒颗粒释放方面。值得注意的是,使用多胺合成的已知抑制剂二氟甲基鸟氨酸(DFMO)进行多胺耗竭可有效抑制MNV和HNoV感染。因此,我们的结果为诺如病毒-宿主相互作用提供了新的见解,并突出了针对宿主的治疗策略在限制诺如病毒感染方面的潜力。此外,我们的发现为进一步研究DFMO,一种已获许可且耐受性良好的药物,作为免疫功能低下患者慢性诺如病毒感染的潜在治疗选项提供了依据。

Polyamine depletion inhibits murine and human norovirus infection

Authors: Maryna Chaika, Heike Laschin, Marina Pekelis, Alexander Müller, Carmen Mirabelli, Sandra Niendorf, Christiane E. Wobus, Stefan Taube
Original abstract (English)
UNLABELLED: Noroviruses are non-enveloped, positive-sense RNA viruses in the family Caliciviridae. Human noroviruses (HNoVs) are the leading cause of viral gastrointestinal disease. Despite their clinical relevance, therapeutic strategies for norovirus infections remain limited, partly due to challenges in cultivating HNoV and limited understanding of virus-host interactions. Polyamines (PAs) are small polycationic metabolites derived from amino acid metabolism that are essential for diverse cellular functions and serve as important host factors for many RNA viruses. However, their role in norovirus infection has remained elusive. Here, we demonstrate that PAs are critical for productive infection for both HNoV and murine norovirus (MNV), underscoring a conserved and functionally significant dependency across species. Functional analysis in MNV-infected cells showed that PA depletion resulted in a marked reduction in major capsid protein synthesis and reduced viral titers following the first replication cycle, coinciding with a loss of virus-induced cytopathic effect (CPE). In PA-depleted cells, viral titers and CPE could be restored by exogenous supplementation with spermine or spermidine (metabolites of the PA biosynthetic pathway), cholesterol, or a PI3-kinase inhibitor. In addition to MNV, depletion of PAs completely ablated HNoV infection in human intestinal enteroids, which was also partially rescued by adding exogenous PAs. Collectively, these findings identify PAs as a novel host dependency factor that facilitates norovirus infection and highlight PA metabolism as a potential therapeutic target for antiviral intervention against HNoV. IMPORTANCE: Human noroviruses (HNoVs) are a major cause of acute viral gastroenteritis worldwide, yet the development of effective antivirals and vaccines has been hampered by the lack of small-animal models and robust in vitro systems to propagate the virus. Murine norovirus (MNV) shares key features with HNoV and serves as a valuable surrogate for studying norovirus biology. In this study, we identify host-derived polyamines (PAs) as essential and conserved metabolites required for productive infection for both HNoV and MNV. PAs exert multifactorial effects on norovirus infection, particularly by facilitating virus-induced apoptosis and promoting virion release in MNV-infected cells. Notably, PA depletion using difluoromethylornithine (DFMO), a well-established inhibitor of PA synthesis, effectively suppressed MNV and HNoV infection. Therefore, our results provide new insights into norovirus-host interactions and highlight the potential of a host-targeting antiviral approach to limit norovirus infection. Furthermore, our findings provide a rationale for further investigating DFMO, a licensed and well-tolerated drug, as a potential therapeutic option for chronic norovirus infections in immunocompromised patients.

Human norovirus infection does not induce serotonin secretion in human intestinal organoids

Authors: Hephzibah Nwanosike, Vijayalakshmi B. Ayyar, Michael J. Bolt, Aaya Boussattach, Yi-Ting Shen, Sarah Elizabeth Blutt, Mary Kolb Estes, Robert L. Atmar, Sasirekha Ramani
Original abstract (English)
ABSTRACT Human noroviruses (HuNoVs) are a leading cause of acute gastroenteritis worldwide. The primary symptoms of HuNoV-associated disease are vomiting and diarrhea; yet the mechanisms underlying these symptoms are poorly understood. Neuroactive hormones such as serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine; 5-HT) secreted by intestinal enteroendocrine cells (EECs) can activate vomiting centers in the brain and play a critical role in regulating emetic signaling. Serotonin release from infected EECs has been implicated in vomiting during other enteric infections. Recent studies have reported the detection of HuNoV RNA and antigen in EECs in jejunal biopsies from immunocompromised patients with chronic HuNoV infection and rhesus macaques. We therefore investigated whether HuNoV infection of human intestinal organoids (HIOs) induces serotonin secretion. We examined infection and serotonin secretion with multiple genogroup II HuNoV strains using adult and infant jejunal HIO models, including a doxycycline-inducible neurogenin-3 HIO line with increased numbers of EECs. HuNoV infection did not induce serotonin secretion in either adult or infant HIOs as measured by ELISA. Consistent with enterocytes being the primary target cell type for HuNoVs, GII.4 HuNoV replication was robust in parental HIOs but reduced in EEC-enriched cultures. Furthermore, supernatants from parental HIOs failed to stimulate serotonin release from EEC-enriched HIOs. Together, these findings demonstrate that HuNoV infection of intestinal epithelial cells is insufficient to trigger serotonin release in HIOs. These results suggest that HuNoV-associated vomiting is unlikely to result from direct epithelial serotonin secretion and may require additional cellular components such as immune or neuronal signaling pathways or may involve other neuroactive hormones. IMPORTANCE Vomiting is a hallmark symptom of human norovirus disease. Yet, the biological mechanisms responsible for this response are unknown. Infection of enteroendocrine cells and secretion of serotonin play key roles in the emetic response to other gastrointestinal viruses such as rotavirus. Since recent studies have detected human norovirus antigens and RNA in enteroendocrine cells, we hypothesized that human norovirus infection could stimulate serotonin secretion. We used jejunal human intestinal organoids from adults and infants, including organoids enriched for enteroendocrine cells, and found that infection with several clinically important strains did not induce serotonin secretion, even under conditions supporting robust viral replication. These data indicate that serotonin release from intestinal epithelial cells alone is unlikely to explain norovirus-induced vomiting and highlights the need to investigate alternative mechanisms, including other neuroactive hormones, and neuronal signaling pathways to better understand the pathogenesis of norovirus-associated vomiting.

Detection of IgG responses in serological surveys could enable new insights into enteric pathogen transmission, but benchmarking serological measures relative to stool-based measures of infection remains a critical evidence gap. We compared measures of disease transmission in longitudinal birth cohort samples using multiplex IgG (1,601 dried blood spots, 370 children) and PCR assays (2,231 stool samples, 370 children) that overlapped for nine pathogens (norovirus GI, GII, Shigella/enteroinvasive Escherichia coli (EIEC), Campylobacter spp., enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (LT-ETEC), Salmonella enterica, Giardia spp., Cryptosporidium spp., Entamoeba histolytica). LT-ETEC and Campylobacter ranked highest while E. enterica ranked lowest by both IgG and PCR measures of infection. Measures of infection were less aligned for norovirus GI, norovirus GII, Cryptosporidium, Shigella/EIEC, and Giardia. Despite this, for most pathogens, and across both measures, force of infection was lowest in the urban city of Esmeraldas and substantially higher in more rural populations, with relative risks in assay measures (seroconversion rate and PCR detected prevalence) over 2.0 for pathogens with the largest differences (Shigella/EIEC and norovirus GI). Together, our results suggest that although absolute levels of infection differed between assays, IgG and PCR captured consistent relative risk of infection across geographic strata, suggesting either method can identify high- versus low-transmission settings.

Scope and question
The review focuses on human noroviruses, specifically Norovirus GI and GII, and their role in causing gastroenteritis in children. The central question is how to measure the force of infection of these viruses using antibody responses in serological surveys, with a comparison to stool-based molecular testing.
Evidence base and method
The review is a systematic analysis of longitudinal birth cohort studies that compared IgG antibody responses in dried blood spots (1,601 samples, 370 children) with PCR assays on stool samples (2,231 samples, 370 children) for nine enteric pathogens, including Norovirus GI and GII. The study design involved overlapping samples across pathogens, and the methods included serological and molecular testing.
Consensus and conclusions
The review concludes that IgG and PCR assays captured consistent relative risk of infection across geographic strata, suggesting either method can identify high- versus low-transmission settings. For most pathogens, including Norovirus GI and GII, the force of infection was lowest in urban areas and higher in more rural populations.
Controversies and gaps
There is heterogeneity in the alignment of infection measures for certain pathogens, including norovirus GI, norovirus GII, Cryptosporidium, Shigella/EIEC, and Giardia. Additionally, benchmarking serological measures against stool-based measures remains a critical evidence gap.
Research and practice implications
The review highlights the need for further research to benchmark serological measures against stool-based molecular testing for enteric pathogens. It also suggests that serological surveys could provide valuable insights into norovirus transmission dynamics, particularly in rural populations where the force of infection is higher.
