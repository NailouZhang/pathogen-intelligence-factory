离体模型中的 诺如病毒 种间 传播 取决于病毒内化能力。

Norovirus inter-species transmission in an ex vivo model is dependent on virus internalization capacity.
作者： Nele Villabruna, Myra Hosmillo, Ian Goodfellow, Niklas Arnberg, Juan Bárcena, Silke Rautenschlein, Benjamin Heidrich, Andreas Beineke, Gisa Gerold
摘要中文翻译人类 诺如病毒 (HuNoV) 是全球病毒性胃肠炎最常见的原因,导致散发病例和 暴发。感染野生和家养动物(包括猪和狗)的 诺如病毒 与人类 诺如病毒 有遗传相关性,增加了跨物种 传播 的可能性。为了研究 诺如病毒 跨越物种障碍的潜力,我们使用了猪、犬和禽类精密切割的肠道切片。我们发现,人 诺如病毒 病毒样颗粒 (VLP) 与猪和狗的肠道组织结合,并分别以与猪和犬 诺如病毒 相似的效率被吸收。相反,在鸡组织中没有检测到人、猪或犬诺如病毒的结合或内化。我们进一步表明,人 诺如病毒 在猪肠道组织中复制。相比之下,虽然动物 诺如病毒 附着在人类肠道细胞上,但细胞内的摄取受到限制。这表明人与动物之间的 传播 比动物与人之间的 传播 更有可能,并且病毒摄取可能存在物种障碍。

问题与背景
人类 诺如病毒 (HuNoV) 是全球病毒性胃肠炎的主要原因,但跨物种 传播 的潜力仍知之甚少。该研究探讨了遗传相关的动物诺如病毒是否可以克服物种障碍以及人类诺如病毒是否可以感染动物宿主。

该研究使用猪、狗和鸡的精密切割肠道切片来模拟 诺如病毒 的种间 传播。将猪、犬和禽组织暴露于人、猪和犬 诺如病毒 以评估结合和内化。

将猪、犬和禽类精密切割的肠道切片与人 诺如病毒 病毒样颗粒 (VLP)、猪 诺如病毒 和犬 诺如病毒 一起孵育,以评估结合和内化效率。评估了人 诺如病毒 在猪肠道组织中的复制。
主要结果
人 诺如病毒 VLP 与猪和狗肠道组织结合并被其内化,其效率分别与猪和犬 诺如病毒 相当。在鸡组织中未检测到人、猪或犬 诺如病毒 的结合或内化。人诺如病毒在猪肠道组织中复制,而动物诺如病毒在人肠道细胞中表现出有限的细胞内摄取。
解释与创新
研究结果表明,人与动物之间的 传播 可能比动物与人之间的 传播 更有可能,病毒摄取起到了物种屏障的作用。这是人类 诺如病毒 在离体猪肠道组织中复制的首次演示。
科研与公卫意义
结果表明,猪可以充当人类 诺如病毒 的 储存宿主,引起人们对人畜共患 跨物种溢出 和食品安全的担忧。该研究强调了 诺如病毒 流行地区对动物 宿主 的 监测 的需求。
局限与证据强度
离体模型可能无法完全复制体内条件,限制了自然感染的普遍性。由于缺乏体内验证且实验设置中样本量较小,证据不充分。

Norovirus inter-species transmission in an ex vivo model is dependent on virus internalization capacity.

Authors: Nele Villabruna, Myra Hosmillo, Ian Goodfellow, Niklas Arnberg, Juan Bárcena, Silke Rautenschlein, Benjamin Heidrich, Andreas Beineke, Gisa Gerold
Original abstract (English)
Human noroviruses (HuNoV) are the most common cause of viral gastroenteritis worldwide, causing sporadic cases and outbreaks. Noroviruses that infect wild and domestic animals, including pigs and dogs, are genetically related to human noroviruses, increasing the potential for cross-species transmission. To investigate the potential of noroviruses to cross the species barrier, we used porcine, canine, and avian precision-cut intestinal slices. We show that human norovirus virus-like particles (VLPs) bind to pig and dog intestinal tissue and are taken up with a similar efficiency as the porcine and canine noroviruses, respectively. In contrast, no binding or internalization of human, porcine, or canine noroviruses was detected in chicken tissue. We further showed that human norovirus replicates in pig intestinal tissue. In contrast, while animal noroviruses attached to human intestinal cells, intracellular uptake was limited. This suggests that human-to-animal transmission is more likely than animal-to-human transmission and that viral uptake likely presents a species barrier.

Question and background
Human noroviruses (HuNoV) are the leading cause of viral gastroenteritis globally, yet the potential for cross-species transmission remains poorly understood. The study addresses whether genetically related animal noroviruses can overcome species barriers and whether human noroviruses can infect animal hosts.
Design and population
The study used precision-cut intestinal slices from pigs, dogs, and chickens to model inter-species transmission of noroviruses. Porcine, canine, and avian tissues were exposed to human, porcine, and canine noroviruses to assess binding and internalization.
Core methods
Porcine, canine, and avian precision-cut intestinal slices were incubated with human norovirus virus-like particles (VLPs), porcine norovirus, and canine norovirus to evaluate binding and internalization efficiency. Replication of human norovirus in pig intestinal tissue was assessed.
Main results
Human norovirus VLPs bound to and were internalized by pig and dog intestinal tissue with efficiency comparable to porcine and canine noroviruses, respectively. No binding or internalization of human, porcine, or canine noroviruses was detected in chicken tissue. Human norovirus replicated in pig intestinal tissue, whereas animal noroviruses showed limited intracellular uptake in human intestinal cells.
Interpretation and novelty
The findings suggest that human-to-animal transmission may be more likely than animal-to-human transmission, with viral uptake acting as a species barrier. This is the first demonstration of human norovirus replication in porcine intestinal tissue ex vivo.
Scientific and public-health significance
The results imply that pigs could serve as reservoirs for human norovirus, raising concerns for zoonotic spillover and food safety. The study highlights the need for surveillance of animal hosts in norovirus-endemic regions.
Limitations and evidence strength
The ex vivo model may not fully replicate in vivo conditions, limiting generalizability to natural infections. The evidence is moderate due to the absence of in vivo validation and small sample size in the experimental setup.

问题与背景
该研究解释了为什么针对人类 诺如病毒 的血清 IgA 反应仍不完全清楚,尽管它们可能具有保护作用。其目的是解决二聚体与单体 IgA 库的克隆组成和结构特征如何与中和相关。

Serum IgA proteomics reveals clonal composition and neutralization of dimeric and monomeric IgA repertoires against human norovirus.

Authors: Juyeon Park, Gyunghee Jo, Yaoska Reyes, Whitney Pickens, Dae Sung Kim, Alexandra Beaver, Verónica P. Costantini, Chang Liu, Daechan Park, Victoria Longo
Original abstract (English)
Protection against human norovirus correlates with attachment ligand blockade antibody titers, fecal IgA titers, and serum IgA titers. IgA responses in serum comprise approximately 80 to 95% of monomeric IgA (mIgA) and 5 to 20% of dimeric IgA (dIgA). Using serum LC-MS/MS proteomics, we established clonal relationships between circulating IgG and IgA, as well as between dIgA and mIgA. We observed a modest degree of clonal overlap between circulating IgG and IgA at steady state and found that more than 80% of antigen-specific mIgA was also detectable as dIgA. We biochemically characterized neutralizing epitopes on norovirus GII.4 virus-like particles (VLPs) targeted by serum IgA clonotypes and demonstrated that dIgA markedly enhances neutralization potency relative to mIgA and IgG in an epitope-specific manner. Cryoelectron microscopy and cryoelectron tomography revealed that whether IgA dimerization enhances viral neutralization depends on epitope accessibility on the VLP and antibody binding orientation. Together, these findings provide molecular-level resolution of the serum IgA response and define the structural basis by which dIgA enhances neutralization potency in an epitope-specific manner.

Question and background
The study addressed why serum IgA responses against human norovirus remain incompletely understood despite their likely role in protection. It aimed to resolve how clonal composition and structural features of dimeric versus monomeric IgA repertoires relate to neutralization.
Design and population
The authors used serum LC-MS/MS proteomics to establish clonal relationships among circulating IgG, IgA, and between dimeric and monomeric IgA. They characterized neutralizing epitopes on norovirus GII.4 virus-like particles (VLPs) targeted by serum IgA clonotypes.
Core methods
Serum IgA clonotypes were identified and compared using LC-MS/MS proteomics to assess clonal overlap between IgG and IgA and between dIgA and mIgA. Neutralization potency of dIgA, mIgA, and IgG was measured against GII.4 VLPs. Cryoelectron microscopy and cryoelectron tomography were used to visualize epitope accessibility and antibody binding orientation.
Main results
A modest clonal overlap was observed between circulating IgG and IgA at steady state. More than 80% of antigen-specific monomeric IgA was also detectable as dimeric IgA. Dimeric IgA markedly enhanced neutralization potency relative to monomeric IgA and IgG in an epitope-specific manner.
Interpretation and novelty
The findings provide molecular-level resolution of the serum IgA response and define the structural basis by which dimeric IgA enhances neutralization potency in an epitope-specific manner. This contrasts with monomeric IgA and IgG, highlighting a previously unappreciated role of IgA dimerization.
Scientific and public-health significance
The results suggest that dimeric IgA may be a more effective target for norovirus vaccine design and serological assays than monomeric IgA or IgG. They also imply that epitope accessibility and binding orientation are critical determinants of neutralization efficacy.
Limitations and evidence strength
The study relies on virus-like particles rather than live virus, which may not fully recapitulate natural infection dynamics. The generalizability of findings to broader norovirus genotypes or clinical settings remains to be established, warranting further validation.

Treatment With Ruxolitinib and TAK-779 Enhances GII.17 Human Norovirus Replication and Enables Serial Passaging in Human Intestinal Enteroids.

Authors: Eri Hiraishi, Tsuyoshi Hayashi, Yoshiki Fujii, Hirokazu Kimura, Kosuke Murakami
Original abstract (English)
Human norovirus (HuNoV) is a major cause of acute viral gastroenteritis. Although it can be replicated in vitro using human intestinal enteroids (HIEs), generating high-titer viral laboratory stocks remains challenging due to low infection and passage efficiencies. Interferon response and chemokine signaling may be key host pathways that restrict efficient HuNoV replication. Here, we evaluated the effect of their inhibitors, ruxolitinib-a Janus kinase (JAK) 1/JAK2 inhibitor-and TAK-779-a C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 3 (CXCR3)/C-C motif chemokine receptor (CCR) 5/CCR2 antagonist-on HuNoV replication in wild-type jejunal HIEs. TAK-779 increased GII.17 HuNoV replication by 2.3- and 6.0-fold at 48 and 96 h postinfection (hpi), respectively, compared with the DMSO-treated control. In contrast, ruxolitinib alone did not affect viral replication. Notably, simultaneous treatment with these compounds further enhanced GII.17 replication by 6.0- and 10.7-fold at 48 and 96 hpi, respectively, which enabled serial passaging. These findings imply that the simultaneous suppression of these pathways potentially benefits GII.17 HuNoV replication.

Viral gastroenteritis: Molecular epidemiology of human norovirus by RT-PCR at University Hospital Center of Brazzaville, Republic of Congo

Authors: Raymond AMONA Fulgence, Magloire Anicet BOUMBA Luc, LOUBANO-VOUMBI Ghislain, Michel Emmanuel MBOUNGOU MALONGA Archange, Launay Gerald MISSAMOU Evrard, KOUEYI ESSOVIA Brunelle, Sophie KOMBO BAYONNE Edith, MOUKASSA Donatien, Antoine ABENA Ange
Original abstract (English)
Acute gastroenteritis (AGE) is a major public health concern in Africa, with noroviruses (NoV) recognized as the predominant viral etiological agents. However, their epidemiological impact remains underreported in the Republic of Congo, particularly in Brazzaville. This study aimed to determine the molecular prevalence of NoV among children presenting with AGE, identify the circulating genogroups, and evaluate associated sociodemographic and clinical factors. A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted from June to November 2020 at the University Hospital Center of Brazzaville. Fifty children aged 28 days to 17 years presenting with AGE were enrolled. Stool samples were analyzed using real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) to detect viral RNA and differentiate between GI and GII genogroups. Eighty percent were aged ≤5 years. The overall prevalence of NoV infection was 44%, with GII (73%) predominating over GI (18%). GI/GII co-infections were detected in 9% of cases. Clinically, fever was reported in 80% of cases, increased stool frequency in 86%, and dehydration was observed in all participants (100%). A significant association was observed between age and NoV genogroup distribution (p = 0.03), with GII being more prevalent among children aged ≤5 years. However, clinical manifestations did not differ significantly between genogroups (p > 0.05). Norovirus represents an important cause of pediatric AGE in Brazzaville. Socioeconomic conditions and inadequate hygiene practices appear to play significant roles in NoV transmission.The integration of molecular diagnostic approaches into national surveillance programs and the strengthening of sanitation measures are recommended to improve prevention and control strategies. Key words: Norovirus, acute gastroenteritis, molecular epidemiology, reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), Congo.

A Wastewater-based Modeling Framework for Inferring Norovirus Transmission Dynamics

A Wastewater-based Modeling Framework for Inferring Norovirus Transmission Dynamics

Authors: Jinze Li, Dhvani Parikh, Tin Phan, Runze Li, Samantha Brozak, Bruce Pell, John Balliew, Kristina D. Mena, Yang Kuang, Fuqing Wu
Original abstract (English)
Abstract Norovirus is a leading cause of gastroenteritis globally, yet its true infection burden in the community remains poorly understood because of widespread asymptomatic transmission and underreporting. Wastewater-based surveillance is a promising approach for tracking infection trends, but methods for mechanistically linking wastewater viral concentrations to infection dynamics remain limited. Here, we developed a mechanistic SEIR-V framework to infer community-level norovirus transmission dynamics from wastewater data by jointly modeling disease transmission, viral-shedding kinetics, and wastewater viral load. We leveraged temporal fecal shedding data from a controlled human challenge study and systematic literature review to parametrize symptom-specific shedding profiles and integrate them into the framework. Applied to wastewater data from four treatment plants in El Paso, Texas, the model showed consistent agreement between out-of-sample predictions and observed wastewater viral load across sewersheds while generating model-inferred temporal infection dynamics across disease compartments. Sensitivity analysis further showed that wastewater viral load was influenced by transmission, shedding, and viral loss parameters, whereas inferred infection dynamics were primarily influenced by the transmission rate and infectious duration. This work provides a mechanistic wastewater modeling framework to infer norovirus transmission dynamics and could support proactive public health surveillance, particularly for pathogens with high asymptomatic transmission and limited clinical reporting.

Microbial assessment of recontamination with multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter in patient room environment in a burn unit.

Authors: William A Rutala, Hajime Kanamori, Maria F Gergen, David J Weber
Original abstract (English)
BACKGROUND: Numerous studies have demonstrated that environmental contamination with several epidemiologically important pathogens, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus spp, Acinetobacter, norovirus, and Clostridioides difficile, can persist for several days to months and contributes to the transmission of these pathogens. METHODS: This study was conducted in a burn intensive care unit to determine the time to recontamination of environmental surfaces with a multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii after disinfection. After disinfection, samples were taken from 6 environmental sites using Rodac plates at 1-hour intervals for 10 hours. RESULTS: Time to recontamination with Acinetobacter was as follows: chair, 3 hours; overbed table, 2 hours; stock cabinet, 3 hours; and IV pump, 2 hours. CONCLUSIONS: Our study demonstrated that recontamination with Acinetobacter occurred rapidly at environmental sites in burn units even after cleaning/disinfection and underscored the need for enhanced environmental cleaning, including continuous disinfection methods, to prevent transmission of health care-associated pathogens.

In this week’s newsletter: As promoters like James Scarlett prove, the UK’s festival circuit continues to weather each disaster with remarkable grit Don’t get The Guide delivered to your inbox? Sign up here Running a festival is not for the faint-hearted. The list of potential weekend-derailers is dauntingly long – licensing issues, awol bands, freak weather events to name but a few – and one misstep could prove disastrous, particularly for those smaller non-conglomerate festivals where every last penny and punter counts. Thirty six UK festivals have been cancelled so far in 2026 – and if a festival is cancelled one year, it makes returning for the next one vastly more challenging. The heartening news is that’s the lowest annual figure for cancelled fests since Covid (though admittedly with still some of the season to run), and less than half the number of festivals that were cancelled in 2024. All things considered, the UK and Ireland’s independent festival scene is – like its able-to-survive-in-a-tent-for-four-days-without-a-shower-or-sleep attenders – remarkably hardy. There are success stories all over the place, from Sea Power’s Krankenhaus event in Cumbria, to the mighty End of the Road festival in Wiltshire, which celebrates its 20th instalment this month. Festival promoter James Scarlett is behind two other festival success stories: rock, punk and emo fest 2000 Trees, which enjoyed its 19th outing last month, and the metal, prog and post-rock-tinged ArcTanGent, whose fully sold out 12th edition takes place next week. Because he doesn’t have enough on, James also, along with fellow festival promoter Gavin McInally, hosts Two Promoters One Pod, a podcast entertainingly sharing the challenges of putting on massive great musical shindigs. Last year, to get a sense of those challenges, we asked James to do some festival firefighting and solve some major theoretical doomsday scenarios, and we enjoyed it so much we’ve booked him again this year for an encore set. He’s already ticked off one potential nightmare this year, with 2000 Trees landing in the middle of our never-ending summer heatwave (the forecast for ArcTanGent is looking thankfully cooler, touch wood), but here are five others: Festival disaster #1 | Your costly Friday night headlining band, a huge draw for punters, comes up to you 15 minutes before they are due to go onstage, and say that their fee is insufficient, and they want another 10% on top. What do you do? I think you try to talk to them about how you’re independent and basically a bunch of friends and family running a festival and pulling this sort of thing isn’t going to look good. I do know a festival that this happened to, which I can’t name: they literally got their banking app out and showed the band how much money the festival had to its name. And the band are like, “Oh, OK, we’ll play.” Otherwise, it’s a shitty thing in the extreme to ask that. Festival disaster #2 | Norovirus sweeps through the site, causing mass nausea, vomiting and other unpleasantness. What do you do? [NB. this actually happened at German electronic festival Whole earlier this summer] How can we stop it spreading? Education! To be honest, I’d probably literally be Googling “how do you avoid transmitting norovirus?” These sorts of things, where you’ve never had them happen before, you kind of wing it. We’ve got an onsite app, so can put up notifications, as well as on social media and put signs up about hand-washing and personal contact. Don’t touch anyone! And we’d make sure hand-washing facilities are up to date. We’d be trying to figure out the source, where the outbreak has come from. Is it from inside the festival? The people who you really can’t let get ill are the bands, so maybe quarantining some of them would be needed too. Festival disaster #3 | Two days into your four-day festival, your supplier runs out of beer. What do you do? View image in fullscreen No party without the beer tent. Photograph: Maja Smiejkowska/Shutterstock You usually know if you’re going to run out half a day before. At that point you’re on the phone to the brewery and they’re hopefully bringing more beer to you. But because we want to work with independent businesses, instead of big corporates, there is a limit to how much beer they’ve got. If your brewery runs out, you’ve got a very difficult decision to make as to whether you try to source some other beer. I mean, you could literally drive to a cash and carry, and buy some cans of Carling. But we have a deal in place with our brewery, so we’ve never done that. Last year at 2000 Trees we ran out on Saturday evening, which was really frustrating because you’re leaving money in people’s pockets, without wanting to sound like a corporate beast! But equally you’re like, “well we’ve sold everything so we must have had a good year!” Festival disaster #4 | An activist group begin a mass sit-in in front your main stage and refuse to leave until the festival is finished. What do you do? skip past newsletter promotion after newsletter promotion My unofficial answer is that I’d be down there with my mates trying to get these people out of the way because at the end of the day, you’ve got 15,000 people there for a festival. I’m all about the right to protest but if you’re disrupting my ability to put food on the table for my family and pay my mortgage, I start to feel a bit different about things. We’ve had something like this happen at 2000 Trees, when we booked Drag Queen Story Hour. Some far-right zealots must have search terms set up, so when the words “drag queen” come up on Facebook they get alerted and go and protest it. They started sending us loads of hate messages, saying that they were going to come in buses, and blockade the road. But those people are cowards. They’re just 45-year-old men sat behind the computer in their mum’s spare room. They’ve got a lot of hate, but nothing to back it up. So it never went anywhere – and we put on Drag Queen Story Hour the following year too. Festival disaster #5 | A huge storm on the first night of the festival causes major disruption, with standing water in much of the campsite and on several of the stages. What do you do? We’ve sucked puddles out before. I mean, how much water you can suck out, is debateable, but don’t forget, you’ve got a toilet contractor on site who is sucking waste out of toilets all weekend. So they have suckers! It would depend how much water there was. Water creates a drowning risk and doesn’t go well with electricity. You have to make sure there’s no power anywhere near it. I think it’s important to get a site with good drainage. Here at ArcTanGent, the site’s on limestone cliffs. So actually, the water does have somewhere to go, because limestone is porous. But on other sites the water doesn’t have anywhere to go. I remember seeing a video of Creamfields one year. And it was like a flash flood in the campsite and there was a raging river going through the campsite. I mean, that’s a life and death thing. You’ve got to be really careful.
