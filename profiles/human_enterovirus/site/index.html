First Adult Case of Echovirus 21–Associated Poliomyelitis-Like Myelitis Presenting with Flaccid Asymmetric Tetraparesis

Authors: Ibrahim Farhood, Lars Schönlau, Thomas Mika, Roland Schroers, Antonios Katsounas, Mohamad Amer Nashtar, Corinna Seliger
Original abstract (English)
Abstract Background Non-polio enterovirus–associated myelitis is a rare condition causing acute flaccid paralysis due to anterior horn cell injury. While most cases occur in children, reports in adults are exceedingly uncommon. Methods We report the first known case in a 63-year-old woman with relapsed/refractory mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) after second-line therapy with rituximab and ibrutinib. Following leukapheresis for planned CAR-T-cell therapy, she received bridging chemotherapy with bendamustine, cytarabine, and rituximab. During prolonged neutropenia and lymphopenia, she developed sudden asymmetric tetraparesis. Diagnostic workup included brain and spinal MRI, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis with cytology and flow cytometry, nerve conduction studies (NCS), and electromyography (EMG). Results NCS and EMG demonstrated a predominantly axonal neuropathy with acute denervation. Spinal MRI revealed cervical intramedullary T2 hyperintensities with a characteristic “snake-eye” sign involving the anterior horns. CSF analysis showed normal cell counts and protein levels without lymphoma involvement. Multiplex PCR detected enteroviruses, subsequently identified as Echovirus-21. Initial treatment with intravenous immunoglobulins for suspected Guillain–Barré syndrome was discontinued after confirmation of enterovirus-associated myelitis, and supportive care was provided. Discussion Although rare in adults, enterovirus-associated myelitis should be considered in immunosuppressed patients presenting with acute flaccid paralysis. Early diagnosis using spinal MRI, CSF PCR, and electrodiagnostic studies is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment.

CATCH-EVseq, an Ultra-Sensitive Capture Assay to Monitor Human Enterovirus RNA

Authors: Arnaud Germain, Maureen R. Hanson
Original abstract (English)
The detection of low-abundance viral RNA in chronic or persistent infections remains a major challenge in virology, constrained by the sensitivity and specificity of traditional methods. One such infection, chronic enterovirus (EV) infection, has been proposed as a potential underlying factor in Myalgic Encephalomyelitis (ME), although this hypothesis remains debated. Supporting evidence for EVs as a viral trigger includes historical spatiotemporal overlaps between ME outbreaks and poliomyelitis epidemics, frequent reports of “flu-like” illness at disease onset, seasonal outbreaks patterns, and serological findings. To address the problem of EV detection, we developed CATCH-EVseq, an ultra-sensitive platform built on the CATCH framework. By integrating high-throughput sequencing with cDNA capture using 49,847 probes targeting 3410 EV genomes, CATCH-EVseq achieved a detection limit in artificially created samples as low as one copy in a microgram of RNA for two out of three biological replicates. Additionally, spike-in controls allow for rigorous validation of the capture and sequencing throughout the CATCH-EVseq procedure. When applied to a small number of blood samples from both ME patients and healthy controls, CATCH-EVseq detected no EV RNA, either in direct assays or after in vitro viral amplification. While these results do not support the presence of circulating EV RNA in the blood of individuals with ME, they do not rule out persistent infection in other tissues or organs. Critically, CATCH-EVseq establishes a robust, ultra-sensitive framework for future investigations of low-level EV persistence in biopsies, post-mortem tissues, or other clinical contexts where chronic enterovirus infection is suspected.

蜱传脑炎和肠病毒性脑膜炎脑脊液蛋白质组的时间动态

Temporal dynamics of the cerebrospinal fluid proteome in tick-borne encephalitis and enteroviral meningitis
作者： Jakob Morén, Peng Li, Aniko Bartfai, Göran Günther, Åke Lundkvist, Jonas Bergquist, Gabriel Westman
摘要中文翻译背景 蜱传脑炎(TBE)是一种严重的中枢神经系统(CNS)病毒感染,可引起急性脑膜脑炎,并常常造成长期的神经认知后遗症。TBE 缺乏特定的 治疗 突出表明需要提高对疾病机制的理解并识别新的生物标志物。肠病毒性脑膜炎通常是一种较温和的中枢神经系统感染,很少引起脑炎。目的 在这项假设生成研究中,我们旨在描述 TBE 中脑脊液 (CSF) 蛋白质组的时间变化,并确定与病理生理机制、疾病严重程度和神经认知结果相关的候选生物标志物。方法追溯地包括先前建立的 队列研究。通过无标记质谱分析脑脊液蛋白质组,然后进行差异蛋白表达分析和功能注释。评估了时间蛋白质组动力学以及蛋白质与疾病严重程度和神经认知结果的关联。数据可通过 ProteomeXchange 获得,标识符为 PXD077931。结果蛋白质组学分析揭示了与突触功能、炎症途径、补体系统和 14-3-3 蛋白家族相关的蛋白质的变化。与肠病毒性脑膜炎相比,TBE后期的肾上腺髓质素显着增加。结论TBE中脑脊液蛋白质组的时间变化表明在急性期发生了强烈的炎症反应,同时与突触功能相关的蛋白质水平降低。这些发现提供了对 TBE 的 宿主 响应和 发病机制 的深入了解,并确定了未来验证可能感兴趣的蛋白质。

Authors: Jakob Morén, Peng Li, Aniko Bartfai, Göran Günther, Åke Lundkvist, Jonas Bergquist, Gabriel Westman
Original abstract (English)
Background Tick-borne encephalitis (TBE) is a severe viral infection of the central nervous system (CNS), causing acute meningoencephalitis and often long-term neurocognitive sequelae. The lack of specific treatment for TBE highlights the need to improve understanding of disease mechanisms and identify novel biomarkers. Enteroviral meningitis is typically a milder infection of the CNS and rarely causes encephalitis.Objectives In this hypothesis-generating study, we aimed to characterise temporal changes in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) proteome in TBE and identify candidate biomarkers associated with pathophysiological mechanisms, disease severity, and neurocognitive outcome.Methods Patients with TBE, enteroviral meningitis, or aseptic meningoencephalitis of unknown origin were retrospectively included from a previously established cohort. CSF proteomes were analysed by label-free mass spectrometry, followed by differential protein expression analysis and functional annotation. Temporal proteome dynamics and protein associations with disease severity and neurocognitive outcome were assessed. Data are available via ProteomeXchange with identifier PXD077931.Results Proteomic analyses revealed alterations in proteins related to synaptic function, inflammatory pathways, the complement system, and the 14-3-3 protein family. These findings provide insights into the host response and pathogenesis of TBE and identify proteins of potential interest for future validation.

Authors: Shi-Jin Wang, Yu-Bin Tang, Song-Yang Lai, Jia-Xin Yang, Qing-Ran Yang, Xi-Ze Liu, Xiao-Fang Yu, Hao-Ran Guo, Wei Wei
Original abstract (English)
Enterovirus A71 (EV-A71) and coxsackievirus A16 (CV-A16) are leading causes of severe hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD) with high childhood mortality and no specific antiviral therapy. SCARB2 is the essential uncoating receptor, yet extracellular SCARB2-Fc decoys fail to block infection, representing a decade-old paradox in enterovirus therapeutics. Here, we overcome this limitation by engineering RMP(R8), a cell-penetrating SCARB2 mimetic peptide comprising the minimal VP2-binding domain (W146-K166) fused to an octa-arginine transporter. RMP(R8) shows potent antiviral activity (EC50 = 0.156 μg/mL), outstanding safety (selectivity index > 2003), and excellent aqueous solubility, a critical asset for clinical translation. It broadly inhibits genetically divergent EV-A71 isolates and CV-A16 in intestinal and neural cells by blocking viral entry. In a lethal neonatal mouse model, RMP(R8) increases survival against EV-A71 and CV-A16 challenge in vivo. Intracellular receptor mimicry thus establishes a generalizable strategy for viruses refractory to extracellular intervention. RMP(R8) is a promising clinical candidate to address the unmet need for targeted anti-enterovirus treatment, and our findings redefine how to drug virus-receptor interactions that act inside cells.

Enterovirus 2C Protein Drug Design, Assay Development, and Drug Resistance Studies

Authors: Kan Li, Hiwot A Demssie, Guangjin Fan, Jun Wang
Original abstract (English)
Conspectus In this Account, we describe our efforts to establish enteroviral 2C as a tractable target for antiviral drug discovery by integrating medicinal chemistry, molecular pharmacology, resistance profiling, structural modeling, and assay development. Rather than relying solely on conventional phenotypic high-throughput screening, our initial studies focused on the rational optimization of reported 2C inhibitors identified from a drug-repurposing campaign. This strategy led to quinoline-based antivirals with substantially improved potency and selectivity against enterovirus D68 (EV-D68), providing early medicinal chemistry insights into ligand recognition by enteroviral 2C and supporting the feasibility of chemically targeting this protein. Building on these findings, we subsequently developed pyrazolopyridine-based inhibitors, which evolved into one of the most potent classes of enteroviral 2C inhibitors reported to date. Mechanistic studies combining differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF), serial viral passage, reverse genetics, and molecular modeling supported direct engagement of a conserved allosteric pocket corresponding to the previously defined (S)-fluoxetine binding site. Structure-guided optimization led to Jun6504, a rationally designed 2C inhibitor with improved metabolic stability, favorable pharmacokinetic properties, and robust in vivo efficacy in a neonatal mouse model of EV-D68-induced paralysis. Collectively, these studies provided strong pharmacological validation of 2C as a therapeutically actionable antiviral target. In parallel, we recognized that the absence of direct, scalable biochemical assays was a major impediment to 2C-targeted antiviral discovery. To address this bottleneck, we developed a fluorescence polarization-based binding assay that monitors ligand engagement of the conserved allosteric pocket in enteroviral 2C proteins. Using Jun14157, a fluorescent probe derived from the pyrazolopyridine inhibitor series, this platform enabled direct, quantitative, and high-throughput characterization of ligand–2C interactions across EV-D68, EV-A71, and CVB3. Coupling this assay with structure-based virtual screening further enabled the identification of new allosteric inhibitor scaffolds and established a generalizable discovery platform for enteroviral 2C inhibitors. Together, these studies define an integrated framework for enteroviral 2C antiviral discovery, spanning hit-to-lead optimization, mechanism of action studies, resistance mapping, pharmacological validation, and assay innovation. Continued advances in inhibitor-bound structural elucidation, broad-spectrum inhibitor design, and next-generation therapeutic strategies should further accelerate the development of clinically useful antivirals targeting enteroviral 2C proteins.

Enterovirus A71, often abbreviated EV-A71, and its frequent companion coxsackievirus A16 are the leading viral culprits behind serious cases of the disease, capable of progressing from fever and blistering rashes to life-threatening brainstem encephalitis, pulmonary edema, and death in infants and small children. Despite decades of research and the deployment of vaccines in some countries, no specific antiviral drug against these pathogens has reached clinical practice, leaving physicians to manage the sickest patients with supportive care alone. A team led by Shijin Wang, Yubin Tang, and colleagues, writing in PLOS Pathogens, now reports a molecule that may change that picture. The researchers describe RMP(R8), an engineered peptide that mimics the part of a human protein called SCARB2, which the viruses use as their essential gateway into cells. In laboratory tests the compound blocked EV-A71 and CV-A16 at remarkably low concentrations, showed an excellent safety profile, and improved survival in a lethal neonatal mouse model of infection. The work also resolves a paradox that has frustrated antiviral developers for roughly a decade, and in doing so proposes a new playbook for drugging virus-receptor interactions that occur deep inside cells rather than on their surfaces. To appreciate why the finding matters, it helps to understand how these viruses gain entry. EV-A71 and CV-A16 belong to the genus Enterovirus within the family Picornaviridae, small non-enveloped viruses whose outer shell is built from four structural proteins arranged with icosahedral symmetry. For enteroviruses of this group, attachment to a surface receptor is only the first act. The truly decisive step, known as uncoating, occurs after the virus has been taken up into the cell, when the protein shell rearranges or releases its RNA genome so replication can begin. SCARB2, a lysosomal membrane protein found abundantly on many human cell types, is the receptor that triggers this uncoating reaction. Structural studies have shown that binding of the virion to SCARB2 destabilizes the capsid, converting the virus into an expanded particle that expels its genome into the cytosol. That mechanism suggests an obvious therapeutic strategy, at least on paper. If a soluble fragment of SCARB2 floating outside the cell could latch onto incoming virus particles and prematurely trip their uncoating switch, the virus would fall apart harmlessly in extracellular fluid. Such receptor decoys have succeeded against other pathogens, and researchers duly built Fc-fusion proteins carrying the extracellular domain of SCARB2. The results were consistently disappointing. The decoys failed to neutralize EV-A71 in meaningful ways, a puzzle that has stood in the literature for some ten years and discouraged further investment in SCARB2-based antivirals. The reason, as the new study makes clear, is that SCARB2 does its critical work not on the cell surface but inside the endosomal and lysosomal compartments, where the acidity and protease environment differ dramatically from conditions outside the cell and where a large, bulky Fc-fusion protein simply cannot follow the virus. The team’s solution was to shrink the receptor to its functional core and smuggle it to where it is needed. Mining the structure of the SCARB2-virion interface, they identified a short stretch of the protein, spanning amino acids W146 through K166, as the minimal region that binds VP2, one of the capsid proteins on the viral shell. They fused this twenty-one-residue binding domain to octa-arginine, a classical cell-penetrating peptide composed of eight positively charged arginine residues. The result is RMP(R8), an intracellular receptor mimetic: small enough to cross cell membranes and enter the cytosol and vesicular compartments, yet retaining the binding surface that the virus itself recognizes. In effect, the molecule impersonates the receptor inside the cell, contacting virus particles at the very stage where extracellular decoys are absent and where premature engagement spells disaster for the pathogen. The numbers reported for the compound are striking. RMP(R8) inhibited EV-A71 with a half-maximal effective concentration of 0.156 micrograms per milliliter, a potent value for a peptide antiviral. Its selectivity index, the ratio comparing the dose that harms host cells with the dose that blocks the virus, exceeded 2003, indicating a wide therapeutic margin. The peptide also dissolved readily in water, an unglamorous but decisive property for any molecule hoping to be formulated, dosed, and distributed clinically. In cultured cells derived from the intestine and the nervous system, tissues that matter most for enterovirus disease, RMP(R8) suppressed infection by genetically divergent EV-A71 isolates as well as by CV-A16, suggesting the activity is not confined to a single laboratory strain but extends across the viral diversity that vaccines and strain-specific antibodies struggle to cover. Mechanistically, the authors traced the compound’s action to the blockage of viral entry. Rather than preventing attachment to the cell surface, RMP(R8) interferes with the intracellular step at which SCARB2 normally catalyzes uncoating. By engaging the virion’s VP2-binding site at the wrong time and place, the mimetic presumably triggers or locks the capsid into a state that cannot deliver its genome productively. This timing explains the failure of the older decoys and the success of the new one: the virus is vulnerable to receptor mimicry only after it has entered the cell, and only a molecule that reaches that compartment can exploit the vulnerability. The concept is generalizable in principle. Any virus that performs its key receptor-dependent step intracellularly, and there are several among the picornaviruses and related families, could in theory be targeted by a cell-penetrating mimetic of the relevant receptor domain. Animal testing provided the strongest evidence that the approach survives contact with a living system. In a lethal neonatal mouse model, the standard challenge assay for EV-A71 pathogenesis, treatment with RMP(R8) significantly increased survival of infected pups following challenge with EV-A71 and with CV-A16 alike. Neonatal mice lack a mature immune system and are exquisitely susceptible to these viruses, so a survival benefit in this setting is a meaningful benchmark for a candidate antiviral. The breadth across two distinct viral species is particularly notable, because cross-species coverage is rare among antivirals that target viral enzymes or capsid pockets, which are easily undermined by a handful of point mutations. Targeting the conserved receptor-binding surface, by contrast, raises the genetic barrier to escape. Considerable work remains between a promising peptide and an approved medicine. RMP(R8) must still demonstrate an acceptable pharmacokinetic profile, stability against proteases, appropriate tissue distribution, and safety in larger animals, and the dosing window in human infection, where treatment would likely begin after symptom onset, will need careful definition. Cell-penetrating peptides also face well-known questions about delivery and clearance in patients. Even so, the study stands out for solving a concrete mechanistic puzzle: it explains why extracellular SCARB2 decoys failed, and it converts that explanation into a working drug candidate with potency, breadth, and in vivo efficacy. For the millions of children at risk of severe hand, foot, and mouth disease each year, and for the clinicians who currently have nothing specific to offer them, an intracellular receptor mimetic that attacks EV-A71 and CV-A16 at their most vulnerable moment represents exactly the kind of targeted therapy the field has been waiting for. It also redraws the boundary of antiviral design, showing that the inside of the cell, long considered hostile territory for receptor-based drugs, can be reached and exploited. Scope of the agreement and approval requirements According to The Edge Malaysia, as reported in the group's stock exchange filing on Thursday, the supply agreement was signed between Taiwan's Enimmune Corp and Duopharma (M) Sdn Bhd, a wholly owned unit of Duopharma Biotech. Under the agreement, Duopharma (M) was granted exclusive rights to advertise, market, sell and obtain marketing approval for the EnVAX-A71 vaccine in Malaysia, Singapore and Brunei. Under the terms, Duopharma (M) is responsible for obtaining regulatory approval for the vaccine in all three countries. If any country rejects approval for the vaccine, the company's obligations for that country will be terminated. The agreement remains in force in each territory until the fifth anniversary of the launch date in the respective jurisdiction, provided that Duopharma (M) meets the minimum annual order quantity after registration is approved. If the company fails to meet 70% of the minimum annual quantity for two consecutive contract years, Enimmune has the right to terminate the agreement. In a separate statement on Thursday, Duopharma Biotech chief executive officer Wan Amir-Jeffery Wan Abdul Majid said the collaboration strengthens the group's vaccine portfolio and helps build a sustainable presence in preventive healthcare. The group also said Enimmune will bear the costs related to Good Manufacturing Practice assessments and provide marketing support for the launch in Malaysia. According to Wan Amir-Jeffery, the group's focus is on bringing specialised vaccines to Malaysia that address unmet needs and complement existing healthcare treatment options. HFMD is an infectious disease that commonly affects children under the age of five, with early symptoms including fever, sore throat and loss of appetite. The disease also causes painful mouth ulcers and rashes on the hands, soles of the feet and buttocks. Duopharma stated that HFMD is a major public health concern because outbreaks occur frequently in Malaysia. The disease is commonly caused by the enteroviruses Coxsackievirus A16 and Enterovirus A71, while EnVAX-A71 is a monovalent vaccine targeting EV71, a strain that can cause severe neurological complications and death among children. There is currently no cure for HFMD, and no vaccine has yet been approved for use in Malaysia. KUALA LUMPUR (Oct 15): Duopharma Biotech Bhd (KL:DPHARMA) said on Thursday its wholly owned unit has secured exclusive rights to distribute Taiwan-based Enimmune Corp's hand, foot and mouth disease (HFMD) vaccine in Malaysia, Singapore and Brunei, subject to regulatory approval in the three countries. According to the group’s bourse filing on Thursday, the supply agreement was signed between Enimmune and Duopharma Biotech's wholly owned unit Duopharma (M) Sdn Bhd (DMSB). Under the deal, DMSB is responsible for getting regulatory approval for the EnVAX-A71 Vaccine in the three countries. The deal also grants DMSB exclusive rights to advertise, market, sell and obtain marketing approval for the vaccine. If a country refuses to approve the vaccine, DMSB's obligations in that country are terminated. In a separate statement on Thursday, Duopharma Biotech chief executive officer Wan Amir-Jeffery Wan Abdul Majid said: “This partnership is a further step in strengthening our vaccine portfolio and building a sustainable presence in preventive healthcare.” The group said Enimmune will cover costs related to Good Manufacturing Practice assessments and provide marketing support for the Malaysian launch. The agreement will remain in force in each territory until the fifth anniversary of the launch date in the respective jurisdictions, contingent on DMSB fulfilling minimum annual order quantities once registration is approved. If DMSB fails to fulfil 70% of the annual minimum order quantity for two consecutive contract years, Enimmune has the right to terminate the agreement. Niche vaccine for major public health concern Duopharma said the deal aligns with its strategy to expand its biologics portfolio. “Our focus is on bringing niche vaccines to Malaysia that address unmet needs and complement the healthcare options available today,” Wan added. HFMD is a contagious disease that commonly infects children under the age of 5. Early symptoms include fever, sore throat and reduced appetite. It also causes painful mouth sores and rashes on the hands, soles of feet and buttocks. Due to its contagious nature and severity, HFMD is a significant public health concern as outbreaks are common in Malaysia. HFMD is usually caused by enteroviruses Coxsackievirus A16 and Enterovirus A71 (EV71). There is no cure for HFMD and there is currently no vaccine approved for use in Malaysia. The EnVAX-A71 Vaccine is a monovalent inoculation that targets EV71, which is the strain that causes severe neurological complications and fatalities in children.
