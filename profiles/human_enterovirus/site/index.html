IMB-A3, an isoindolinone derivative, functions as a molecular glue to promote CRBN-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of enterovirus A71 2C protein.

Authors: Boming Cui, Huiqiang Wang, Ge Yang, Weiyi Yin, Lijun Qiao, Shuo Wu, Haiyan Yan, Kun Wang, Zhuorong Li, Yanping Li
Original abstract (English)
EV-A71 is one of the main culprits causing hand-foot-mouth disease in children. Currently, there are no specific drugs for the prevention and treatment of EV-A71 infection. In our previous studies, a series of 2-aryl-isoindolin-1-one compounds were found to exhibit anti-EV-A71 activity, among which IMB-A3 demonstrated the most potent antiviral activity. Here, we systematically characterized the in vitro and in vivo antiviral efficacy of IMB-A3 and investigated its mechanism of action. The results showed that IMB-A3 treatment decreased the levels of EV-A71 VP1 protein and viral RNA in the infected cells and mice muscles. IMB-A3 could effectively protected mice from lethal EV-A71 infection. Mechanism studies have shown that IMB-A3 interacts directly with the EV-A71 2C protein, but IMB-A3 has no effect on the ATPase and helicase activities of the 2C protein. Interestingly, IMB-A3 acts as a molecular glue to facilitate the interaction between 2C and CRBN, thereby promoting the ubiquitination and degradation of the N-terminal region of the 2C protein and exerting an antiviral effect. These findings indicate that IMB-A3 is a degrader of the EV-A71 2C protein, providing a basis for the future design and development of targeted degraders of the EV-A71 2C protein.

Scientific and public-health significance
The study identifies IMB-A3 as a promising candidate for the development of targeted antiviral therapies against EV-A71, potentially addressing the current lack of specific treatments. Its mechanism of action suggests broader applicability for designing degraders of viral proteins.
Limitations and evidence strength
The evidence is limited to in vitro and mouse models, which may not fully recapitulate human infection dynamics or safety profiles. While the mechanism is well-characterized, further studies are needed to evaluate efficacy, pharmacokinetics, and safety in humans before clinical translation.

Original source text (English)
Hand, Foot, and Mouth Disease (HFMD) is caused primarily by two types of viruses: Coxsackievirus A6 & A16 and Enterovirus A71 (EV-A71) . This disease primarily affects young children and can sometimes lead to severe complications. Symptoms typically appear 2–3 days after incubation, starting with fever. HFMD is most common in children aged 6 months to 5 years. However, both children and caregivers can take preventive measures to reduce the risk of infection. Symptoms of HFMD The initial symptoms of HFMD include high fever, decreased appetite, and excessive drooling. Upon physical examination, ulcers can be found inside the cheeks and on the soft palate. Red spots or fluid-filled blisters may appear on the palms, soles of the feet, buttocks, genitals, arms, and legs. These symptoms generally improve within 2–3 days. Most children over the age of 5 develop immunity to the virus and are less likely to contract the disease. Although rare, adults who have never been infected and have not developed immunity may also contract HFMD, as can older children who lack prior exposure to the virus. Transmission of HFMD HFMD spreads through direct contact with nasal secretions, saliva, or feces of an infected person. The virus is especially concentrated in stool, making it highly contagious. Infected children may also transmit the virus through contact with fluid from blisters. The virus can persist in the body for weeks or even months after recovery, meaning that individuals can continue to spread the infection even after symptoms subside. Indirect transmission is also possible through contaminated objects. For example, if an infected child touches toys, other children who play with the same toys can contract the virus. Additionally, HFMD can spread through contaminated food or water, making young children particularly vulnerable. High-Risk Environments Childcare facilities, especially nurseries where caregivers frequently change diapers, pose a high risk for HFMD transmission. If proper hygiene practices are not followed, the virus can easily spread through contaminated hands or surfaces. Play areas, such as ball pits, are also potential hotspots due to inadequate cleaning. Most children recover from HFMD without complications. However, infections caused by Enterovirus A71 can lead to severe complications. Complications of HFMD Severe complications from HFMD include brainstem encephalitis, meningitis, heart failure, and acute paralysis , all of which can be life-threatening. Warning signs of serious complications include lethargy, muscle weakness, seizures, tremors, difficulty walking, rapid breathing, and vomiting. If any of these symptoms appear, immediate medical attention is required. No Specific Treatment Available Currently, no antiviral medication specifically targets HFMD. Treatment focuses on managing symptoms. Fever-reducing medications can be used to control fever. If a child has difficulty eating, intravenous fluids may be administered. Soft, easily digestible, and cold foods that do not irritate the mouth are recommended, along with adequate hydration to support recovery. HFMD Prevention Caregivers can help prevent HFMD by avoiding contact with contaminated surfaces and maintaining proper hygiene. Since this virus is resistant to alcohol-based hand sanitizers, proper handwashing with soap and water is crucial. Key preventive measures include: Frequent Handwashing: Wash hands with soap and water before and after meals, after using the restroom, and after changing diapers. Avoiding Shared Items: Children should not share utensils, bottles, or other personal items, particularly in schools or daycare settings. Cleaning Toys and Surfaces: Parents should regularly clean toys and play areas using soap and water, thoroughly drying them before use. Detergents can also be used for disinfection. Keeping Sick Children at Home: If a child is unwell, they should stay home to prevent spreading the virus to others. HFMD Vaccination A vaccine is available to protect against HFMD caused by Enterovirus A71 . Children aged 6 months to under 6 years can receive two doses, administered one month apart. Conditions Sudden Arm or Leg Weakness in a Child Is an Emergency, and Late Summer Is When It Has Appeared Before Published Jul 30, 2026 12:00 PM EDT By Elena Vega ORLANDO SIERRA | AFP via Getty Images Share on Twitter Share on Facebook Share on Pocket What Parents Should Recognize The scenario worth knowing is specific: a child who had a cold or a fever in the past week or two suddenly cannot lift an arm, or a leg gives out. Acute flaccid myelitis is a rare neurologic condition that causes sudden weakness in one or more limbs, with damage to the gray matter of the spinal cord. It predominantly affects previously healthy children, and it can progress quickly, in some cases to breathing difficulty requiring intensive care. The weakness is the signal. It is flaccid, meaning the limb is limp rather than stiff, and it comes on over hours to days rather than gradually. It frequently follows a respiratory illness or fever by a few days to a couple of weeks. CDC guidance to clinicians has been consistent across surveillance reports: suspect AFM "in any child with acute flaccid limb weakness," particularly after a recent febrile or respiratory illness. Escalation signs matter more than the initial weakness. Neck weakness or difficulty holding the head up, facial droop, drooping eyelids, difficulty swallowing, slurred speech, or any change in breathing all indicate the problem may be affecting muscles needed to protect the airway. Those warrant emergency care immediately rather than a call to the pediatrician's office. What the New Surveillance Report Found The report published this month is reassuring on frequency, which is worth stating clearly before anything else. Between 2020 and 2025, the CDC recorded between 17 and 48 AFM cases nationally per year. For comparison, the peak years of 2014, 2016 and 2018 produced 120 to 238 cases annually. The more interesting finding is that cases stayed low even when the virus most associated with AFM did not. Enterovirus D68, or EV-D68, drove those earlier peaks, and the report notes increases in EV-D68-associated respiratory illness in 2022, 2024, and 2025 without a corresponding rise in AFM. Why that link appears to have weakened is not established. It may reflect changes in circulating viral strains, population immunity, or factors not yet identified. The honest position is that nobody knows, and nobody can currently predict when or whether cases will rise again. For a parent, the practical meaning is proportion. AFM is genuinely rare, and the overwhelming majority of children who get a summer respiratory virus will not develop it. Nothing in this report suggests families should worry more this year than last. Why Late Summer Matters The seasonality is real, and it comes from the virus rather than from anything about the season itself. In the peak years, AFM cases clustered in late summer and early fall. That timing tracks enterovirus circulation, which rises as children return to school and shed viruses in close quarters. Enteroviruses are a large family, most of which cause nothing worse than a summer cold, hand-foot-and-mouth disease, or a few days of fever. So the window from roughly August through October is when clinicians historically have been most alert. That is useful context for a parent trying to judge whether a symptom deserves urgency, and it is also a reason not to over-read it, since low case counts have held through recent seasons regardless of month. Cases have been reported in 49 states and the District of Columbia over the surveillance period, so this is not a regional concern. There is no vaccine for EV-D68 and no specific antiviral treatment for AFM. Care is supportive, focused on protecting breathing and swallowing and on rehabilitation afterward. Historical data indicate that roughly half of patients required intensive care and a smaller share required mechanical ventilation. The Polio Question in the Report The finding likely to matter most to public health is one a parent might otherwise skip, and it has a simple household action attached. AFM is, in the report's words, clinically and radiologically indistinguishable from the paralysis caused by poliovirus. A doctor looking at a child with sudden limb weakness and an MRI showing spinal cord lesions cannot tell from those alone which one they are dealing with. That makes laboratory testing the only way to distinguish them, and the report found a gap. Approximately half of AFM patients had stool specimens tested for poliovirus. The recommendation is that stool specimens should be collected and tested from all patients suspected of having AFM. The reason this is not theoretical: one polio case was identified in New York in 2022. The report also found that between 75 and 100 percent of AFM patients had received three or more polio vaccine doses, and its stated implication is that "remaining current with polio vaccinations can reduce the risk for poliovirus infection." For families, that is the concrete takeaway. The routine childhood polio series is protection against the one preventable cause of sudden paralysis. When to Go to the Emergency Department The decision rule here is simpler than most, because the threshold for acting is low. Go to an emergency department, or call 911, for sudden weakness in an arm or leg in a child, particularly following a recent illness. Do not wait for a next-day appointment, and do not wait to see whether it improves. Rapid evaluation matters because the condition can progress and because supportive care is most effective when started early. Call 911 rather than driving for any difficulty breathing, difficulty swallowing or managing saliva, slurred speech, or a child who cannot hold up their head. At the hospital, it is reasonable to ask whether AFM is being considered, whether an MRI of the spine is planned, and whether stool and respiratory specimens are being collected. Those questions are appropriate rather than pushy, and given the testing gap the report identified, the stool specimen question is fair. Confirm your child's polio vaccinations are current at the next well visit. Complaining of tired legs after a long day is not this. Waking up unable to lift an arm is. The confirmed findings are 17 to 48 annual AFM cases since 2020, well below the peak years, with no rise despite increased EV-D68 activity. Those most affected are previously healthy children, historically in late summer and early fall. Reasonable actions include emergency evaluation for sudden limb weakness and keeping polio vaccination current. The central uncertainty is why the link to EV-D68 weakened and whether cases will peak again. The next expected development is CDC's next annual surveillance update. Abstract Disseminated viral infections in immunocompetent adults are uncommon and may mimic autoimmune or bacterial conditions, including Behçet’s disease and disseminated herpes simplex virus (HSV) infection. We report a case of a 33-year-old immunocompetent woman who developed severe, painful vulvar ulcerations, oral ulcers, and a painful maculopapular rash involving both lower extremities that evolved into pustules. She was afebrile and hemodynamically stable but demonstrated a marked systemic inflammatory response, including leukocytosis (white blood cell count: 21.3×109/L; 85% neutrophils) and elevated inflammatory markers (erythrocyte sedimentation rate: 42 mm/h; C-reactive protein: 15.4 mg/dL). Extensive infectious and autoimmune evaluation was unrevealing, and imaging showed no drainable pelvic fluid collection. She received empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics and was transitioned to valacyclovir for suspected disseminated HSV infection; leukocytosis improved rapidly with persistently sterile cultures. Subsequent serologic testing demonstrated elevated Coxsackievirus A and B titers (≥1:100), supporting an enteroviral etiology in the appropriate clinical context; however, the limitations of serologic testing preclude definitive confirmation of acute infection in the absence of paired titers or lesion-based polymerase chain reaction testing. She was transferred to a tertiary center for dermatologic evaluation due to the absence of local specialty services, where skin biopsy demonstrated nonspecific inflammatory changes with negative direct immunofluorescence, arguing against immune complex-mediated vasculitis. This case highlights a rare adult presentation of Coxsackievirus infection with prominent genital and oral ulcerations mimicking HSV and Behçet’s disease, underscoring the importance of considering viral etiologies in sterile inflammatory mucocutaneous eruptions to avoid unnecessary antimicrobial exposure. Introduction Enteroviruses, including Coxsackieviruses, classically cause self-limited febrile illness and hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD) in children. However, adult infections are increasingly recognized and may present with more severe and atypical mucocutaneous manifestations than traditionally described, including disseminated involvement characterized by widespread cutaneous and mucosal disease extending beyond the classic palmoplantar distribution [1-3]. Outbreaks associated with Coxsackievirus A6 have demonstrated an expanded clinical spectrum, including vesiculobullous eruptions and widespread cutaneous involvement [1,2,4]. Early recognition of these atypical presentations is important to support antimicrobial stewardship by avoiding unnecessary broad-spectrum antibiotic and antiviral exposure. Acute painful oral and genital ulcerations accompanied by systemic inflammation in an immunocompetent host can closely resemble disseminated herpes simplex virus (HSV) infection, sexually transmitted infections, drug eruptions, or inflammatory disorders, such as Behçet's disease, a chronic systemic vasculitis characterized by recurrent oral and genital ulcerations with potential ocular, vascular, and neurologic involvement [5,6]. This diagnostic overlap may prompt extensive testing and empiric antimicrobial therapy before viral etiologies are considered. We describe an immunocompetent adult presenting with painful oral and vulvar ulcerations and a pustular lower-extremity eruption initially concerning for disseminated HSV infection and Behçet's disease, later supported by elevated Coxsackievirus serologies. Although several reports have documented atypical adult Coxsackievirus presentations, including widespread vesiculobullous eruptions, purpuric lesions, and eczema coxsackium, reports of adult enteroviral infection presenting with severe genital and oral ulcerations, pustular lower-extremity lesions, and a clinical picture closely resembling both disseminated herpes simplex virus infection and Behçet's disease have not been previously described [3]. Case Presentation A 33-year-old immunocompetent woman with no significant past medical history presented with severe vulvar pain and mucocutaneous lesions. Symptoms began approximately one week prior with vulvar burning that progressed to severe, painful labial ulcerations. Over the subsequent 48 h, she developed painful oral mucosal ulcerations and a maculopapular rash involving both lower extremities that evolved into tender pustules. The eruption spared the trunk, palms, and soles. She reported malaise and chills prior to admission but was uncertain about documented fever. She denied recent sexual activity, prior sexually transmitted infections, autoimmune disease, or similar dermatologic episodes. Recent travel included a trip to Toronto, though symptoms preceded travel. She was not immunocompromised and was not receiving immunosuppressive therapy. On presentation, she was afebrile and hemodynamically stable. Physical examination revealed irregular vulvar and perineal ulcerations with whitish discoloration and no purulent drainage, along with multiple shallow, round-to-oval oral ulcers with erythematous borders involving the buccal mucosa, ranging from approximately 3 to 5 mm in diameter. The lower-extremity lesions consisted of a symmetric maculopapular eruption with pustular evolution (Figures 1, 2). No lymphadenopathy was appreciated. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated marked leukocytosis (white blood cell count: 21.3×109/L with 85% neutrophils) and elevated inflammatory markers, including an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 42 mm/h and C-reactive protein of 15.4 mg/dL (Table 1). Renal and hepatic function were normal. Urinalysis showed pyuria; however, urine and blood cultures remained sterile. Computed tomography of the pelvis demonstrated an intrauterine device in situ without evidence of a drainable fluid collection. Given concern for severe bacterial infection in the setting of systemic inflammation, empiric therapy with vancomycin, meropenem, and clindamycin was initiated. Due to persistent concern for disseminated HSV infection, valacyclovir was added. Comprehensive infectious testing was negative, including HIV, treponemal serologies, gonorrhea and chlamydia testing, trichomonas testing, Candida polymerase chain reaction, and negative serum HSV-1/2 polymerase chain reaction. Autoimmune evaluation, including antinuclear antibody and HLA-B51 testing, was unrevealing (Table 2). Over the subsequent three days, leukocytosis improved from 21.3 to 8.7×109/L with continued clinical stability and persistently negative cultures (Table 3). Empiric antibacterial therapy was administered for approximately three days and discontinued following improvement in leukocytosis and persistently sterile cultures. Supportive care included topical analgesics and local wound care for the mucocutaneous lesions. Because dermatology services were unavailable locally, the patient was transferred to a tertiary care center for further evaluation and consideration of a biopsy. Subsequent serologic testing demonstrated elevated Coxsackievirus A (A7-A24) and B (B1-B6) titers (≥1:100), suggesting recent enteroviral exposure in the appropriate clinical context. Following the transfer, dermatologic evaluation was performed. A skin biopsy obtained from a representative lower-extremity lesion demonstrated nonspecific inflammatory changes without evidence of leukocytoclastic vasculitis or viral cytopathic effect. Frozen section confirmed adequate tissue sampling for direct immunofluorescence, which was negative for all immunoreactants, including IgA, IgG, IgM, C3, albumin, and fibrinogen, arguing against immune complex-mediated vasculitis. During that hospitalization, the patient also developed transient bilateral ankle arthralgia with mild swelling, consistent with an acute inflammatory process. No erosive changes or features suggestive of chronic inflammatory arthritis were identified. The patient was subsequently contacted for follow-up and provided the final pathological results, which confirmed these findings. At follow-up, the vulvar and oral ulcerations had resolved, and the lower-extremity eruption had faded without scarring. No recurrence of symptoms was reported. Discussion Disseminated Coxsackievirus infections in immunocompetent adults are rare and can be diagnostically challenging due to overlap with more common infectious and inflammatory conditions. Although hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD) is classically considered a pediatric illness, adult cases are increasingly recognized and may present with atypical or severe mucocutaneous involvement [3-7]. Reported adult manifestations include vesiculobullous eruptions, eczema coxsackium, and widespread cutaneous disease, reflecting an expanded clinical spectrum beyond classic pediatric presentations. In this case, the presence of painful oral and genital ulcerations with systemic inflammation appropriately raised concern for disseminated HSV infection and inflammatory disorders such as Behçet's disease. Disseminated HSV remains an important diagnostic consideration given its potential severity and need for prompt antiviral therapy [8]. Behçet's disease is characterized by recurrent oral and genital ulcerations with possible multisystem involvement; however, the absence of prior episodes, lack of ocular or neurologic findings, and negative autoimmune evaluation made a primary inflammatory disorder less likely in this patient [9,10]. Additionally, dermatopathological evaluation at the tertiary center demonstrated nonspecific inflammatory changes and negative direct immunofluorescence, arguing against immune complex-mediated vasculitis and further reducing concern for an underlying autoimmune vasculitis. The clinical distinction among these entities can be further clarified by their characteristic features. Disseminated HSV typically presents with grouped vesicles on an erythematous base that evolve into shallow ulcerations, often with positive viral PCR or culture and characteristic multinucleated giant cells on biopsy. Behçet's disease is defined by recurrent episodes of oral and genital ulcerations, often accompanied by ocular inflammation, pathergy, or neurologic involvement, with biopsy typically showing neutrophilic vasculitis. In contrast, the present case demonstrated a single episode of oral and genital ulcerations with a pustular lower-extremity eruption, negative HSV PCR, negative autoimmune markers, no prior episodes, and biopsy findings showing nonspecific perivascular inflammation without vasculitis or viral cytopathic effect, a pattern more consistent with a self-limited viral process than with either HSV or Behçet's disease. Beyond HSV and Behçet's disease, several other entities warrant consideration in the differential diagnosis of acute oral and genital ulcerations. Lipschütz ulcer is characterized by the sudden onset of painful, necrotic vulvar ulcerations in young women, often preceded by a flu-like prodrome, and has been linked to Epstein-Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, and Mycoplasma species. Like the present case, Lipschütz ulcers are a diagnosis of exclusion, demonstrate nonspecific histopathology, and resolve spontaneously within two to four weeks. However, Lipschütz ulcers typically present with deep necrotic ulcerations rather than the pustular lower-extremity eruption observed in our patient and are not typically associated with enteroviral infection. Mycoplasma pneumoniae-induced rash and mucositis (MIRM) is another important consideration, characterized by prominent oral, ocular, and urogenital mucositis with sparse cutaneous involvement, typically following respiratory symptoms. The absence of respiratory symptoms, negative findings for Mycoplasma, and the prominent pustular cutaneous eruption in our patient argued against MIRM. Erythema multiforme, which may present with oral and genital mucosal erosions and targetoid cutaneous lesions, was also considered; however, the absence of classic target lesions and the pustular rather than vesiculobullous morphology of the skin eruption made this diagnosis less likely. The clinical overlap among these entities underscores the diagnostic challenge posed by acute mucocutaneous ulcerations and the importance of a systematic approach to exclude infectious, inflammatory, and reactive etiologies [3,7]. Although Coxsackievirus serologic testing has limitations, including cross-reactivity and the inability to definitively confirm acute infection without paired titers or lesion-based polymerase chain reaction, elevated titers in the setting of a compatible clinical syndrome and exclusion of alternative etiologies support an enteroviral cause. Paired acute and convalescent titers were not obtained in this case, which would have strengthened the serologic evidence by demonstrating a fourfold rise in antibody levels. Additionally, lesion-based PCR was not performed for either enterovirus or HSV, as dermatology services and specialized virologic testing were unavailable at the initial facility; the patient required transfer to a tertiary center for biopsy alone. Notably, HSV-1 and HSV-2 PCR testing was performed on serum rather than from lesion swabs, which may reduce diagnostic sensitivity for mucocutaneous HSV infection, as lesion-based PCR is the preferred method for detecting active cutaneous or genital herpes simplex virus infection. The absence of definitive virologic confirmation, either through lesion PCR or paired serologies, represents the principal diagnostic limitation of this report. When available, lesion polymerase chain reaction testing may further improve diagnostic certainty. This case underscores the importance of considering viral etiologies in immunocompetent adults presenting with sterile inflammatory mucocutaneous eruptions and systemic inflammatory responses. Early recognition of atypical enteroviral infections may facilitate timely antimicrobial de-escalation, reduce unnecessary broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure, and mitigate medication-related adverse effects, supporting antimicrobial stewardship efforts [11]. Conclusions Coxsackievirus infection, classically associated with pediatric hand-foot-and-mouth disease, can present atypically in immunocompetent adults with severe mucocutaneous involvement and a marked systemic inflammatory response. In this case, prominent genital and oral ulcerations with leukocytosis and elevated inflammatory markers closely mimicked disseminated HSV infection and inflammatory disorders such as Behçet's disease, creating substantial diagnostic uncertainty. The absence of positive bacterial cultures, negative herpes simplex virus testing, unremarkable autoimmune evaluation, and nondiagnostic biopsy findings, combined with elevated Coxsackievirus serologic titers in a compatible clinical context, supported but did not definitively confirm an enteroviral etiology. Recognition of this atypical presentation is important to avoid unnecessary antimicrobial exposure, reduce invasive diagnostic interventions, and support appropriate supportive care. Increased awareness of adult enteroviral infections may improve diagnostic accuracy and reinforce antimicrobial stewardship efforts in similar clinical scenarios. What AFM Is and Why It Is Confirmed So Carefully AFM is a rare neurologic condition that affects the spinal cord's gray matter and predominantly strikes previously healthy children. Weakness comes on fast, within hours to a few days, in one or more limbs, and tends to be more pronounced closer to the trunk than at the hands and feet. In severe cases, it progresses to respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation, and it can leave permanent paralysis. Confirmation involves three things working together. A clinical picture of sudden flaccid limb weakness. An MRI showing a lesion largely restricted to the spinal cord gray matter across one or more segments.
