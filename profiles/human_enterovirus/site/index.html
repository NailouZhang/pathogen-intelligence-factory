作者： Kazuma Okada, Junki Hirano, Takumi Adachi, Takafumi Noguchi, Kentaro Uemura, Chikako Ono, Shogo Nagata, Yuki Yamamoto, Tetsuharu Nagamoto, Shuhei Taguwa

A versatile and stable reporter platform for human rhinovirus and other enteroviruses

Authors: Kazuma Okada, Junki Hirano, Takumi Adachi, Takafumi Noguchi, Kentaro Uemura, Chikako Ono, Shogo Nagata, Yuki Yamamoto, Tetsuharu Nagamoto, Shuhei Taguwa
Original abstract (English)
ABSTRACT Human rhinovirus (HRV), a major cause of upper respiratory infections, has increasingly been associated with lower respiratory tract disease. Although tools to investigate HRV pathogenesis are needed, they remain limited. Reporter viruses expressing fluorescent or luminescent proteins are powerful tools for analyzing viral distribution and replication; however, insertion of a reporter gene often impairs viral replication, and the inserted gene is readily lost during serial passage. To address these problems, we established a versatile and stable strategy for generating reporter HRV and other enteroviruses. The gene encoding mScarlet3, a red fluorescent protein, was inserted between the 5′UTR and VP4 gene, a site previously used in other enteroviruses but not in HRV. Optimizing the 2A cleavage sequence between mScarlet3 and VP4 yielded a reporter HRV with growth kinetics comparable to those of the wild-type virus. To improve the genetic stability of the reporter insert, we evaluated GC-content optimization of the reporter gene and relocation of the cis-acting replication element (CRE). GC-content optimization substantially improved reporter stability in both HRV-B14 and EV-A71, whereas CRE relocation provided additional stabilization in EV-A71 but not in HRV-B14, demonstrating the potential applicability of this strategy to other enteroviruses. This strategy for constructing stable reporter HRV and other enteroviruses will facilitate studies of enterovirus pathogenesis. IMPORTANCE Human rhinovirus (HRV) has lacked a reporter system expressing widely used reporter proteins, such as GFP, that enables visualization of viral dynamics in infected cells and tissues. The only reporter HRV reported to date loses fluorescence upon fixation and tolerates only small inserts, limiting its utility. Meanwhile, the clinical importance of HRV has become increasingly recognized, as reflected by its high detection rate among hospitalized children and recent outbreaks associated with fatal cases. A versatile and stable strategy for constructing reporter HRV is therefore urgently needed. Here, we overcome the two major obstacles to generating stable reporter HRV - impaired viral replication and loss of the inserted gene - and demonstrate that this strategy is also applicable to other enteroviruses. By providing a broadly applicable framework for constructing stable reporter viruses, this work establishes a foundation for visualizing infection dynamics and studying the pathogenesis of HRV and other enteroviruses.

Complement-related gene polymorphisms and severity of Coxsackievirus A6-induced hand, foot, and mouth disease in chinese han children: a candidate-gene association study

Authors: Ying Li, Zi-Jie Li, Guang-Cai Duan, Shou-Hang Chen, Yao-Dong Zhang, Fang Wang, Bo-Wen Dai, Yue-Fei Jin, 段广才
Original abstract (English)
Severe hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD) remains an important threat to child health. Host genetic factors, particularly those involved in complement activation, may contribute to HFMD susceptibility, but the role of complement-related gene polymorphisms in disease severity remains unclear. We enrolled 189 children with Coxsackievirus A6 (CVA6)-induced HFMD from Henan Children’s Hospital, including 94 severe and 95 mild cases. Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes using a modified phenol-chloroform method, and SNP genotyping was performed by PCR-RFLP. Crude and multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for age group and sex were used to evaluate associations between SNP genotypes and severe HFMD. In models adjusted for age group and sex, nominal associations with severe CVA6-induced HFMD were observed for C5 rs3761847 GA versus GG (adjusted OR = 2.523, 95% CI: 1.036–6.143), C3 rs2250656 TC versus TT (adjusted OR = 1.982, 95% CI: 1.061–3.704), and CFH rs1065489 TT versus GG (adjusted OR = 3.125, 95% CI: 1.249–7.818) and GT versus GG (adjusted OR = 2.499, 95% CI: 1.118–5.588). However, none of these associations remained statistically significant after Bonferroni correction for multiple testing. The combined SNP model showed limited discriminatory performance, with an apparent AUC of 0.653 and a bootstrap optimism-corrected AUC of 0.631. Exploratory genotype-related differences were also observed in selected laboratory parameters. C5, C3, and CFH variants showed nominal associations with severe CVA6-induced HFMD in this exploratory candidate-gene study, but these findings did not remain statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons. Further validation in larger independent cohorts is required.

First identified in California in 1962, it was initially reported infrequently. However, major outbreaks, particularly in 2014, established EV-D68 as an important cause of respiratory illness, especially among children. Unlike many enteroviruses associated with gastrointestinal or neurological diseases, EV-D68 primarily affects the respiratory tract. Infection may cause mild cold-like symptoms, but some patients develop severe respiratory distress, particularly children with asthma or other underlying respiratory conditions. EV-D68 has also been associated with acute flaccid myelitis (AFM), a rare neurological syndrome that can cause sudden weakness in the arms or legs. Routes of Spread EV-D68 spreads primarily through respiratory secretions and close interpersonal contact. Important transmission routes include: • Respiratory droplets: Secretions released during coughing and sneezing can expose people nearby. • Direct contact: Contact with nasal mucus or saliva from an infected person may transfer the virus. • Contaminated hands and surfaces: Touching contaminated objects and subsequently touching the eyes, nose, or mouth may contribute to transmission. • Close-contact environments: Homes, schools, childcare facilities, and healthcare settings may facilitate spread when preventive measures are inadequate. People with mild symptoms or no noticeable symptoms may contribute to transmission, making case identification difficult. The relative contribution of different transmission routes is not fully established, and EV-D68 should not be assumed to spread in exactly the same way as every other enterovirus. Strains and Genetic Diversity EV-D68 is genetically diverse, with major clades A, B, C, and D identified through molecular classification, primarily using the VP1 gene. Clade B, including subclades B1, B2, and B3, has been prominent in several outbreaks, while clade distribution varies geographically and over time. Prevalence and Global Distribution EV-D68 occurs internationally, but its true prevalence is difficult to determine. Many infections are mild or asymptomatic, and routine respiratory testing may not distinguish EV-D68 from other enteroviruses or rhinoviruses. Consequently, reported laboratory detections represent only part of the total burden of infection. Exposure to EV-D68 is widespread. Antibodies against the virus increase with age, and some study populations have approached 100% seropositivity by approximately 20 years of age. However, seropositivity indicates previous immune recognition; it does not establish the number of current infections or guarantee complete protection. In temperate climates, EV-D68 activity often increases during summer and autumn, although infections can occur throughout the year. The timing of peaks varies geographically. Symptoms and Clinical Presentation The severity of EV-D68 infection varies considerably. Mild symptoms include: • Runny or blocked nose. • Sneezing and cough. • Sore throat. • Muscle and body aches. • Fever, which may be absent. More serious respiratory manifestations include: • Wheezing and worsening asthma. • Rapid or labored breathing. • Lower respiratory tract infection. • Pneumonia and, in severe cases, respiratory failure. Infants, children, and adolescents are important affected groups because they may have less prior immunity. Children with asthma or other underlying medical conditions may face a greater risk of severe respiratory illness. Nevertheless, severe infection is possible in people without known chronic disease. Complications EV-D68 is important because it can increase pediatric hospital admissions, strain respiratory care services, and cause serious complications. One of the most concerning complications is acute flaccid myelitis (AFM), a rare neurological syndrome involving spinal cord gray matter. It can cause sudden weakness in one or more limbs, reduced muscle tone and reflexes, pain, swallowing difficulties, facial weakness, or slurred speech. Neurological symptoms may develop after a respiratory illness rather than at the same time. EV-D68 has been strongly associated with AFM, but most infected individuals do not develop it, and not every AFM case is caused by EV-D68. The broader public health priorities are early recognition, outbreak investigation, molecular surveillance, protection of children with asthma, and prompt evaluation of respiratory distress or sudden limb weakness. COVID-19's Impact on Enterovirus D68 The COVID-19 pandemic severely disrupted the natural transmission and biennial seasonal cycles of Enterovirus D68 (EV-D68) globally. During the height of the pandemic, public health measures like social distancing and mask mandates successfully suppressed the virus, causing case counts and genetic diversity to drop to historic lows. However, this prolonged lack of exposure created a significant "immunity gap" in the pediatric population. When pandemic restrictions were lifted, the virus rebounded sharply, causing atypical, out-of-season resurgences that shifted traditional biennial timelines and triggered widespread respiratory illness in children. Additionally, these post-pandemic changes heavily altered the predictability of Acute Flaccid Myelitis (AFM). Why Is There No Vaccine Against EV-D68? Developing an EV-D68 vaccine faces scientific and practical challenges, but vaccination remains a potential future strategy. Genetic diversity: Multiple viral clades complicate the development of vaccines that provide broad, durable protection. Uncertain protective immunity: Researchers still need to establish which immune responses prevent infection, severe respiratory disease, and neurological complications. Clinical trial challenges: Severe complications are uncommon, and unpredictable seasonal outbreaks make vaccine efficacy studies difficult and costly. Research and funding: Sustained investment is needed to develop, test, and manufacture effective vaccines. Continued research into vaccine candidates and protective immunity is essential. Prevention No specific vaccine is currently available to prevent EV-D68 infection. Prevention therefore depends on reducing exposure and recognizing illness early. Recommended measures include: 1. Hand hygiene: Wash hands thoroughly with soap and water, particularly after coughing, sneezing, or wiping the nose. 2. Respiratory hygiene: Cover coughs and sneezes with a tissue or the elbow and dispose of used tissues safely. 3. Surface cleaning: Regularly clean frequently touched objects, toys, and shared surfaces using appropriate products. 4. Reducing exposure: Avoid close contact with people who are ill, and stay home when sick when feasible. 5. Healthcare precautions: Apply appropriate standard, contact, and droplet precautions according to clinical infection-control guidance. 6. Asthma management: Ensure that children with asthma follow their prescribed treatment plans and have access to appropriate rescue medication. Difficulty breathing requires urgent medical assessment. Nominal signals that vanish after correction may reflect true effects that the study lacked the statistical power to confirm, or they may simply be chance findings that arise whenever many genetic variants are tested simultaneously in a finite sample. Distinguishing between these possibilities requires replication in independent cohorts, which is precisely what the authors call for. Beyond the individual SNP analyses, the team also evaluated whether a combined model incorporating multiple SNPs could discriminate severe from mild cases. The combined model showed limited discriminatory performance, achieving an apparent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.653, which declined to 0.631 after bootstrap-based optimism correction. An AUC of 0.5 indicates discrimination no better than chance, while 1.0 indicates perfect discrimination, so a corrected value of 0.631 suggests that complement-related variants alone, at least in this cohort, provide only modest predictive information about disease severity. This finding underscores a broader lesson in genetic epidemiology: complex clinical outcomes such as infection severity are rarely determined by a handful of variants in a single pathway, and even biologically plausible genetic associations often translate into weak predictive models when considered in isolation. The study also reported exploratory differences in selected laboratory parameters across genotype groups, hinting that complement variants might correlate with measurable differences in the host response to infection, although the authors frame these observations as preliminary. Such genotype-phenotype correlations, if confirmed, could eventually help explain the biological mechanism linking specific variants to clinical outcomes, for example by altering complement protein levels, activation efficiency, or regulation of the inflammatory response during enterovirus infection. Coxsackievirus A6 has emerged as a major cause of hand, foot, and mouth disease in China and elsewhere, and unlike enterovirus 71, for which vaccines have been developed and deployed, CVA6 has no licensed vaccine. Surveillance data have shown shifting patterns of enterovirus dominance, with CVA6 outbreaks producing atypical and sometimes severe presentations, including widespread eczema coxsackium in children with atopic dermatitis and occasional neurological involvement. Understanding the host-side determinants of severity therefore has practical value: if genetic risk factors could be reliably identified and validated, they might eventually inform risk stratification in clinical settings, helping clinicians identify which children warrant closer monitoring during outbreaks.
