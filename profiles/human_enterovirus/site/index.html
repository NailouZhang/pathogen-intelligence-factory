Enterovirus D68 in Europe, 2025: continent-wide predominance of subclade A2/D and shift from pediatric to adult cases

Authors: Robert Dyrdak, Gerog Navér, Nadia Neuner-Jehle, Alejandra Gonzalez Sanchez, Yemisi Adedeji, Lili Andersson-Li, Cristina Andrés, Andrés Antón, Brynja Armannsdottir, Kafayat Arowolo
Original abstract (English)
Abstract Background Enterovirus D68 (EV-D68) has predominantly caused infections in children, and detected cases have mainly been infected by subclade B3. In 2025, however, a substantial number of infections in adults was noted. Aim To detail the molecular epidemiology of the 2025 season in Europe. Methods Case and sequencing data was collected from members in the European Non-Polio Enterovirus Network (ENPEN). The participating sites included both clinical and public health laboratories. Results Data was collected from 13 countries at 23 sites. There were 187 clinical cases infected with EV-D68 and 127 detections in wastewater. Age data were available for 76% (143/187), among which 90 cases were adults. VP1-based genotyping showed subclade A2/D to predominate in all age groups and across all participating countries, as well as in the sewage sampling performed at one site. Two interclade recombinant sequences were detected among 22 whole genome sequences. Conclusion The observed shift towards adult cases and subclade A2/D predominance warrants continued monitoring to determine whether this represents a sustained epidemiological change. The detection of interclade recombinant variants highlights a need to supplement regular VP1-based genotyping with whole-genome sequencing.

Viral shedding and clinical differences in hand, foot and mouth disease associated with coxsackievirus A16 and coxsackievirus A10

Authors: Bai-Qun Wu, Ye Tong, Yin Wang, 杨慧敏, Hui Hang, Ming-Yang Lu, Liang-Jian Ling, Jing Wang, Huan Fan, Feng-Feng Liu
Original abstract (English)
Hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD) remains a major and persistent public health challenge in China and across many parts of Asia. Following widespread implementation of enterovirus A71 vaccination in China, coxsackievirus A10 (CVA10) and coxsackievirus A16 (CVA16) have emerged as the dominant serotypes associated with outbreaks in children. To investigate the viral shedding duration and clinical differences of CVA10 and CVA16 in HFMD outbreaks in Jiangsu Province, and to generate evidence for optimizing case management and outbreak control. In 2024, a total of 63 HFMD cases were prospectively followed daily to monitor symptom progression. Pharyngeal swabs were collected once at symptom onset and on days 3, 7, 10, and 14 post-onset from cases and close contacts. All specimens were tested by RT-PCR for nucleic acid detection and serotype identification. CVA10-infected children had a significantly longer duration (9.5 days) than those with CVA16 (8.0 days, P = 0.012). CVA16 infection correlated with more symptomatic cases, especially higher rash incidence, and increased healthcare-seeking behavior. Viral load dropped sharply from days 1–4, and symptom duration was positively correlated with viral shedding duration in symptomatic children ( r = 0.33); 91.7% of children tested negative within 5 days after symptom resolution. This study suggests that symptom resolution may provide a potential reference for evaluating isolation strategies, early monitoring is critical due to rapid viral load decline, and symptom duration may serve as an indicator of viral RNA shedding duration. Given CVA10’s prolonged viral shedding and CVA16’s symptom-driven transmission, serotype-specific and tailored management strategies may be valuable for HFMD control. However, infectivity was not directly assessed, and these findings should be further validated using viral culture.

DDX58 基因敲低对 EV-A71 复制和 RIG-I 介导的 抗病毒药物 调节的影响

Effects of DDX58 Gene Knockdown on EV-A71 Replication and Modulation of RIG-I–Mediated Antiviral
作者： Guo-E Gou, Mei Li, Meng Zhang, Xin-Yu Wang, Wen Zhang, Song Zhai, Mu-Qi Wang, Chen-Rui Liu, Ting Li, Ya-Ping Li
摘要中文翻译摘要 目的:本研究的目的是探讨 DDX58 (RIG-I) 在 EV-A71 复制中的作用及其对 RIG-I 介导的 抗病毒药物 信号反应的影响。方法:通过在 RD 细胞中转染 siRNA,然后感染 EV-A71,实现 DDX58 敲低。通过 qRT-PCR 和蛋白质印迹评估病毒复制和 宿主 抗病毒药物 反应。通过ELISA测定干扰素(IFN-α和IFN-β)和CXCL-10的分泌。结果:DDX58 敲低显着减少了 EV-A71 复制,病毒 RNA 水平和 VP1 蛋白表达降低表明。此外,关键 抗病毒药物 信号分子(包括 IRF3、IRF7 和 CXCL-10)的表达在感染后 24 小时显示短暂增加,随后在 36 小时下降。一致地,IFN-α和IFN-β水平表现出类似的时间依赖性模式,早期升高,随后降低。结论:这些发现表明 DDX58 可能对 EV-A71 复制和 抗病毒药物 信号传导发挥上下文和时间依赖性调节作用。尽管 DDX58 被敲低,但观察到的下游通路的短暂激活表明补偿性先天免疫机制的潜在参与。需要进一步的研究来阐明潜在的分子相互作用。

Authors: Yi Zhang, Zhi Li, 谷雨, Bo Huang, Zhen-Zhuang Zou, Yu Gu
Original abstract (English)
Enterovirus A71 (EV-A71) is an important cause of severe neurological complications of hand, foot, and mouth disease in children. Most infections remain confined to peripheral sites, whereas only a small proportion progress to central nervous system (CNS) disease, and the mechanisms underlying this transition remain incompletely understood. Here, we reappraise EV-A71 neuropathogenesis as a multistage process comprising peripheral replication, systemic dissemination, CNS entry, establishment of productive CNS infection, and subsequent tissue injury. This distinction is important because these events are mechanistically connected but not interchangeable. Current evidence supports both neurovascular and peripheral neural routes of CNS entry, although the nature and strength of the supporting evidence differ. Human-derived blood–brain barrier (BBB) models demonstrate that EV-A71 and virus-associated carriers can engage and cross neurovascular interfaces, whereas temporal studies in animals, perturbation of axonal transport, and compartmentalized neuronal models provide stronger causal support for retrograde axonal transport once the virus has accessed peripheral nerves. These findings establish mechanistic feasibility, but the timing, necessity, and relative contribution of each route during natural infection in children remain unresolved. We further emphasize that BBB crossing is not synonymous with BBB disruption, and that CNS entry does not necessarily result in productive CNS infection. On this basis, we propose a multistage progression-constraint model, in which progression to severe neurological disease requires EV-A71 to overcome distinct constraints imposed by tissue context, viral properties, and host factors at successive stages. This framework provides a testable explanation for why most infections remain peripheral while only a minority progress to severe neurological disease, and highlights the need for longitudinal clinical studies and directly comparable experimental models to identify where disease trajectories first diverge.

The study aimed to analyze the epidemiological characteristics of severe hand, foot and mouth disease (HFMD) cases caused by Coxsackievirus A6 (CV-A6) and to investigate the genetic characteristics of some CV-A6 strains in southern Vietnam from 2022 to 2025. Scope and question
This review investigates the association between Enterovirus D68 (EV-D68) and plastic bronchitis in children, focusing on the clinical presentation, diagnostic methods, and treatment outcomes of a rare case. The central question is whether EV-D68 can cause plastic bronchitis and severe pneumonia, expanding the known clinical spectrum of this infection.

The review is based on a case report and a systematic literature search conducted using PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science databases from January 2006 to June 2026. The search strategy included the keywords 'Enterovirus D68', 'plastic bronchitis', and 'severe pneumonia'. The case report confirms that EV-D68 can induce plastic bronchitis in previously healthy school-aged children, leading to airway obstruction, respiratory distress, and even respiratory failure. Controversies and gaps
The review notes that while EV-D68 has been associated with acute flaccid myelitis (AFM), its potential to trigger severe lower airway disease with plastic cast formation remains underappreciated. The clinical significance of recognizing plastic bronchitis in the context of EV-D68 outbreaks includes the need for early bronchoscopic intervention for cast removal and airway clearance, which directly impacts survival. Despite decades of research, no virus-specific therapy against this broad group of pathogens has been approved by regulatory agencies, leaving clinicians to manage infections ranging from mild respiratory illness to myocarditis, encephalitis and acute flaccid myelitis with supportive care alone. A new computational study published in BMC Methods now reports a detailed molecular modelling campaign on novel derivatives of pleconaril, one of the most extensively studied capsid-binding antienteroviral candidates, in an effort to identify promising lead compounds for future drug development. The research, led by Chetan Palvi, Snehal Ukhade, Isha S. Ingle and Hemchandra K. Chaudhari with Sanchali S. Gadade, assembled a ligand library of twenty-four novel pleconaril derivatives drawn from the literature, each with reported antienteroviral activity. Using the PHASE module of the Schrödinger modelling suite, the team built atom-based and field-based three-dimensional quantitative structure-activity relationship (3D-QSAR) models, generated a common pharmacophore hypothesis, docked every compound into the capsid protein of enterovirus D68 and estimated binding free energies with the MM/GBSA method. Molecular properties and absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion parameters were assessed with the QikProp utility and SwissADME. The biological target was chosen with care. The crystal structure of human enterovirus D68 in complex with pleconaril, deposited in the Protein Data Bank under the identifier 4WM7, was resolved at 2.32 angstroms and imported into Maestro for preparation. Bound ligands, cofactors and water molecules were removed, side chains that did not participate in the binding cavity were neutralised with the OPLS_2005 force field, and hydrogen atoms were added. The quality of the prepared structure was checked with a Ramachandran plot before docking grids were generated around the centroid of the co-crystallised ligand. Enteroviruses belong to the Enterovirus genus of the Picornaviridae family and comprise roughly one hundred serotypes across species A to D. This diversity is a major obstacle to vaccination: the only vaccines currently available target poliomyelitis caused by polioviruses, two inactivated enterovirus A71 vaccines used in China against hand-foot-and-mouth disease, and a vaccine against the foot-and-mouth disease virus of livestock. Small molecules that attack conserved steps of the viral life cycle therefore address a significant unmet clinical need, and the capsid’s hydrophobic pocket has long been viewed as an attractive point of attack. That pocket normally holds a fatty acid known as the pocket factor, which is expelled when the virus attaches to a host cell. The resulting conformational changes in the capsid facilitate the release of the viral genomic RNA. Capsid-binding inhibitors compete with the pocket factor for occupancy of the hydrophobic cavity and can block viral attachment or uncoating. Pleconaril, vapendavir and pocapavir are among the most extensively studied candidates exploiting this mechanism, yet none has reached clinical approval, which is precisely why the authors focused their modelling effort on pleconaril analogues. The clinical setbacks in this drug class are instructive. Pleconaril was not approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for treating the common cold because of adverse effects including headache, diarrhoea and nausea, its induction of the cytochrome P-450 enzyme CYP3A4, which accelerates the metabolism of ethinyl estradiol, and the emergence of pleconaril-resistant viruses. In February 2017, vapendavir failed in a phase IIb trial owing to insufficient efficacy against rhinoviruses compared with placebo. In a randomised, blinded, placebo-controlled study of poliovirus-infected patients treated with pocapavir, resistance was recorded in 44 percent of the treated group versus 10 percent of the placebo group. Developing effective, selective and broad-spectrum antienteroviral agents remains a key focus for medicinal chemists. Against this backdrop, the docking results provide the study’s headline numbers. The 12b complex with 4WM7 also yielded a favourable Prime MM/GBSA binding free energy of minus 111.54 kilocalories per mole, which the authors report as superior to the complexes formed with the reference standards. Structural analysis of the docked poses indicated pi-pi stacking interactions between the isoxazole ring of the derivatives and the residue TYR193 within the binding site. Validation of the docking protocol was built into the workflow. The co-crystallised native ligand was re-docked into its binding site, and the resulting root-mean-square-deviation values of less than 2 angstroms confirmed that the procedure reproduced the experimentally observed binding mode. Scoring was performed in Glide’s Extra Precision mode, and all twenty-four compounds were allowed to dock into the same cavity. Swiss Target Prediction was then applied to the best-docked molecule, 12b, suggesting probable engagement of four target families: kinases at 60 percent, enzymes at 13.3 percent, and G-protein coupled receptors and proteases at 6.7 percent each. The pharmacophore work distilled the activity data into a spatial template. Ligands with a pIC50 value above 6.3 were classed as active and those below as inactive, yielding seventeen active and seven inactive compounds. Twenty hypotheses were generated, and the top-ranked six-point model, designated AHHRRR_1, was selected on the basis of a site score of 0.917, a phase hyposcore of 1.337 and a survival score of 5.610. The model combines one hydrogen bond acceptor feature, two hydrophobic features and three aromatic ring features, and every active compound in the dataset could be aligned onto it, offering a blueprint for designing future analogues. Leave-one-out cross-validation was used to assess stability and robustness, while RMSE, Q2 and Pearson’s r values for the test set quantified predictive power. The contour maps localise the structure-activity signals: blue cubes around the 5-amidoisoxazole moiety suggest that electron-withdrawing capability there may benefit activity, whereas red cubes over the pyridine region warn that further electron-withdrawing substitution could hinder it. In the field-based maps, a favoured electropositive zone surrounds the carboxylic portion attached to the isoxazole ring, a favourable steric region encircles the pyridine ring, and hydrophobic substitution near the pyridine core correlates with intermediate activity. The ADME screening added a drug-like filter to the computational picture. QikProp calculations showed that all compounds, with a few exceptions, fell within reasonable ranges for molecular properties, the rule-of-five values lay within acceptable limits, and the predicted percentage of human oral absorption exceeded 85 percent for most molecules. One outlier illustrates the practical limits of such predictions: molecule 12c, the weakest docked compound, contains an ester and an imine that can hydrolyse at high or low pH, so QikProp could not produce data for it, and the profile of the second-to-last molecule, 19, was reported instead. The authors are careful to frame the work as computational hypothesis generation rather than proof of therapeutic value. Docking scores, MM/GBSA energies and QSAR statistics rank and rationalise compounds against a single crystal structure; they do not demonstrate antiviral efficacy in infected cells, animals or patients, and the twenty-four-compound dataset is modest by QSAR standards. The value of the study lies in the tools it leaves behind: validated atom-based and field-based 3D-QSAR models, a six-point pharmacophore that all active ligands satisfy, and a comparative docking map of the capsid pocket that together could guide the design of the next generation of pleconaril derivatives as candidate antienteroviral medicines. With hand, foot, and mouth disease, the answer isn’t always as simple as looking at the rash. Some warning signs can be easy to miss, especially in the early stages. So, what should parents watch for, and how can they care for their child safely at home? In today’s Doctor at Home, pediatrician Dr. Nguyen Tri Minh explains how to recognize warning signs, care for a sick child, and help prevent the spread of hand, foot, and mouth disease. Reporter: Hello, Dr. Tri Minh. It’s great to have you with us today. Let’s start with a question many parents have: What does a mild case of hand, foot, and mouth disease typically look like in children? Dr. Tri Minh: Most cases of hand, foot and mouth disease are mild. In fact, some cases are so mild that parents may not even realize their child has had the infection. The child may only have a low-grade fever, become a little irritable, eat or drink less than usual, or simply seem less active for a few days. Some children develop the typical painful mouth ulcers and small red spots or blisters, usually on the hands, feet, knees, or buttocks. But these skin lesions are not always very obvious. And this brings us to two common misunderstandings about hand, foot and mouth disease. The first is: “It’s usually a mild viral illness, so there’s nothing to worry about.” Yes, most children recover well, and some may even have such mild symptoms that the illness goes almost unnoticed. But a small proportion of children can progress to more serious disease, so parents still need to monitor the child closely during the illness. The second misunderstanding is: “The more blisters my child has, the more severe the disease must be.” That’s not true. Some children may have many obvious blisters and still have a mild illness, while another child may have only a few skin lesions but become more seriously unwell. So rather than counting the blisters, look at the child as a whole—how they’re behaving, eating and drinking, sleeping, and interacting with you. The number of blisters does not tell us how severe hand, foot, and mouth disease is. Reporter: Most cases are mild, so what changes should alert parents that the illness may be becoming more serious? Dr. Tri Minh: Parents should become concerned when their child starts behaving differently from usual, especially if there is frequent startling, persistent high fever, repeated vomiting, or unusual irritability or drowsiness. One sign that I particularly want parents to recognize is frequent startling or sudden jerking movements. If this happens more than once, especially while the child is awake, the child should be examined by a pediatrician. Fever also matters. A temperature above 39 degrees Celsius, fever lasting more than 48 hours, or persistent high fever despite appropriate fever management should not simply be watched at home. These signs don’t necessarily mean that severe complications have already developed, but they do mean that the child should be examined by a pediatrician rather than simply waiting at home. Reporter: What warning signs should parents watch for at home? Dr. Tri Minh: The major warning signs of severe disease involve the nervous system, breathing, and circulation. Neurologically, parents may notice tremors, unsteady walking, weakness, seizures, increasing drowsiness, or the child becoming less responsive. If breathing is affected, the child may breathe very quickly, have difficulty breathing, or develop an unusual or irregular breathing pattern. And if circulation becomes affected, the child may become very pale, sweaty, have cold hands and feet, or look extremely weak and unwell. If you see neurological symptoms, abnormal breathing, or signs of poor circulation, the child needs urgent medical care. Dr. Nguyen Tri Minh is a pediatrician with more than seven years of clinical experience caring for children. He has worked in pediatric care at District 8 Hospital and Xuyen A Tay Ninh General Hospital, and completed clinical rotations at Children’s Hospital 1 and Children’s Hospital 2 in Ho Chi Minh City with training in neonatology, respiratory medicine, infectious diseases, and pediatric emergency care. He currently serves as a Lecturer in Pediatrics at the School of Medicine, Tan Tao University. Dr. Minh holds a Doctor of Medicine degree and a Specialist Level I qualification in Pediatrics from Pham Ngoc Thach University of Medicine. Reporter: How should parents manage mouth sores and keep their child hydrated at home? Dr. Tri Minh: For a mild case, home care mainly comes down to two things: keeping the child comfortable and keeping them well hydrated. There is currently no specific antiviral treatment for uncomplicated hand, foot and mouth disease, so treatment is mainly supportive. For fever or painful mouth ulcers, parents can use paracetamol at the appropriate dose for the child’s weight. Because mouth ulcers can make eating and drinking painful, don’t force the child to finish a normal meal. Offer small amounts more frequently. Cool drinks, milk, and soft foods are easier for them to tolerate, while very hot, spicy, salty, or acidic foods may make the pain worse. What matters more in the short term is whether the child is drinking and urinating normally. A dry mouth, much less urine than usual, no tears when crying, or increasing tiredness may suggest dehydration. So don’t worry too much if your child eats less for a few days. Keeping fluids going is the priority. Reporter: What common mistakes should parents avoid when treating hand, foot and mouth disease at home? Dr. Tri Minh: The most common mistakes I see are unnecessary medication, over-treating the skin lesions, and forcing a child to eat. Hand, foot and mouth disease is caused by viruses, so antibiotics do not treat the disease unless there is a separate bacterial infection. Parents also don’t need to apply many different creams, powders, or home remedies to the blisters, and they shouldn’t intentionally break them. And one more thing: don’t become so focused on bringing the temperature back to normal that you forget to look at the child. How the child behaves, drinks, sleeps, and interacts with you often tells us much more than one number on the thermometer. Reporter: At what point does a child need immediate emergency medical care? Dr. Tri Minh: A child needs emergency medical care if they develop difficulty or abnormal breathing, have a seizure, become very weak or difficult to wake, lose consciousness, or show signs of poor circulation such as becoming very pale, cold, or unusually sweaty. At this stage, don’t wait for another dose of fever medicine to work, and don’t wait until the next morning. Take the child to the emergency room immediately. It’s always better to have a child assessed early than to wait until they become critically ill. Reporter: During an outbreak, what can families do to avoid infection at home, daycare, or school? Dr. Tri Minh: The most effective everyday measures are good handwashing, proper cleaning of contaminated objects and surfaces, and reducing contact with the child’s secretions and stool. Hand, foot and mouth disease spreads very easily among young children because they touch everything, share toys, and frequently put their hands in their mouths. So wash your hands and your child’s hands with soap under clean running water for at least 20 seconds, especially after using the toilet or changing diapers, and before preparing food or feeding a child. Toys, tables, door handles, and other frequently touched surfaces should also be cleaned and appropriately disinfected. Children should avoid sharing cups, spoons, towels, and other personal items while they are sick. And there’s one thing parents may not realize: even when the child looks better, the virus can continue to be shed in the stool for some time. So good hand hygiene, particularly after toileting or changing diapers, remains important even after the obvious symptoms have improved. Reporter: Many parents are curious about active prevention options, especially the hand, foot and mouth disease vaccine. Could you share your insights on its availability, effectiveness, and role in protecting children? Dr. Tri Minh: Yes. An EV-A71 vaccine is now available in Vietnam, and the clinical-trial data are very encouraging. The vaccine showed about 97 percent efficacy against hand, foot and mouth disease caused by EV-A71. But the words “caused by EV-A71” are very important. Because hand, foot, and mouth disease can be caused by several different enteroviruses, this vaccine does not prevent every case of the disease. A vaccinated child can still develop disease caused by other viruses. EV-A71 is an important target because it has been associated with some of the more serious neurological and cardiopulmonary complications. The vaccine is intended for young children, and protection is not immediate after the first injection, so parents should follow the recommended vaccination schedule. In clinical studies, most reported reactions were mild and temporary, such as pain or redness at the injection site, fever, irritability, or decreased appetite. So I see vaccination as an important additional layer of protection, especially against EV-A71, but it doesn’t replace handwashing and the other preventive measures. Reporter: Do you have any additional advice for parents caring for a child with hand, foot and mouth disease? Dr. Tri Minh: I would give parents three simple things to remember: First, don’t panic, but don’t underestimate the disease either. Most children recover well, but parents still need to watch them closely for any change in their general condition. Second, don’t judge the severity by the number of blisters. Pay more attention to how your child is behaving, drinking, sleeping, and breathing, and watch for warning signs such as frequent startling, persistent high fever, repeated vomiting, or unusual irritability or drowsiness. I think the message is quite simple: Watch the child, know the warning signs, and keep those little hands clean. Reporter: And that brings us to the end of today’s Doctor at Home. Many thanks to Dr. Nguyen Tri Minh for joining us and sharing his advice with parents.
