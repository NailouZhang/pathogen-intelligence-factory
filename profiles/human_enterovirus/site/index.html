Targeting the miR-3156-3p/K+ channel axis suppresses viral release and paralysis in enterovirus D68 infection.

Targeting the miR-3156-3p/K+ channel axis suppresses viral release and paralysis in enterovirus D68 infection.

Authors: Yueh-Te Lin, Yu-An Kung, Yu-Nong Gong, Huan-Jung Chiang, Kuei-Ching Hsiung, Hou-Chen Lee, Wen-Fang Tang, Chung-Guei Huang, Jim-Tong Horng, Shin-Ru Shih
Original abstract (English)
Viruses rely on egress machinery to exit cells, a step that is crucial for their transmission. While non-enveloped RNA viruses, such as enteroviruses, are typically associated with the lytic release pathway, emerging evidence suggests they can also be packaged within vesicles for non-lytic release. However, the molecular mechanisms that regulate viral release remain unclear. Here, we performed a genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screen in A549 cells and identified the microRNA hsa-miR-3156-1 as a critical host factor for enterovirus infection. Both the primary and mature forms of miR-3156-1 were upregulated following infection, with miR-3156-3p acting as a positive regulator of viral infection by directly targeting the 3'-untranslated regions of the K + channel-associated genes KCNV1, KCNJ2, KCNK3, and KCNK5, thereby causing plasma membrane depolarization and Ca 2+ influx, which increase viral release. Pre-administration of a miR-3156-3p antagomir alleviated virus-associated acute flaccid paralysis in an ICR neonatal mouse model. Our findings reveal how the miR-3156-3p/K + channel axis controls enterovirus release, highlighting potential prophylactic targets.

The study aimed to uncover the molecular mechanisms that regulate viral release in human enteroviruses, as this step is crucial for their transmission. The research was motivated by the lack of clarity regarding these mechanisms, particularly for non-polio enteroviruses like EV-D68.

The study was conducted in A549 cells and an ICR neonatal mouse model to investigate the role of miR-3156-3p in enterovirus infection. The research focused on the effects of miR-3156-3p on viral release and paralysis associated with enterovirus D68.
Core methods
A genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screen was performed in A549 cells to identify host factors involved in enterovirus infection. Additionally, the study used antagomirs to target miR-3156-3p and assessed their impact on viral release and acute flaccid paralysis in a mouse model.
Main results
The primary and mature forms of miR-3156-1 were upregulated following enterovirus D68 infection. miR-3156-3p was found to directly target the 3'-untranslated regions of K+ channel-associated genes, leading to plasma membrane depolarization and Ca2+ influx, which increased viral release. Pre-administration of a miR-3156-3p antagomir reduced virus-associated acute flaccid paralysis in the ICR neonatal mouse model.
Interpretation and novelty
The authors interpreted their findings as revealing a novel mechanism by which the miR-3156-3p/K+ channel axis regulates enterovirus release, offering potential prophylactic targets for future interventions.
Scientific and public-health significance
The findings provide insights into the molecular regulation of viral release in enterovirus D68 infection, which could inform the development of new therapeutic strategies for preventing viral spread and associated neurological complications.
Limitations and evidence strength
The study is limited by its use of a mouse model, which may not fully represent human physiology. The evidence is moderate, as the findings are based on in vitro and in vivo experiments without direct clinical validation.

Discovery of oxochromanyl–naphthoquinone hybrids as a new chemotype of selective Enterovirus A71 inhibitors

Discovery of oxochromanyl–naphthoquinone hybrids as a new chemotype of selective Enterovirus A71 inhibitors

Authors: Maria Chiara Cabua, Rebecca Piras, Luca Virdis, Marta Cogoni, Valeria Manca, Roberta Loddo, Valentina Onnis, Aldo Manzin, Giuseppina Sanna, Francesco Secci
Original abstract (English)
Abstract Enterovirus A71 (EV-A71) is a major causative agent of hand, foot, and mouth disease and is associated with severe neurological complications for which effective antiviral therapies remain unavailable. Inspired by the documented antiviral properties of both naphthoquinone and chromone-based scaffolds, we designed and synthesized a focused library of oxochromanyl–naphthoquinone hybrids through a tandem Michael addition–decarboxylation strategy. The resulting compounds were evaluated for cytotoxicity and antiviral activity against a panel of RNA viruses, including EV-A71, coxsackievirus B5 (CVB5), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and SARS-CoV-2. All derivatives exhibited low cytotoxicity in Vero-76 cells (CC50 ≥ 100 μM), while showing a remarkable degree of selectivity toward EV-A71. Structure–activity relationship analysis revealed that antiviral activity was retained across multiple substitution patterns, with several analogues displaying submicromolar potency, indicating that inhibition is a general feature of the oxochromanyl scaffold rather than a feature of a single compound. Several derivatives displayed submicromolar antiviral activity, with compound 3k emerging as the most potent analogue, exhibiting an EC50 value of 0.1 ± 0.01 μM and an estimated selectivity index of approximately 1000. Yield-reduction assays confirmed a dose-dependent suppression of viral production, resulting in a reduction of infectious virus titers of approximately two orders of magnitude. Furthermore, virucidal and time-of-addition experiments demonstrated that the antiviral activity of 3k is not associated with direct virion inactivation but is consistent with interference during the earliest stages of the EV-A71 replication cycle. Collectively, these findings identify oxochromanyl–naphthoquinone hybrids as a previously unexplored class of selective EV-A71 inhibitors and establish the oxochromanyl scaffold as a promising platform for the development of new anti-enteroviral agents.

This article reports that confirmed cases of acute flaccid myelitis (AFM) in the United States remained low from 2020 through 2025, despite increases in enterovirus D68 (EV-D68)–associated respiratory illnesses in 2022, 2024, and 2025. The CDC noted that while EV-D68 was linked to AFM peaks in 2014, 2016, and 2018, the recent years saw no corresponding rise in AFM cases. During the six-year period, 172 confirmed AFM cases were reported, ranging from 17 to 48 annually. Only one AFM specimen tested positive for EV-D68 in 2025. The CDC highlighted the need for continued surveillance due to the similarity of AFM to paralytic poliomyelitis and the limitations in viral detection, particularly when specimens are collected after the onset of symptoms. The findings suggest that EV-D68 circulation does not necessarily lead to AFM cases, and further research is needed to understand this relationship.

The condition is characterized by acute onset of flaccid weakness in one or more limbs and spinal cord gray matter abnormalities. Nationwide AFM surveillance began in 2014, when an increase in cases was first recognized. EV-D68 circulation was associated with biennial peaks in AFM cases in 2014, 2016, and 2018, when 120 to 238 cases were reported annually. During 2020 through 2025, however, confirmed AFM cases remained lower, ranging from 17 to 48 cases per year. The CDC received reports of 172 confirmed cases during the six-year period: 34 in 2020, 29 in 2021, 48 in 2022, 19 in 2023, 25 in 2024, and 17 in 2025. Although sentinel respiratory surveillance identified increases in EV-D68 detections in respiratory specimens from children with acute respiratory illnesses in 2022, 2024, and 2025, they were not accompanied by concurrent increases in AFM cases nationally. EV-D68 was detected in one specimen from a patient with AFM in 2025. “These findings indicate that increases in EV-D68–associated respiratory illnesses do not necessarily result in increases in AFM cases,” Gabe Alvarado, a CDC spokesperson, told Infectious Disease Special Edition. “Although EV-D68 is considered the primary cause of the peaks in AFM cases observed in 2014, 2016, and 2018, questions remain about the relationship between EV-D68 circulation and AFM,” Alvarado said. “During 2020-2025, increases in EV-D68–associated respiratory illnesses were not accompanied by increases in AFM cases. Additional research is needed to better understand this pattern, including whether changes in circulating EV-D68 strains or other factors may influence the risk for AFM.” What Are the Limitations? The investigators noted that additional AFM patients during the study period might have had EV-D68 infection, but viral detection can be limited by the timing of specimen collection. Specimens from patients with AFM are often collected after the onset of limb weakness rather than during the preceding illness, when viral detection may be more likely. Among patients with confirmed AFM and documented receipt of any polio vaccine doses, 75% to 100% annually were reported to be up to date with polio vaccination. However, only about half of patients had stool specimens collected and tested for poliovirus. In 2022, a case of paralytic poliomyelitis in an unvaccinated person in New York was identified through testing of stool specimens collected through AFM surveillance. The Importance of Surveillance “The report also underscores the importance of continued AFM surveillance,” Alvarado said. “Because AFM can present similarly to paralytic poliomyelitis, surveillance supports timely detection and evaluation of patients with acute flaccid weakness or paralysis and strengthens U.S. polio preparedness. Continued surveillance also provides critical information to improve understanding of AFM epidemiology and monitor trends over time.” Clinicians should remain alert for patients suspected of having AFM, report cases to local or state health departments, obtain polio vaccination status, and collect stool specimens from patients with acute onset of flaccid weakness or paralysis, the CDC said. 3 viruses hit ‘high’ levels in Boise area. See which illnesses are trending

News Brief
In the Boise area, three viruses—enterovirus D68, COVID-19, and norovirus—are showing high levels in wastewater as of Wednesday, Sept. 30, 2026. Enterovirus D68, a respiratory virus linked to paralysis, has been trending upward for at least 21 days, reaching "high levels" in the region. Similarly, norovirus and SARS-CoV-2 (the virus causing COVID-19) have also reached high concentrations, indicating increased infections. These viruses can cause mild to severe symptoms, including respiratory issues, gastrointestinal problems, and neurological complications like acute flaccid myelitis. The CDC and Mayo Clinic emphasize that while most people may experience mild symptoms, children, older adults, and those with weakened immune systems are at higher risk for severe outcomes. There are no specific vaccines for EVD68 or noroviruses, but antiviral medications and general prevention measures are recommended. Testing is encouraged to help track disease trends and prevent outbreaks.
Original source text (English)
Three viruses are rampant in Boise. As of Wednesday, Sept. 30, levels of enterovirus D68 , a respiratory virus that can lead to paralysis, remained " high" in Boise's wastewater , according to the WasterWater Scan dashboard. COVID-19 and norovirus have been trending upward in the Boise area for at least 21 days, according to the dashboard. Both diseases have now reached "high levels" indicating an increase in infections . All three illnesses can have deadly consequences. Here's what Boise-area residents should know: Three viruses show a “high” presence in Boise wastewater. What is enterovirus D68? Enterovirus D68 is a virus that can cause "mild to severe respiratory illness," the CDC said. Infections from EVD68 and other non-polio enteroviruses are most common in the summer and fall, but you can get sick year-round, according to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. ​​Non-polio enteroviruses cause 10 to 15 million infections and tens of thousands of hospitalizations each year in the United States, the CDC said. "The virus can be found in an infected person's respiratory secretions," the CDC said. This includes saliva and mucus. Infection most likely spreads through coughs, sneezes and by touching surfaces that have come into contact with those who carry the virus. What are symptoms of ED68? In mild cases, symptoms of enterovirus D68 include runny nose, sneezing, cough and body and muscle aches, according to the CDC. "Serious symptoms may include wheezing, difficulty breathing, or pneumonia," the federal health agency said. Severe infections can lead to serious health complications such as pneumonia, encephalitis or meningitis, the Cleveland Clinic said. In the most extreme cases, EVD68 can lead to acute flaccid myelitis, an "uncommon but dangerous neurological condition that mostly affects children," the CDC said. The development of acute flaccid myelitis can cause muscular paralysis , according to the Cleveland Clinic. How common is virus linked to paralysis? Contracting the enterovirus is pretty common , according to the Cleveland Clinic. "In fact, most people over the age of 5 have evidence of a previous infection," the clinic said. Some people show no symptoms at all. SARS-CoV-2 presence spikes in Boise wastewater. COVID-19 infections may be on the rise. How contagious is COVID-19? How does it spread? COVID-19 is a respiratory disease caused by the SARS-CoV-2 virus, according to the Mayo Clinic. "It can be very contagious and can spread quickly," the CDC said. The virus that causes COVID-19 "spreads between people in close contact ," the Mayo Clinic said, most commonly through tiny airborne droplets. "When people with COVID-19 cough, sneeze, breathe, sing or talk, their breath may contain the COVID-19 virus," the Mayo Clinic said. How can I tell if I have COVID-19? People with COVID-19 can experience a "wide range" of symptoms , according to the CDC. Many of these are similar to those of the flu, pneumonia or the common cold. According to the Mayo Clinic, signs you have COVID-19 can include: Nausea, vomiting or diarrhea "Symptoms may change with new COVID-19 variants and can vary depending on vaccination status," the CDC said. That's why it's important to get tested for coronavirus in order to receive proper treatment from medical professionals. "Most people with COVID-19 have mild symptoms, but some people can become severely ill resulting in hospitalization or death ," Idaho Department of Health and Welfare said. You do not have to experience symptoms to carry the virus, according to the CDC. WastewaterSCAN data shows “high concentrations” of noroviruses in Boise. Do I have a norovirus infection? Noroviruses are extremely contagious and can produce severe and immediate symptoms. According to the Mayo Clinic, noroviruses are "c ommonly spread through food or water that is contaminated during preparation or through contaminated surfaces. Noroviruses can also spread through close contact with a person who has norovirus infection." Symptoms typically begin with diarrhea, stomach pains, nausea and vomiting and begin within 12 to 48 hours after infection, the Mayo Clinic said. Fevers, headaches and body aches can also accompany main symptoms of noroviruses. Norovirus symptoms can lead to severe dehydration which may require hospitalization, according to the CDC. Signs of dehydration include: Infrequent urination or dark-colored urine Dizziness or being lightheaded Call a doctor or your primary care physician if you have symptoms of a viral infection. What should I do if I'm sick? First and foremost, call your doctor and get tested, according to the CDC. Getting tested helps health officials track disease trends and monitor for potential outbreaks. Children ages 7 and younger, older adults and people with weakened immune systems are most at risk of developing severe symptoms from viral infections caused by COVID, EVD68 and noroviruses, according to the CDC. While there are no known treatments that directly target EVD68 or noroviruses, doctors can attend to some symptoms. If you're sick with the novel coronavirus, there are several antiviral medications that can alleviate symptoms and over-the-counter cold medicines may help as well, according to the CDC. Antiviral medications for COVID-19 such as Paxlovid can interfere with other medications, so talk to your doctor about which treatment plan works best for you. EVD68 and noroviruses don’t have a vaccine to help build immunity against infection. What are ways I can prevent viral infections? " Getting a COVID-19 vaccine helps to keep you healthy," the Mayo Clinic said, protecting "against serious illness, the need for hospital care due to COVID-19 and death from COVID-19." This is especially true for people who are more at risk, including seniors over 65, pregnant people and people with chronic health conditions or weakened immune systems. Moderna, Pfizer-BioNTech and Novavax have COVID-19 vaccines available in the United States. There are no specific vaccines approved for EVD68 and noroviruses. People build immunity from enterovirus D68 through exposure over time. "Adults can get infected with enteroviruses, but they are more likely to have no symptoms or mild symptoms," the CDC said. General guidelines for reducing the spread of respiratory infections apply to enterovirus D68, too: Cover your coughs and sneezes Disinfect high-contact surfaces Don't share personal items like drinks, food or clothing Noroviruses can hit hard, fast and multiple times in a row regardless of previous infections. Pay attention to food recall notices and avoid foods that are potentially contaminated with norovirus. Wash foods, surfaces and hands thoroughly before, during and after preparing and consuming meals. Be cautious when consuming raw seafood, especially shellfish. "If eaten raw, oysters and other filter-feeding shellfish can contain viruses and bacteria that can cause illness or death," the CDC said. "Anyone who consumes raw shellfish is at risk of contracting norovirus."
