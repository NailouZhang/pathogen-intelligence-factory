Infection-specific long-chain fatty acid metabolism as a broad anti-enterovirus target

Authors: Marcos Florentin, Jeffrey Loube, Ekaterina G. Viktorova, Samuel Gabaglio, Elizabeth Tanner, Margaret A. Scull, George A. Belov
Original abstract (English)
ABSTRACT Enteroviruses are arguably the most numerous group of viruses infecting humans. While most enterovirus infections are benign and self-resolving, their sheer number inevitably increases the chances of multiple complications. The diversity of enteroviruses means that the development of vaccines is only economically feasible against a select few, and no direct-acting or host-targeted anti-virals are approved to treat enteroviral infections, largely due to the rapid development of resistance against all experimental drugs. Here, we explored a universal property of enterovirus infection—a massive upregulation of phospholipid synthesis as a target for anti-viral interventions. The increased phospholipid synthesis consumes endogenously and exogenously derived long-chain fatty acids (LCFA). We demonstrate that polyunsaturated LCFAs can have a broad anti-enteroviral effect, affecting multiple steps of the virus life cycle. The anti-viral activity of LCFAs did not strictly depend on the degree of unsaturation or their capacity to induce lipid peroxidation, but significantly correlated with their conformation. This suggests that their incorporation into the phospholipid molecules makes the replication organelle membranes incapable of properly accommodating viral replication machinery. Accordingly, the inhibition of neutral lipid synthesis promoted LCFAs retargeting to the membranes in infected cells and increased their anti-viral potency. We show that this approach is effective against diverse enteroviruses in different cell types, including differentiated primary cells, and that attempts to establish viruses resistant to such treatment were unsuccessful. IMPORTANCE Humans are hosts to more than 300 distinct enteroviruses. Enterovirus infections can induce multiple short-term and long-term complications, including such serious conditions as paralysis, myocarditis, aseptic meningitis, and others. Yet, licensed vaccines are available only against poliovirus and enterovirus A71, and no specific anti-enterovirus drugs are approved for clinical practice. These viruses rapidly develop resistance to either virus- or host-acting experimental compounds. We explored a shared feature of enterovirus infection, a strong upregulation of phospholipid synthesis in infected cells, as a tool to control enterovirus replication. By altering the long-chain fatty acids composition available to infected cells, replication of diverse enteroviruses can be similarly suppressed, and the selection of resistant mutants was not successful. Thus, targeting infection-induced changes in cellular lipid metabolism, rather than individual viral or host factors, may offer a promising approach for controlling enterovirus infections.

Development of Therapeutics for Orthopoxvirus and Enterovirus infections: Small Molecule Antiviral Drug Discovery and Library Generation

Authors: Ryan Joyce
Original abstract (English)
Endemic diseases have the potential to spread quickly and overwhelm vulnerable populations, leading to the necessity for antiviral drugs to treat infections. This work focuses on the development of antivirals for orthopoxviruses and enteroviruses, as well as compound library development. Orthopoxviruses, which include monkeypox virus (mpox), vaccinia virus, and the eradicated smallpox virus, have few approved therapies, with no antiviral drug being approved for mpox infection. The viral p37 protein is a known therapeutic target for poxvirus antivirals. Tecovirimat is a known p37 inhibitor; however, it did not effectively treat mpox infections in clinical trials. Tecovirimat contains a large hydrocarbon motif, and previous changes in this motif have retained some antiviral efficacy. We identified a series of compounds containing amide, hydrazide, and carbamide linkers with adamantane, noradamantane, and other cyclic hydrocarbon substitutions with sub-micromolar potency targeting p37. The second focus of this work was the development of antivirals targeting Enterovirus-D68 (EV-D68). EV-D68 is an upper respiratory virus associated with polio-like acute flaccid myelitis (AFM) and primarily represents a threat to children under 16. Currently, there is no vaccine or Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved drug to combat EV-D68 infections. We used high-throughput screening to identify a promising hit molecule that we further optimized. We identified that the target was the VP1 capsid protein. We identified candidates Jun14135 and Jun14934 having broad-spectrum antiviral activity against multiple strains of EV-D68. Furthermore, we identified the potential for co-therapy opportunities of Jun16934 with the EV-D68 2C inhibitor Jun6504. Finally, we look to improve the speed of preclinical development for covalent inhibitors by developing a direct-to-biology (D2B) methodology to quickly synthesize molecules for screening. D2B is being increasingly utilized by both industry and academia, primarily due to the potential to save both time and cost by using small amounts of crude reactions for use in biological assays.

The following month, Sir Ken McCallum, the director general of MI5, warned AI had the potential to "change the stakes on existing or even new bioweapons". Terrorists had long sought access to chemical and biological weapons, he said, but the technology could put dangerous knowledge within reach of people who would otherwise lack it. The threat is not confined to biology. AI is already changing the machinery of war. "We are already in the age of AI warfare," says Paul Scharre, executive vice president at the Centre for a New American Security and formerly of the US office of the secretary of defence. AI is increasingly being used to analyse battlefield intelligence, identify potential targets and help military commanders make decisions at speed. In Ukraine, both sides have deployed AI-assisted drones and targeting systems, such as Russia's Lancet drone, while Israel has used AI tools to help identify targets in Gaza. When Mr Turner left Google DeepMind in June, he cited Google's alleged broken promise about selling its system to the US military "without restrictions against killer robots or mass spying". "I left Deepmind because I was concerned about Google selling its AI to the US military in a way that didn't respect the lives of the people around the world, given its potential for irresponsible integration into AI weapons without humans overseeing them," he told The Telegraph. Mr Turner is particularly concerned about weapons that could carry out attacks without a clear chain of human accountability. "AI-piloted weapons that allow killing people in a way that's extremely hard to attribute," he said. So what might Weapon X actually look like? The idea of an AI superweapon will not be a singular physical weapon, but "more like a network of capabilities of smart systems that are able to respond to high level commands, collaborate with each other, and do things with a high level of autonomy", said Vincent Boulanin, director of the Governance of Artificial Intelligence Programme at the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute. One possible manifestation of this is AI-enabled drone swarms: large groups of unmanned aircraft that use AI to communicate, coordinate their movements and operate as a single system. Zachary Kallenborn, an autonomous weapons and drone swarms analyst, argues such swarms could become weapons of mass destruction. "Drone swarms are potential future weapons of mass destruction," he said. One of the greatest dangers, he argued, is the possibility of errors multiplying as large numbers of autonomous machines operate together. "What happens if one of those drones confuses a school bus for a tank, and then the whole thing potentially goes out of control?" he asked. The technology is already being integrated into the systems used by some of the world's most powerful militaries. The Pentagon's Maven Smart System, developed by defence technology company Palantir, brings together information from sources including satellite imagery, drone footage and radar to help identify and prioritise potential targets. During the first 24 hours of Operation Epic Fury, Maven was used to help process and prioritise more than 1,000 targets across Iran. Anthropic's Claude models were integrated into the system, helping military planners analyse intelligence and accelerate the targeting process. The scale of investment is considerable. "In the long run, the dangerous prospect for militaries is that we shift to a world where humans are no longer in control of war in any meaningful way, and the pace and tempo of war occurs at machine speed," he said. Michael Horowitz, a former senior Pentagon official who worked on the US government's approach to autonomous weapons, says the problem has been recognised for decades. "Even in the early days, 15 or 20 years ago, people could see the technology was going towards greater autonomy, and that that was going to raise some really tough questions about the role of humans in warfare," he said. The technology is changing not only how wars are fought, but how quickly decisions about life and death are made, and who makes them. "When I listen to companies like Palantir and what they're selling, we are just very much on track for a Terminator-style scenario," said David Scott Krueger, a professor of Responsible AI. The race to build Weapon X is already under way. The race to stop it is proving considerably harder. There is a fine line between stopping the development of AI altogether and slowing it down safely. For Ed Leon Klinger, chief executive of the AI company Flock, the answer is not for the West to slow its development. "I think we are in a race and I think the worst thing we could possibly do in this race is slow down," he says. If the US and allies hold back, he argues, China could gain an overwhelming advantage, with consequences for Western security and its way of life. Mr Turner, the former Google DeepMind researcher, believes any meaningful attempt to halt the most dangerous developments will have to involve both superpowers. "It seems hard to imagine that this could be stopped without the US and China being very much on board," he says. "They'll have to kind of see that this is a threat to their national security and to their interests." The two sides did agree to establish a dialogue on superintelligence and a communications channel for serious AI-related incidents, but no agreement to slow development emerged. They propose that companies and governments share information about their progress and agree on where the risks become too great. If each side knew its rivals were not pushing into dangerous territory, the incentive to do so itself would diminish. But that would require a level of transparency and trust that is currently lacking, particularly between Washington and Beijing. In the meantime, governments, scientists and AI companies are taking steps to reduce the risks. Biological weapons are already prohibited under the Biological Weapons Convention. National laws and regulations impose further restrictions, while laboratories operate under strict biosecurity guidelines. AI companies are also introducing safeguards, including testing models for dangerous capabilities and monitoring attempts to misuse them, as outlined in Anthropic's recent report. Kevin Esvelt, an evolutionary biologist at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, said a jailbroken model will instruct a user that the simplest way is to place an infectious virus solution in a medical nebuliser and spray it in the nose of those sleeping in public. "It's hard to imagine us catching someone trying to cause a pandemic like this. We don't really have defences against that," he said. There is another balance to be struck. The same technology that could develop a biological weapon could also accelerate the development of vaccines and treatments. Hayley Severance, the NTI's deputy vice president for global biological policy and programmes, said one aim of its exercise was to demonstrate AI's positive potential in responding to an outbreak, including through the rapid development of vaccines and treatments. But the consequences of striking the wrong balance could be immense. But have we already opened Pandora's box? Is there a possibility of AI developing this independently? Dr Filippa Lentoz, a researcher specialising in biological threats at King's College London, says terror groups are not interested in bioweapons. "We can see from the history of bioterrorism that even without AI, there has been little interest from the terrorism side in biological agents. They are unpredictable. They take time to manifest. Terrorists tend to be interested in a big bang that makes an impact right away," she said. This leaves the possibility of AI agents going rogue, as seen in the Hugging Face incident, in which a swarm of autonomous AI agents escaped their sandbox environment and hacked into the app. Nate Soares, who worked as a software engineer at Google and Microsoft and is now president of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute, says designing a dangerous pathogen would be only one part of the problem for a rogue AI. "Super viruses are a very obvious thing that would be easy for AI to make. We already have signs that AIs can make viruses not known to nature. It would not be hard to make a hyper-lethal virus," he said. "The hard part is in the AIs becoming self-sufficient, if AI makes a virus to kill humans that's suicide until they have their own ability to generate more energy infrastructure and keep the computers running." Mr Soares argues a sufficiently capable AI might instead seek to persuade or recruit people to help it. He puts the possibility of AI-driven human extinction within the next decade at 50 per cent. He has even stopped saving for retirement because of his assessment of the risk. "If humanity creates machines that are far smarter than us, without knowing what we're doing and without knowing how to make them care about us, then the most likely outcome is that we die," he says. Mr Krueger believes the biological threat could become pressing much sooner. "I would say we should be worried about this in the next year, at least with bioweapons," he says. "It's not something to be messing around with and gambling billions of people's lives on." Weapon X may not emerge as a single machine or weapon. It could be the combined power of technologies that are already being developed, each advancing faster than the safeguards intended to govern it. The question is whether the world can agree on limits before the race makes them impossible to impose. The race to build is well under way. The race to stop it may already be falling behind.
