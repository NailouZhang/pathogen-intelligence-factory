Deterministic and ANN-based modelling of measles transmission using real epidemiological data

Authors: Kamil Shah, Changqing Du, Ali Akgül, Farad Sameer Alshammari, Sanaa Ahmed Bajri, Hamiden Abd El-Wahed Khalifa
Original abstract (English)
This study aims to develop a nonlinear SVLIQR epidemic model to investigate measles transmission dynamics by incorporating vaccination, latency, quarantine, and treatment effects. The qualitative properties of the model, including positivity and boundedness of solutions, are established. The basic reproduction number is derived using the next-generation matrix method, and the existence of disease-free and endemic equilibrium points is examined. The local stability of the disease-free equilibrium is analysed using the Routh-Hurwitz criterion, while global stability is studied through the Castillo-Chávez approach. In addition, model parameters are estimated using the least squares method based on reported measles cases in China from 2005 to 2017. To improve the numerical approximation and capture the nonlinear dynamics of the system, an ANN-based solver coupled with the ode45 scheme is implemented. The results show that the model fits the reported data well, and the ANN framework provides highly accurate and convergent approximations under different epidemiological parameter settings. Numerical simulations further indicate that higher vaccination and quarantine rates significantly reduce measles transmission. Overall, the proposed framework provides a useful and reliable tool for understanding measles dynamics and assessing effective intervention strategies.

A longitudinal study on how trust shapes psychological reactance to the German measles vaccine mandate

Authors: Julia Neufeind, Philipp Schmid, Nora Schmid-Küpke
Original abstract (English)
Abstract Background Vaccine mandates can increase uptake, yet their psychological effects remain underexplored. Psychological reactance theory suggests that mandates may prompt resistance and reduce acceptance of voluntary vaccination. We examined changes in reactance and attitudes toward the measles vaccine mandate introduced in Germany in 2020, the extent to which reactance is associated with attempts at freedom restoration (i.e. subsequent voluntary vaccine uptake), and the role of trust in mandate acceptance. Methods We conducted a preregistered longitudinal online survey with an initial sample of 6901 respondents, followed across six time points over 20 months (Aug 2020 – Mar 2022). The sample included parents affected by the mandate and adults without children as a comparison group. We used multilevel modelling to examine the temporal development of reactance, group differences between those affected and not affected, and trust as a determinant. Logistic regression analysis was used to assess whether levels of reactance were associated with uptake of a voluntary vaccine at a later time point. Results Data show that reactance levels are low but stable over time, while attitudes toward the mandate become increasingly negative (β = -0·01, 95% CI [-0·02, -0·01]). Higher trust is associated with lower reactance (β = -0·28, 95% CI [-0·30, -0·26]) and more positive attitudes (β = 0·43, 95% CI [0·41, 0·45]). Importantly, higher initial reactance predicts a lower likelihood of later uptake of voluntary vaccines (OR = 0.49, 95% CI [0·34, 0·68]). Conclusions These findings suggest that reactance to public health policies may persist rather than fade and may shape downstream health behaviour. Building trust and ensuring effective enforcement may help legitimise mandates and mitigate opposition.

Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation for Life-Threatening Measles in Children: A Case Series

Authors: Salmas Watad, Adi Avniel Aran, Asaf Mandel, Yshia Langer, Ezra Weinblatt, Isaac Manaster, Natalie Barsoum, Sergei Elber Dorozko, Dina Averbuch, Oren Gordon
Original abstract (English)
Abstract Purpose Measles is resurgent worldwide, and although vaccine-preventable, severe disease can progress to cardiorespiratory failure refractory to conventional support. This study aimed to determine whether extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is feasible in children with measles. Methods A retrospective case series at a tertiary pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) with a regional ECMO program, of all children younger than 18 years with laboratory-confirmed measles who received ECMO during the August 2025-May 2026 outbreak. Results Of 49 children with measles admitted to the PICU, 10 received ECMO. Five were male; median age was 11 months (IQR, 10–24), and none had completed age-appropriate vaccination. Indications were septic shock or cardiorespiratory failure (n = 5), acute respiratory distress syndrome with septic shock (n = 2), hypoxemic respiratory failure (n = 2), and suspected myocarditis (n = 1). Seven received venoarterial, 1 veno-venoarterial, and 2 venovenous ECMO. Bacteremia preceded ECMO in 5 children and positive sputum culture in 8. Candida tropicalis candidemia developed in 3 of the first 5 patients, with none among the subsequent 5 after antifungal prophylaxis at cannulation. Left ventricular dysfunction was present in 7 of 9 assessed, 4 required decompression, and neuroimaging was abnormal in 4 of 8 imaged. Six children (60%) survived to discharge. Conclusions ECMO was feasible and potentially lifesaving in measles-associated cardiorespiratory failure, with survival comparable to other high-acuity pediatric indications, but the course was complicated by bacterial and fungal coinfection, cardiac dysfunction, and neurologic injury. These resource-intensive presentations occurred exclusively in unvaccinated children and were preventable, underscoring measles vaccination coverage as a public health priority.

A Multiscale Dynamical-Systems Model of Measles Immuno-Epidemiology with ODE-to-Cellular-Automaton Coupling

Authors: Sergio Pérez Montes, Juan Carlos Chimal-Eguía
Original abstract (English)
Measles virus infection couples nonlinear processes across biological scales, including within-host viral amplification, immune-cell depletion, delayed adaptive control, persistent viral RNA, heterogeneous host severity and vaccination-dependent population spread. A multiscale mathematical framework is developed by coupling a seven-variable within-host ordinary differential equation model to a stochastic cellular automaton. The within-host system extends a four-variable measles immunodynamics core by including IFN-γ-dominant and IL-17-associated immune responses, persistent viral RNA and neutralizing antibodies. Six host archetypes are represented as structured parameter perturbations of this common dynamical core. The principal novelty is an explicit cross-scale coupling operator that separates genuinely ODE-derived host descriptors from hybrid epidemiological mapping rules and independently specified population-level contact and susceptibility assumptions, allowing within-host heterogeneity to propagate transparently into a spatial stochastic epidemic model. An explicit ODE-to-cellular-automaton map translates within-host trajectories into infectious timing, daily infectivity profiles and an illustrative ODE-informed severity-to-death transition mapping used internally by the cellular automaton. The mortality map depends on viral burden, infectious duration, IFN-γ deficit, cumulative infectivity and an immune-deficit–infectivity interaction term. Population simulations show a nonlinear reduction in attack rate with increasing vaccination coverage, reduced modeled death burden under targeted high-risk in silico perturbations and additional suppression under reactive vaccination campaigns. A direct local cellular-automaton secondary-infection estimate is reported instead of interpreting cumulative infectivity burden as a reproduction number. A targeted contact-structure sensitivity further shows that matching the expected local direct-secondary-infection potential does not imply equivalent population-level attack rates, emphasizing that the quantitative CA outcomes are geometry specific. Sobol sensitivity analysis with convergence up to Nbase=4096 identifies core viral and immune parameters as dominant drivers of within-host and multiscale outputs. The framework provides an explicit dynamical-systems approach for coupling differential-equation immunodynamics to spatial stochastic population models in mathematical biology.

问题与背景

Question and background
Measles remains a public health concern in Malaysia despite high reported vaccine coverage. This study aimed to characterise the molecular epidemiology of measles virus (MeV) circulating in Malaysia from 2018 to 2024.

Core methods
Partial N-gene (N450) sequences were genotyped using the WHO classification framework. Temporal genotype trends were assessed using logistic regression, genotype distributions were compared using χ2 tests, and phylogenetic analyses were performed using the Nextstrain Augur pipeline.

Protect yourself with the measles vaccine Measles vaccines are available year-round for those who are not up-to-date. An early dose is currently recommended for infants ages 6-11 months to protect them against the higher potential for exposure. This year, there have been 28 cases of measles reported in Maricopa County and more than 3,000 cases in the U.S. Get seasonal respiratory vaccines in early fall Updated vaccines for seasonal flu, COVID-19, and RSV are available now for the upcoming respiratory virus season. Getting protected against seasonal respiratory viruses by the end of October helps the body build its immunity in time for end-of-year holidays and gatherings. Learn to recognize symptoms and what to do if you get them Fever, chills, body aches, coughs, and congestion are common symptoms of viruses. If you develop symptoms, stay home and away from others to prevent spreading germs. Seek healthcare if you are at higher risk of complications or your symptoms are concerning. Why prevention matters Sickness from preventable illnesses cause many impacts within our community. Some of them include: Lost days of learning for kids who have to stay home Lost wages for people who are sick or need to stay home with sick family members More hospital visits for preventable illnesses, which reduces the number of hospital beds and staff available for issues like accidents, heart attacks, and other emergencies Avoid long-term impacts: Some viruses can cause long-term health risks. For example, measles infection can lead to a dangerous brain-swelling condition several years after a person recovers from their initial infection. Similarly, varicella infection (also known as chickenpox) can lead to shingles later in life, a painful complication that can be avoided with the varicella vaccine or, if you’ve already had varicella, with the shingles vaccine. Common respiratory viruses can develop into complicated pneumonias, hospitalizations, and death. As of July, the country has recorded more measles cases than in any other year since 1991. Before the vaccine became available in 1963, measles sickened 3 million to 4 million people and led to about 500 deaths every year in the United States. Measles was declared eliminated from the United States in 2000, but recent outbreaks mean that status will be reassessed in November. In a Tuesday Senate hearing, the nominee for deputy secretary of the US Department of Health and Human Services, Chris Klomp, appeared to acknowledge the US had lost its elimination status, and endorsed measles vaccination strongly as the best way to regain it. “Unquestionably, the MMR [measles, mumps, rubella] vaccine is the single most effective public health tool that we have against measles,” Klomp, a former healthcare executive, told the Senate Finance Committee in the first of two confirmation hearings over two days. “It’s 97% effective after two doses to preventing transmission, and is the single best thing that we can do to eradicate and establish our exemption from measles again.” How common are measles deaths globally? Measles deaths are much more common in low-income countries with weaker health infrastructure than in the United States or similar countries. Globally, an estimated 95,000 people died from measles in 2024, according to the World Health Organization. Ninety-five percent of those deaths were in low-income countries. Measles is considered the most contagious infectious disease known to humans; up to 9 out of 10 unvaccinated people who come in contact with an infected person will catch the virus. Vaccinations have proven highly successful, preventing an estimated 59 million deaths from 2000 to 2024, WHO said. Before widespread vaccination, measles caused an estimated 2.6 million deaths globally each year. Other high-income countries have seen record numbers of measles cases in recent years as well; last year, Canada reported 5,462 measles cases and two deaths. The country lost its elimination status because of sustained transmission for more than a year, a fact HHS nominee Klomp pointed out. Many countries have lost their elimination status, Klomp told Sen. Ron Wyden, a Democrat from Oregon and the ranking member of the finance committee. “We’ve seen vaccination rates dropping all over the world, not just the United States,” Klomp said. Nonetheless, Klomp’s unequivocal embrace of the MMR vaccine contrasted with the mixed messaging from his boss, HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., a longtime vaccine critic who has downplayed the seriousness of measles. Globally, measles typically kills nearly 3 out of every 1,000 people who get sick; in the United States, it’s about 1 in 1,000, research shows. As of this week, Pennsylvania had counted at least 693 measles cases, 133 hospitalizations and four deaths, according to the Pennsylvania health department. The official tally is likely an undercount. Measles cases are often unreported when people have milder symptoms and don’t see a doctor who would report the case. Pennsylvania’s death toll is “on the high side,” said Johns Hopkins University Professor Dr. William Moss, an epidemiologist who has studied measles for years. Moss believes the higher number of deaths in Pennsylvania “reflects the fact that these infections occurred in individuals, except for the 18-year-old, with underlying risk factors for severe illness and mortality, particularly the two infants.” One infant who died had a ruptured spleen and died shortly after birth. The other infant had Amish microcephaly, a rare and severe genetic disorder that causes a small head and underdeveloped brain and leaves children with little chance of surviving beyond a couple of months, Moss said. The 40-year-old woman who died had chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, which increased her risk for a severe lung infection from measles. “They’re all tragic cases, all unvaccinated cases, but kind of unique in their underlying risk factors for severe measles,” Moss said. What is acute disseminated encephalomyelitis? The Mifflin County, Pennsylvania, coroner said this week that an 18-year-old with measles died from acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, known as ADEM. Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis is a severe neurological complication of measles that triggers rapid inflammation and swelling in the brain and spinal cord. It develops in about 1 in 1,000 measles cases. “It’s just a relatively rare, but known complication of measles that can occur in anyone, even otherwise healthy individuals,” Moss said. Could there be something new or worse about the virus circulating now? Neither Moss nor Dr. David Margolius, director of the Cleveland Department of Health, believes the virus has mutated to become more deadly. There may be more deaths because there are more cases and fewer people are getting vaccinated, Margolius said. “So it’s been given a door in, and it’s taken advantage and the virus has spread across many populations across the world,” Margolius said. Measles vaccination is very safe and highly effective, providing lifelong protection for most people. Joe Raedle/Getty Images There is no specific treatment for measles, so prevention is the best way to reduce risk of serious illness or death. The measles vaccine is highly effective, providing 97% protection for most people. “It’s not perfect and we have a two-dose vaccine schedule for a reason,” Moss said. “Not everyone responds to the first dose.” The risk of death from measles for vaccinated people is “very low, unless you’re immunosuppressed in some way,” Moss said. Measles, mumps and rubella protection are combined into one vaccine administered when a baby is between 12 to 15 months old. Children get a second dose between ages 4 and 6. For most people, protection provided by the vaccine lasts a lifetime. Who’s most at risk of serious illness from measles? Measles is well known for its rash, fever and cough, but it can also cause seizures, blindness, deafness and permanent lung damage. Measles can also weaken the central nervous system and immune system, resulting in serious disease or death years later. The measles virus can also essentially wipe out the body’s previous immune memory, making people vulnerable to other dangerous infections, even long after they recover from the initial infection. Subacute sclerosing panencephalitis, or SSPE, is a rare but fatal central nervous system disease that can develop seven to 10 years after a person is infected and seems to have recovered. Before vaccination was widely available, about 1,000 people developed chronic disability from acute encephalitis caused by measles annually, according to the CDC. Callista Images/Image Source/Getty Images A 40-year-old woman who lived in Jefferson County, Pennsylvania, has died from measles complications, the county coroner confirmed Sunday. It’s the third measles-associated death in a fast-growing outbreak that’s sparked a political clash between state and federal officials. The woman died Saturday, according to a news release from the Jefferson County Coroner’s Office, which said it was working with the Pennsylvania Department of Health on the case. The woman hadn’t been vaccinated against measles, the coroner, Greg Furlong, confirmed to CNN. The death follows two measles-associated deaths reported in Lancaster County at the end of August, the first connected to the viral illness in the state in 35 years. That county is the epicenter of Pennsylvania’s measles outbreak, which had reached 676 cases as of Friday, 108 of which were added in the previous week. One hundred twenty-four people have been hospitalized. The woman contracted measles after a family member had it as well, Furlong told CNN, and noted she also had chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or COPD, and asthma, and had been on supplemental oxygen 24 hours a day before getting measles. The state health department said it’s in touch with the coroner and is conducting its own review of the case, and said it will provide the public with updated information when it becomes available. In some of her first public comments about the country’s measles outbreaks, Dr. Erica Schwartz, the newly confirmed director of the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, said in a statement that the agency hadn’t been notified by Pennsylvania officials of the death. “Despite repeated offers of assistance, Pennsylvania has not requested a CDC Epi-Aid, which would deploy epidemiologists and provide on-the-ground measles outbreak support,” Schwartz’s statement, shared with CNN by email, said. “By contrast, Texas, South Carolina, Virginia, Kansas, New Mexico, and Wisconsin have officially requested CDC Epi-Aids and worked alongside federal teams to help bring their measles outbreaks under control. CDC remains ready to provide Epi-Aid support to Pennsylvania as soon as the state officially requests our help.” Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro, a Democrat, said in late August when the state announced the first two measles-associated deaths that he’d declined an offer from Robert F. Kennedy Jr., secretary of the US Department of Health and Human Services, for federal help. He then said he’d told the secretary that his “actions and his rhetoric,” including spreading misinformation about vaccines, was harming communities. Kennedy, an outspoken vaccine skeptic, shot back on social media, accusing Shapiro of fearmongering and the “weaponization of infectious disease” for political gain. He suggested the deaths may have been “altogether fabricated” by one of Shapiro’s “hopeful staffers” to support a narrative that measles infection is more dangerous than it actually is. The two deaths in Lancaster County were in infants, the county coroner there said, but his office disputed whether one of the deaths was caused by measles. A newborn died of a ruptured spleen, which the coroner, Dr. Stephen Diamantoni, said his forensic pathologist deemed wasn’t caused by the viral illness. A second death, in a six-week-old infant with a serious genetic condition called Amish lethal microcephaly, was caused by measles, Diamantoni told CNN. Measles typically causes about one in five people who aren’t vaccinated to be hospitalized, according to the CDC. As many as one out of every 20 children with measles gets pneumonia, the most common cause of death from measles in young children. One of every 1,000 kids with measles will develop brain swelling. The virus kills one to three of every 1,000 children who become infected. “Many people associate measles with a childhood illness, but it can lead to severe complications in individuals of any age,” Jefferson County Chief Deputy Coroner and Public Relations Officer Jason Stiver said in the office’s statement. “Measles is highly contagious and can result in serious respiratory illness, hospitalization, and in rare cases like this, death.” The MMR vaccine is the best way to protect against measles, the CDC says. Two doses provide 97% protection against measles. Approved since 1963, the vaccine is very safe, and side effects are usually mild and go away on their own, the agency says. In its statement Sunday, the state health department reiterated that last month, it “confirmed two measles-associated deaths in Pennsylvania,” adding, “The Department’s primary focus remains on stopping the spread of measles across the Commonwealth by expanding access to measles vaccination.” CNN’s Brenda Goodman contributed to this report.
