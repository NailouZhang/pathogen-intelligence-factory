One Stop Shop Model of Hepatitis B Virus Case Management and Hepatitis C Virus Elimination among Men who have sex with men and Transgender Individuals in Lahore Punjab and Larkana Sindh, Pakistan

Authors: Hassan Mahmood, Alina Rashid, Quaid Saeed, Zain-ul Abdeen, Syed Raza Haider Tirmizi, Sarwat Farooq, Wajid Ali, Sadam Hussain Dero, Mahnoor Chaudhary, Nayab Shahid
Original abstract (English)
Abstract Background Pakistan bears one of the highest global burdens of viral hepatitis, yet limited data exist on Hepatitis B Virus (HBV) and Hepatitis C Virus (HCV) prevalence among transgender individuals and men who have sex with men (MSM). The purpose of the study was to estimate Hepatitis B Virus and Hepatitis C Virus among transgender individuals and MSM in Pakistan and to evaluate a community-based, integrated screening and treatment model using decentralized molecular diagnostics. Methods A cross-sectional study was conducted from October 2019 to October 2021 in Lahore, Punjab and Larkana, Sindh. A total of 2,241 participants were enrolled through community-based organizations under the National AIDS Control Programme. Participants were screened for HBsAg and anti-HCV using World Health Organization pre-qualified rapid diagnostic tests, with confirmation using Gene Xpert. Individuals who tested positive were linked with care. Differences between men who have sex with men and transgender individuals were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test exact test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for APRI scores.

Question and background
The study aimed to address the limited data on Hepatitis B Virus (HBV) and Hepatitis C Virus (HCV) prevalence among transgender individuals and men who have sex with men (MSM) in Pakistan, given the high global burden of viral hepatitis in the country.
Design and population
A cross-sectional study was conducted from October 2019 to October 2021 in Lahore, Punjab and Larkana, Sindh, involving 2,241 participants enrolled through community-based organizations under the National AIDS Control Programme.
Core methods
Participants were screened for HBsAg and anti-HCV using WHO pre-qualified rapid diagnostic tests, with confirmation using Gene Xpert. Differences between men who have sex with men and transgender individuals were assessed using statistical tests.
Main results
The study found that 12.3% of participants tested positive for HBsAg and 15.6% for anti-HCV. No significant differences were observed between men who have sex with men and transgender individuals in terms of HBV and HCV positivity.
Interpretation and novelty
The findings suggest the need for targeted screening and treatment programs for HBV and HCV among MSM and transgender individuals in Pakistan, highlighting the importance of a community-based, integrated screening and treatment model.
Scientific and public-health significance
The study’s findings can inform surveillance and treatment strategies for HBV and HCV among high-risk populations in Pakistan, contributing to the national efforts towards hepatitis elimination.
Limitations and evidence strength
The study’s design limitations include the potential for selection bias due to the reliance on community-based organizations and the lack of randomization. The evidence strength is moderate due to the observational nature of the study.

Hepatitis B virus genome mutations in precore and basal core promoter regions among HBeAg-negative chronic hepatitis B patients with high viral load in Indonesia.

Authors: Dewi Wulandari, Yan Mardian, Sunita, Kadek Aditya Darmayoga, Rindha Agustiani, Khoirunnisa, Youdiil Ophinni
Original abstract (English)
Hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg) is widely used as a marker for active HBV replication and serves as a surrogate for HBV DNA > 200,000 IU/mL to determine eligibility for tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) prophylaxis to prevent vertical transmission, according to WHO guidelines. However, some HBeAg-negative patients still harbor high viral loads. Mutations in the precore (PC) and basal core promoter (BCP) regions may reduce or abolish HBeAg expression without necessarily suppressing viral replication. Next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based characterization of these mutations remains limited in Indonesia. This study aimed to analyze the mutation prevalence in the BCP and PC regions associated with HBeAg negativity in Indonesian patients. We conducted a cross-sectional study of 32 chronic HBV treatment-naïve, unvaccinated patients with HBV DNA > 200,000 IU/mL (16 HBeAg-negative, 16 HBeAg-positive) at Cipto Mangunkusumo General Hospital. BCP and PC mutations were analyzed using NGS, classifying mutations as major (mutation frequency index [MFI] ≥20%) or minor (MFI 1- < 20%). Associations were analyzed using the Chi-square or Fisher's exact test and p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Among 29 major mutation sites, PC mutations A1846T/C and G1896A were more frequent in HBeAg-negative than HBeAg-positive patients (81.3% vs 6.3% and 75.0% vs 12.5%, respectively; all adjusted p = 0.019). Combined analysis showed higher mutation frequencies in HBeAg-negative patients (93.8%, 81.3%, and 62.5% for A1846T/C, G1896A, and G1899A, respectively; all adjusted p = 0.015). In conclusion, HBeAg-negative patients with high viral loads are strongly associated with PC mutations, particularly G1896A, A1846T/C, and G1899A. These exploratory findings provide regional NGS-based molecular evidence that established PC mutations may contribute to the coexistence of HBeAg negativity and continued high-level HBV replication in Indonesian patients. Larger studies incorporating broader virological and clinical comparison groups are required to determine the clinical significance of these findings.

Original abstract (English)

Mechanistically, IFN-λ-IFNLR1 signaling suppressed LXR-dependent lipid metabolism, thereby promoting tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α and IL-1β production, and LXR pathway inhibition favored M1-macrophage polarization. Collectively, IFNLR1 identifies a pro-fibrotic and immunoregulatory monocyte/macrophage subset. Targeting IFNLR1-LXR axis may represent promising anti-fibrotic strategies for HBV-related liver fibrosis.

Original abstract (English)
Developing highly sensitive, efficient, stable, and low-cost viral antigen detection strategies suitable for primary healthcare settings is of great significance for the early diagnosis and treatment of viral infections. Herein, we report a highly efficient fluorescent immunoassay strategy for the detection of hepatitis B envelope antigen (HBeAg) based on aggregation-induced emission (AIE) molecule-encoded fluorescent nanospheres. In addition, it exhibited excellent specificity and robust anti-interference performance in spiked fresh mouse serum samples. Overall, this work presents a sensitive, convenient, and cost-effective sensing platform for HBeAg detection, showing great potential for rapid and on-site screening of viral antigens in clinical applications.

Dr. Chung notes that data across two trials pointed to a functional cure rate of roughly 20% in this patient group and asks Dr. Gish to react to those findings and weigh the efficacy signal against safety considerations in a population that is already well suppressed and feeling well on long-term nucleoside therapy. Dr. Gish describes himself as the optimist in a class that is one-third full or two-thirds empty, noting that nearly every patient he sees with hepatitis B asks when they might be able to stop treatment. He believes most patients already understand the functional cure concept and considers this therapy a genuine leap forward, though not yet approved and still pending FDA review. He compares the side effect profile to interferon therapy, an agent he and Dr. Chung both have extensive experience treating. He notes that 70% of patients on placebo in the Be-Well studies also experienced significant adverse events, suggesting hepatitis B itself, as a disease, contributes meaningfully to how patients feel. He considers grade 3 adverse events the more meaningful safety threshold, occurring in 16% of bepirovirsen recipients versus 3% on placebo, likely reflecting an immunologic response tied to ALT flares that he views as probably beneficial. He adds that kidney and bone marrow monitoring will be necessary, requiring meaningful clinical infrastructure. Asked which patients he sees as the best candidates once the therapy is approved, Dr. Gish points to three criteria: non-cirrhotic status, a favorable surface antigen window, and patient motivation, noting that more than half of his patients are already asking about this emerging data and preparing for that conversation. Researchers identify ANKRD11 as a brake on T-cell responses to chronic hepatitis B virus and cancer - EurekAlert!

News Brief
Researchers from the Chinese Academy of Sciences and collaborating institutions have identified ANKRD11 as a key regulator that reduces the activity of CD8+ T cells during chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection and cancer. The study, published in Nature Immunology, found that ANKRD11 suppresses the AP-1 signaling pathway, which usually helps T cells mount effective responses against infections and tumors. The findings suggest that targeting ANKRD11 could provide a potential strategy for strengthening the body's immune response to chronic HBV infection and cancer, particularly in cases where existing therapies are insufficient.

The researchers found that ANKRD11 suppresses the AP-1 signaling pathway, which usually helps CD8+ T cells mount effective responses against infections and tumors. The study was published in Nature Immunology on September 11. HBV infects about 296 million people worldwide and is a leading cause of serious liver diseases such as cirrhosis and liver cancer. One major reason the immune system in controling chronic HBV is that CD8+ T cells, which normally attack infected cells, lose their strength and can't multiply well. The liver environment can further suppress immune responses, making some existing immune-based therapies less effective against HBV. Identifying new ways to restore T cell function is therefore an important goal in the treatment of chronic HBV infection. Building on their previous work, the researchers had developed a mouse model that mimics key features of human immune responses to HBV and identified a major target of the antiviral immune response. In the new study, they used advanced genetic tools to look across the whole genome and identify genes that might affect how T cells respond to chronic HBV. Among the candidate genes, they focused on Ankrd11 because it was new, showed strong results, and made sense as a regulator of how genes are turned on or off in immune cells. Deleting Ankrd11 specifically in T cells did not affect their development, but substantially enhanced their ability to fight infection. The modified T cells produced higher levels of key immune molecules and were more effective at attacking infected and cancerous cells. Further analysis showed that genes involved in T cell function were more readily activated in the absence of ANKRD11, indicating that the protein acts as a brake on T cell activity. The researchers also found that ANKRD11-deficient T cells were more resistant to signals that normally suppress immune responses. The reserchers then tested their findings in several disease models. In mice with HBV, removing Ankrd11 from T cells led to more active immune cells in the liver, helped clear the virus, and improved disease outcomes. In another virus model and in a cancer model, T cells without Ankrd11 could better fight the virus and shrink tumors—even in cases where other treatments had failed. The researchers also found that combining this approach with existing therapies made the treatment even stronger. The findings identify ANKRD11 as an important regulator that restrains T cell responses against chronic viral infection and cancer. Targeting ANKRD11 could provide a potential strategy for strengthening the body's own immune response to chronic HBV infection and cancer, particularly in cases where existing therapies are insufficient.
