Safety, Tolerability and Antiviral Activity of GST ‐ HG131 in Chronic Hepatitis B: A Randomized, Double‐Blind Phase IIa Study

Authors: Yu-Ming Guo, Zhe Xu, Yuan-Ying Li, John Mao, Tian-Xiang Zhang, Fan-Ping Meng, Jun-Liang Fu, Dong Ji, Ying-Jie Ji, Yong-Qian Cheng
Original abstract (English)
ABSTRACT Background and Aims Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) remains a major global health burden. Current therapies rarely achieve a functional cure due to low rates of HBsAg clearance, the persistence of the viral replication template—covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA)—and the need for lifelong treatment. GST‐HG131 is a first‐in‐class, orally bioavailable small‐molecule inhibitor of PAPD5/7, key host factors involved in HBV mRNA stabilization and HBsAg production. We evaluated the safety, tolerability and antiviral activity of GST‐HG131 in patients with CHB receiving nucleos(t)ide analogue (NUC) therapy in a randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled Phase IIa study. Methods This double‐blind, randomized, placebo‐controlled Phase IIa study evaluated the efficacy and safety of GST‐HG131 in 45 CHB patients. Cohorts 1 and 2 ( n = 10 each; 8 active, 2 placebo) received 30 mg or 60 mg BID for 28 days, respectively. Cohort 3 ( n = 25; 20 active, five placebo) received 30 mg BID for 12 weeks. All participants were on nucleos(t)ide analog monotherapy for more than 6 months, with HBsAg between 100 and 1500 IU/mL, and serum ALT below 1× ULN. Results At Day 28, HBsAg reductions were −0.65 log 10 IU/mL in the 30 mg BID 28‐day group and −0.53 log 10 IU/mL in the 60 mg BID 28‐day group. The 30 mg BID 12‐week group showed a greater reduction of −0.89 log 10 IU/mL at the end of treatment versus placebo ( p 50% reduction in HBsAg with a maximum decrease of 1.64 log 10 IU/mL. Notably, 76.5% of patients in the 12‐week group achieved HBsAg below 100 IU/mL. Rebounds in HBsAg were observed following treatment discontinuation. GST‐HG131 was well tolerated, with no dose‐related toxicity or emerging safety concerns across all cohorts. Conclusion GST‐HG131, a first‐in‐class oral PAPD5/7 inhibitor, demonstrated favourable safety and significant HBsAg reduction in patients with CHB. The greatest antiviral activity was observed after 12 weeks of treatment, supporting further clinical development of GST‐HG131 as a component of functional cure strategies for CHB. Circulation and recombination-driven diversity of duck hepatitis B virus in domesticated geese in Guangdong, southern China

Authors: Wei-Hao Tan, Jie-Yue Liang, Xue-Lian Zhang, Jian-Wei Shao
Original abstract (English)
Duck hepatitis B virus (DHBV) is a valuable comparative model for human hepatitis B virus (HBV) due to its shared genomic organization and reverse transcription-based replication, despite having only ∼40% nucleotide identity. Infection is generally asymptomatic in adult birds but causes reduced hatchability, impaired embryo development, and growth retardation in young waterfowl, leading to economic losses. As a major waterfowl producer, China faces risks from this endemic virus. This study analyzed 901 deceased domesticated geese liver samples from five regions in Guangdong province, southern China. Nested PCR screening revealed an overall infection prevalence of 34.1% (307/901). Amino acid analysis of the preS domain of the large envelope protein showed extensive variability, including the G133E mutation in 47.7% (21/44) of strains and genotype-associated substitutions at residues 117 and 120. Genetic characterization of 44 complete genomes revealed exclusive circulation of genotypes I and II (DHBV-I and DHBV-II), with no detection of the Western-associated DHBV-III. Phylogenetic analysis indicated frequent duck-goose host-switching and viral circulation between provinces, likely facilitated by the live-poultry trade. We identified 20 recombination events, six of which involved our sequences as parental strains, demonstrating that active recombination among co-circulating strains drives viral heterogeneity in Guangdong. These findings highlight the ongoing circulation and evolutionary complexity of DHBV in southern China and underscore the need for longitudinal genomic surveillance to detect emerging variants.

This study aimed to investigate the circulation and recombination-driven diversity of duck hepatitis B virus (DHBV) in domesticated geese in Guangdong, southern China, as DHBV is a valuable comparative model for human hepatitis B virus (HBV) due to its shared genomic organization and reverse transcription-based replication, despite having only ∼40% nucleotide identity.

The findings highlight the ongoing circulation and evolutionary complexity of DHBV in southern China, emphasizing the role of active recombination among co-circulating strains in driving viral heterogeneity.
Scientific and public-health significance
These results underscore the need for longitudinal genomic surveillance to detect emerging DHBV variants and inform strategies for monitoring and managing the virus in domesticated geese populations.
Limitations and evidence strength
The study is limited by its cross-sectional design, which may not capture the full dynamics of DHBV circulation over time. The evidence is moderate, based on prevalence and genetic analysis of a single geographic region.

Original abstract (English)
Antiviral therapy with nucleos(t)ide analogues is recommended for patients with viremic hepatitis B virus (HBV)-related cirrhosis regardless of levels of viremia or liver enzymes. However, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is not an antiviral treatment criterion in the U.S. HBV treatment guidelines, though prior studies have reported improved survival with antiviral treatment in HBV-related HCC. To inform guidelines, we estimated antiviral treatment rates and their association with survival and HCC recurrence rates in HBV-related HCC. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies published between 2006 and 2024 that reported antiviral treatment, survival, and/or HCC recurrence rates in adults with HBV-related HCC, following PRISMA guidelines. Random-effects models pooled study estimates. Of the initial 4556 studies, 178 underwent full-text review, and 78 (68,243 patients) were analysed. The pooled antiviral treatment rate (52 studies, 41,345 patients) was 53.1%. In studies reporting pre-HCC antiviral initiation, 17.2% of HBV-related HCC patients received therapy before diagnosis. Treated (vs untreated) patients had higher 3- and 5-year OS (80.4% vs. 46.8%, both p < 0.01). Five-year OS was 78.0% vs. 56.4% in inactive HCC (p < 0.01) and 48.2% vs. 22.6% in active HCC (p = 0.14). Antiviral therapy remained independently associated with improved 5-year OS in multivariable meta-regression. There was no significant difference in HCC recurrence rates. Antiviral therapy is associated with improved long-term survival but remains underutilized in HBV-related HCC. These findings highlight a critical gap in care and underscore the need for earlier and more consistent antiviral initiation in patients with HBV-related HCC.

Original abstract (English)
BACKGROUND: Prognosis after radical hepatectomy for chronic hepatitis B (CHB) related hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is poor. Comorbidity of CHB and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has grown increasingly common, with MASLD further modifying the hepatic microenvironment beyond HBV-induced injury. However, its impact on postoperative prognosis remains unclear. METHODS: Combining a retrospective study with meta-analysis, this study systematically compared prognostic differences and influencing factors in 424 hepatectomy patients (MASLD-CHB-HCC group vs. CHB-HCC group) from multiple centers. Prevalence and determinants of hepatitis B vaccination uptake among pregnant women in sub-Saharan Africa: a systematic review and meta-analysis.

Authors: Ismail Bamidele Afolabi, J Craig Phillips, Darine El-Chaâr, Shannon A Bainbridge, Karen P Phillips
Original abstract (English)
OBJECTIVE: Despite more than two decades of hepatitis B vaccination implementation across sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), hepatitis B virus (HBV) remains hyperendemic in the region, with vertical transmission an important route of infection. However, there is a lack of regional evidence on HBV vaccination uptake among pregnant women, a priority population in HBV prevention. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the prevalence of HBV vaccination uptake and identify its determinants among pregnant women in SSA. DESIGN: Systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. DATA SOURCES: Following prospective registration (PROSPERO), we searched MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science and Scopus for peer-reviewed studies published up to August 2026, supplemented by African Journals Online and Google Scholar. ELIGIBILITY CRITERIA FOR SELECTING STUDIES: Observational studies reporting HBV vaccination uptake among pregnant women in SSA countries were included. DATA EXTRACTION AND SYNTHESIS: Two reviewers independently screened studies, extracted data and assessed methodological quality using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist for analytical cross-sectional studies. A random-effects meta-analysis was performed to estimate pooled prevalence. Subgroup and meta-regression sensitivity analyses explored sources of heterogeneity and robustness of findings. Determinants of vaccination uptake were synthesised narratively. RESULTS: Of 1041 records screened, 21 studies involving 15 804 pregnant women from seven SSA countries were included. The pooled prevalence of HBV vaccination uptake was 18% (95% CI 14% to 21%) with substantial heterogeneity (I2=99.2%) observed. Studies from West Africa (12; n=8062) reported higher vaccine uptake (24%, 95% CI 17% to 31%; p<0.001). Available studies identified socioeconomic, behavioural and healthcare-related factors associated with uptake, though evidence was limited. Evidence of small-study effects or potential publication bias was observed (Egger’s test, p<0.001). The overall certainty of evidence was considered very low using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach, reflecting serious concerns regarding inconsistency, risk of bias and small-study effects. CONCLUSIONS: HBV vaccination uptake among pregnant women in SSA remains suboptimal and represents an important unmet need for preventing vertical transmission. Targeted, context-specific interventions to improve awareness, access and integration of vaccination into antenatal care services are needed to enhance uptake. Future research should examine barriers and evaluate interventions to increase vaccination coverage in this priority population. PROSPERO REGISTRATION NUMBER: CRD420251120357.

Albania and Armenia achieve hepatitis B control milestone, strengthening the WHO European Region’s path to elimination - World Health Organization (WHO)

News Brief
Albania and Armenia have achieved hepatitis B control targets, marking a significant milestone in the WHO European Region’s efforts to eliminate the virus. This accomplishment was validated by the European Technical Advisory Group of Experts on Immunization (ETAGE) following an assessment of country reports and recommendations from the ETAGE Working Group on Hepatitis B at its sixth meeting on 10 April 2026. Country serosurveys revealed that over 20 years of sustained vaccination efforts have led to the elimination of hepatitis B among children aged 6–8 years, with no positive cases detected. The validation ceremony took place on 6 October 2026, where the certificate was handed over to the Armenian Minister of Health. Hepatitis B can cause lifelong infection and serious complications such as cirrhosis and liver cancer, making early prevention critical. This achievement joins other countries in the WHO European Region that have already met similar targets, highlighting progress in public health initiatives.

Original source text (English)
Abstract Immune-mediated hepatotoxicity (IMH) is a typical immune-related adverse event associated with the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), while hepatitis B virus reactivation (HBVr) is an infectious complication that may also occur during ICI therapy. However, these two conditions are usually independent and rarely co-occur in the same individual. Herein, we describe a patient with lung squamous cell carcinoma who developed HBVr and IMH simultaneously after receiving tislelizumab, and the co-existence of two episodes of HBVr brings challenges to the diagnosis and treatment of IMH. In the diagnosis of IMH, the co-existence of HBVr masked the underlying IMH, resulting in a delay in IMH treatment. In the treatment of IMH, the re-occurrence of HBVr led to a dilemma of continuing to use glucocorticoids (GCs) or stopping them. In such a clinical scenario, the multidisciplinary approach, close monitoring of liver function and HBV-DNA, and a carefully balanced treatment strategy combining GCs with intensified antiviral therapy are essential to realize the optimal management for IMH. In conclusion, medical staff should be alert to the coexistence of HBVr and IMH in patients receiving ICIs, so as to avoid missed diagnosis and treatment delay. 1 Introduction Tislelizumab is a monoclonal IgG4 antibody against human programmed death receptor −1, which is widely used in the treatment of various malignant tumors at present, including non-small cell lung cancer ( Lee and Keam, 2020 ). Although immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), represented by tislelizumab, have become an effective cancer treatment, they can also lead to a breach in self-tolerance of the patient, resulting in immune-related adverse events (irAEs) ( Singh et al., 2023 ). The liver is one of the most frequently affected organs in irAEs, and it is reported that about 14% of patients will suffer from immune-mediated hepatotoxicity (IMH) during ICI treatment ( Jiang et al., 2024 ). Hepatitis B virus (HBV) is a common cause of liver injury in the clinic and also a differential diagnosis that needs to be excluded before making an IMH diagnosis ( Peeraphatdit et al., 2020 ). In clinical practice, it is not uncommon for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg)-positive cancer patients receiving ICIs to experience HBV reactivation (HBVr). A retrospective cohort study reported that about 5.3% of HBsAg-positive cancer patients developed HBVr after exposure to ICIs and showed transaminase elevation ( Zhang et al., 2019 ). However, IMH and HBVr are usually independent events that rarely occur in the same individual at the same time. Herein, we describe a patient with lung squamous cell carcinoma who developed HBVr and IMH simultaneously after receiving tislelizumab. In this case, the two episodes of HBVr greatly complicated the diagnosis and treatment of IMH, which suggested that medical staff should be alert to the coexistence of HBVr and IMH in patients receiving ICIs to avoid missed diagnosis and treatment delay. 2 Case description A 61-year-old man without a history of alcohol use was diagnosed with lung squamous cell carcinoma of the right upper lobe (cT4N3M0, stage IIIC) in April 2026. He has no mutation in the EGFR/ALK/ROS1 genes, and the PD-L1 expression level is about 1%. On 22 April 2026, the patient received the first cycle of anti-tumor therapy, consisting of albumin-bound paclitaxel (400 mg), carboplatin (450 mg), and tislelizumab (200 mg). Before starting anti-tumor therapy, the laboratory tests showed that the liver function index of this patient was basically normal: alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 40 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 47 U/L, total bilirubin (TBil) 6.67 μmol/L, gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT) 32 U/L, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) 72 U/L, HBV-DNA was below the detection limit of 30 IU/mL, and the hepatobiliary sonography was also normal without liver fibrosis or cirrhosis. On 13 May 2026, the patient plans to receive the second cycle of anti-tumor therapy. However, in the pre-treatment examination, the laboratory tests showed that the liver function index of this patient was significantly abnormal (ALT 578 U/L, AST 589 U/L, TBil 13.50 μmol/L, GGT 67 U/L, ALP 83 U/L). In addition, the HBV-DNA detection result is 1320 IU/mL, which indicates that HBV was reactivated. Due to liver injury and HBVr, the anti-tumor therapy was postponed. Meanwhile, considering that entecavir had been unable to control HBV replication and HBV had the risk of further activation, tenofovir disoproxil fumarate was used instead of entecavir to strengthen anti-HBV therapy. Seven days later (20 May 2026), the laboratory tests showed the HBV-DNA quantification returned to the lower detection limit (< 30 IU/mL) again, and the liver function index of this patient has also declined significantly (ALT 240 U/L, AST 258 U/L, TBil 11.55 μmol/L, GGT 131 U/L, ALP 126 U/L), suggesting HBVr is the cause of liver injury. G6PD deficiency is one of the most common enzymopathies in the world, affecting an estimated hundreds of millions of people, with particularly high prevalence in parts of Africa, the Mediterranean, the Middle East, and southern and eastern Asia. The condition arises from mutations in the gene encoding glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, an enzyme critical to the pentose phosphate pathway that protects red blood cells from oxidative damage. Individuals with the deficiency are typically asymptomatic but can experience acute hemolytic anemia when exposed to certain drugs, infections, fava beans, or other oxidative stressors. Because vaccination involves an immune stimulus and, in some contexts, mild oxidative stress, researchers have long wondered whether children with G6PD deficiency might respond differently to routine immunizations, either in terms of antibody production or in terms of adverse reactions. Hepatitis B vaccination is a cornerstone of global infant immunization programs. The vaccine works by presenting the hepatitis B surface antigen, a purified viral protein, to the immune system, prompting the generation of antibodies known as anti-HBs. A concentration of at least 10 milli-international units per milliliter is conventionally regarded as the threshold of seroprotection, meaning the child is considered protected against chronic hepatitis B virus infection. Antibody levels wane over time in many vaccinated individuals, but immune memory generally persists, and the durability of the initial response is an important indicator of how well a vaccine schedule is performing in a given population. Whether G6PD status alters this durability had not been rigorously examined in young children before this study. A prospective multicentre trial now adds an important layer of nuance to that prediction, showing that the coarse label of ethnicity fails to capture clinically meaningful differences in off-treatment outcomes, whereas genomic ancestry and hepatitis B virus genotype do carry relevant signal. The study, known as COIN-B and published in Nature Communications, followed 91 patients who discontinued long-term nucleos(t)ide analogue therapy and tracked their viral and serological course over 72 weeks. Nucleos(t)ide analogues such as entecavir and tenofovir suppress hepatitis B virus replication with remarkable potency, but they rarely eradicate the reservoir of covalently closed circular DNA that persists in hepatocytes. For HBeAg-negative patients without cirrhosis, treatment guidelines therefore allow, and in some settings encourage, a finite course of therapy followed by careful monitoring. The rationale is that stopping drugs can provoke an immune-mediated flare that, in a minority of patients, leads to durable viral control or even loss of hepatitis B surface antigen, the closest available surrogate of a functional cure. The challenge is that relapse is common, flares can be severe, and clinicians have lacked reliable tools to identify who will benefit and who will simply rebound. Ethnicity has repeatedly been proposed as one such tool. Observational studies across Europe and Asia have suggested that patients of Asian descent relapse more frequently after treatment cessation than those of European background, and some guidelines have quietly incorporated this notion into patient selection. The problem, as the COIN-B investigators argue, is that ethnicity is a subjective and inconsistently applied construct, blending self-identification, place of birth, language and social factors into a category whose biological content is undefined. To test whether the apparent ethnic effect is real, the team designed a trial that would measure it prospectively and then dissect it with more rigorous instruments: genome-wide ancestry markers and viral genotype. All participants stopped their antiviral drug under protocol and were followed intensively for 72 weeks, with serial measurements of HBV DNA, hepatitis B surface antigen, liver biochemistry and, where applicable, retreatment when predefined safety or virological thresholds were crossed. Of the 91 patients who discontinued treatment, 84 completed the full 72-week protocol, providing a substantial prospective dataset for the prespecified analyses. The primary endpoint, off-treatment viral control at week 72, did not differ between the two prespecified ethnic groups. Among 41 patients classified as Caucasian, 41.5 percent maintained viral control, compared with 30.2 percent among 43 non-Caucasian patients, a difference that fell well short of statistical significance with a p value of 0.28. On its face, this negative result might seem to close the door on the ethnicity hypothesis. But the exploratory analyses that followed revealed a more interesting picture, one in which the blunt ethnic dichotomy had been masking genuine heterogeneity that finer-grained variables could detect. Hepatitis B surface antigen loss, the secondary endpoint and the most desirable outcome short of eradication, occurred in 7.1 percent of participants overall, and every single case arose in a patient with predominantly European or North African and Middle Eastern ancestry. Conversely, a decline of at least one log in surface antigen levels, an accepted on-treatment marker of a favorable immune trajectory, was significantly less frequent among patients with Asian or Sub-Saharan African ancestry, with a p value of 0.01. These findings suggest that ancestry-related factors, whether immunogenetic, viral or both, do influence the probability of a functional cure after treatment withdrawal. The viral side of the equation proved equally informative. Hepatitis B virus is classified into at least ten genotypes with distinct geographic distributions, and genotype has long been suspected to modulate disease course and treatment response. In the COIN-B analysis, lower hepatitis B surface antigen levels at the end of treatment independently predicted viral control, surface antigen loss and meaningful surface antigen decline, confirming that this biomarker remains the strongest single predictor available to clinicians. But even after adjusting for end-of-treatment surface antigen, patients infected with genotype C or genotype D achieved less viral control and required retreatment more often than those infected with genotype A. The result aligns with a growing body of evidence that viral genotype is not a passive phylogenetic label but a biologically active determinant of how the infection behaves once drug pressure is removed. Second, the results argue for more granular characterization of patients considered for treatment withdrawal: quantified genomic ancestry, viral genotype and, above all, carefully measured end-of-treatment surface antigen levels. The goal is no longer to ask whether a patient’s ethnicity predicts relapse, but to identify the specific host and viral factors that ancestry and genotype merely tag.
