The study aimed to investigate the evolutionary dynamics and phylodynamics of Andes orthohantavirus (ANDV) following the 2026 outbreak, as ANDV represents a notable exception within the Orthohantavirus genus due to its unique capacity for sustained person-to-person transmission.

CD8 + regulatory T cells limit immunopathology but impair viral clearance in Hantaan virus infection.

Authors: Kang Tang, Xiao-Yue Xu, He Liu, Yu-Si Zhang, Chun-Mei Zhang, Kai Li, Jia-Jia Zuo, Ke-Xuan Zhao, Xu-Yang Zheng, Feng-Lan Wang
Original abstract (English)
In hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) caused by Hantaan virus (HTNV), virus-specific CD8 + T cells mediate both viral clearance and immunopathology. Here, we characterized a CD8 + regulatory T cell (Treg) subset restricted by HLA-E (and its murine ortholog Qa-1) in HFRS patients and in a murine HTNV infection model. CD8 + Tregs (CD122 + Eomes + CD49d lo KIR + ), a subset substantially enriched for HLA-E-restricted T cells, were significantly reduced in peripheral blood of HFRS patients and displayed a terminally differentiated profile with elevated expression of KLRG-1, NKG2A, CTLA-4, and Fas-L, correlating with disease severity. Two HTNV glycoprotein-derived nonapeptides that bound Qa-1 were identified. Following immunization with the identified HTNV peptides and subsequent HTNV challenge, wild-type (WT) mice showed attenuated renal immunopathology, whereas D227K knock-in mice (with disrupted Qa-1-CD8/TCR interaction) showed enhanced viral clearance in the spleen and kidney but exacerbated renal immunopathology. Mechanistically, Qa-1-restricted CD8 + Tregs from WT mice suppressed effector CD8 + T cell proliferation in a peptide-specific, CD8/TCR-dependent manner via enhanced TGF-β secretion. Strikingly, D227K mutation dichotomously reshaped Treg phenotype: bulk polyclonal CD8 + Tregs displayed compensatory upregulation of inhibitory receptors (NKG2A, CTLA-4, PD-1) and TGF-β, whereas epitope-specific CD8 + Tregs showed impaired antigen-specific suppressive function and attenuated TGF-β production. Collectively, these findings indicate that HLA-E/Qa-1-restricted CD8 + Tregs can restrain immunopathology during HTNV infection, potentially at the cost of delayed viral elimination. Modulation of this axis may provide insights for therapeutic strategies in HFRS and related viral hemorrhagic fevers.

Original abstract (English)
Hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) is a rodent-borne zoonosis of public health concern in West Kazakhstan, yet long-term studies linking human and reservoir surveillance are scarce. We analyzed laboratory-confirmed HFRS cases (2001–2025) and routine rodent surveillance data from five districts of the West Kazakhstan Region within a One Health framework. Associations were estimated using negative binomial regression with district fixed effects and year-clustered robust standard errors (2002–2024; 115 district-years). Overall, 314 cases were recorded (81.2% male), with a major outbreak in 2005 (85 cases) and a resurgence in 2024–2025 (27 and 41 cases). Burlinsky District reported the most cases (44.9%), whereas Baiterek District had the highest incidence in 2016–2025. Of 132,694 rodents examined, 1083 (0.82%) were hantavirus antigen-positive. A larger share of the annual Mus musculus catch obtained in spring–summer was associated with higher case counts (incidence rate ratio 1.051 per percentage point; 95% CI: 1.024–1.080), and this was robust to excluding outbreak years. The number of positive rodents was associated with cases, but not after standardization for sampling effort. These associations do not establish M. musculus as a reservoir and are consistent with, but do not demonstrate, increased peridomestic exposure.

The origin of the MV Hondius Andes outbreak

Authors: Lissette Ulloa-Zepeda, Edyth Parker, Karthik Gangavarapu, Andrés Castillo, Catalina Pavez, Jorge Fernández, Marcelo Vilches, Pablo Vial, Andrew Rambaut, Kristian G. Andersen
Original abstract (English)
In April-July 2026, Andes virus (ANDV) caused an outbreak aboard the MV Hondius cruise ship resulting in 12 confirmed cases and three deaths. Before symptom onset, the index case had travelled through hantavirus-endemic regions of Chile and Argentina, leaving the geographic origin of infection unresolved. We conducted phylogenetic and phylogeographic analyses using newly generated ANDV genomes from human cases and rodent reservoirs to investigate the likely origin of the outbreak. Through phylogenetic analyses, we show that the outbreak genome falls within a geographically structured lineage spanning southern Chile and Argentine Andean Patagonia. Using phylogeographic reconstructions across all three segments, integrated with the index case's travel itinerary, we identified the Toltén-Lanco area of southern Chile as the most likely geographic origin of the outbreak genome, with two candidate locations accounting for >98% of the posterior support. However, the index case visited this area 62-66 days before symptom onset, exceeding the conventionally reported ANDV incubation period of 7-42 days. Thus, while genomic evidence supports a southern Chilean origin of the outbreak genome, the timing and circumstances of transmission to the index case remain unresolved.

Authors: P. Palanivelu
Original abstract (English)
The proofreading 3′→5′ exonuclease not only represents a crucial class of enzymes responsible for maintaining genome integrity across diverse groups of organisms, but also plays a critical role in ensuring replication fidelity. In the non-segmented, positive-strand bat-borne RNA viruses like Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronaviruses (SARS-CoVs and MERS-CoV), it is found along with the methyltransferase (MTase) gene of the non-structural protein 14 (NSP14). In contrast, in the non-segmented, but negative-strand bat-borne RNA viruses such as Marburg, Ebola and Nipah viruses, it has been identified together with a novel type of capping enzyme, viz. guanosine diphosphate polyribonucleotidyltransferase (PRNTase). Hantaviruses, one of the fatal and emerging human pathogens in recent times, belong to segmented, negative-strand RNA viruses and are primarily rodent-borne. Currently, no universally approved vaccine or antiviral therapy exists for all hantavirus infections. The presence of a proofreading (PR) exonuclease domain has not been reported in hantaviruses until now. Interestingly, a putative PR exonuclease domain has been identified for the first time in these viruses within the large L protein, preceding the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp). The newly identified domain belongs to the DEDD superfamily, but exhibits a distinct structural deviation, notably the replacement of the first catalytic triad -DxE- in the Old World hantaviruses with an -NxE- motif in most of the New World hantaviruses, and the use of an invariant tyrosine instead of histidine at the proton acceptor site. This feature closely resembles the DEDD superfamily of PR exonucleases reported in bacterial DNA polymerases I and II and in the DNA-dependent DNA polymerases of DNA viruses. To the best of my knowledge, this study provides the first evidence for the presence of a typical DEDD superfamily of PR exonuclease domain in hantaviruses, and thus, expanding our understanding of the structure and catalytic mechanism of 3′→5′ PR exonucleases in segmented, negative-strand RNA viruses and further presenting a novel structural target for the development of universally effective antiviral therapies against fatal hantavirus infections.

"WHO is facilitating medical evacuation of two symptomatic passengers, conducting a full risk assessment, and supporting affected people onboard," Ghebreysus wrote. What is hantavirus? Hantaviruses are a family of viruses that can cause serious illnesses and death, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). The viruses cause two syndromes: hantavirus pulmonary syndrome (HPS) and hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS). HPS is mostly found in the Western Hemisphere, including the U.S., and HFRS is mostly found in Europe and Asia. Stock photo of a colorized electron micrograph of the Hantavirus. Alfred Pasieka/Science Photo Lib/STOCK PHOTO/Getty Images Surveillance for hantavirus in the U.S. began in 1993 during an outbreak in the Four Corners region, where Arizona, Colorado, New Mexico and Utah meet, the CDC said. Between 1993 and the end of 2023, 890 cases of hantavirus disease have been reported in the U.S., according to the CDC. Hantavirus became more widely known after being identified as the cause of death of the wife of actor Gene Hackman after the couple was found dead at their New Mexico home in February 2025. How does hantavirus spread? Hantaviruses are usually spread through rodents, including rats and mice, mostly from exposure to urine, droppings or saliva. Although the viruses can spread through a rodent bite or scratch, such infection is rare, the CDC says. Hantaviruses may also spread from person to person but that also is rare and only suspected for one subtype from South America, according to the WHO. What are the hantavirus symptoms? HPS symptoms typically appear from one to eight weeks after contact with the virus, with early signs including fever, fatigue and muscle aches, according to the CDC. Half of HPS patients will experience headaches, chills, dizziness, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and abdominal pain. The CDC says that between four and 10 days after the initial phase of illness, the late symptoms will appear, including coughing, shortness of breath and tightness in the chest as the lungs fill with fluid. Symptoms of HFRS usually develop one to two weeks after exposure and typically include intense headaches, fever, chills, back pain, abdominal pain, nausea and vomiting, the CDC says. Later symptoms may include low blood pressure, lack of blood flow, internal bleeding and acute kidney failure. Depending on the virus causing the infection, fatality among infected humans can range from less than 1% to as high as 15%, the CDC says. What hantavirus treatments are available? No specific treatment is available for hantavirus infection, with the CDC recommending patients receive supportive care such as rest, hydration and treatment of symptoms. Because HPS can cause breathing difficulties, patients may need breathing support, such as intubation, the CDC says. Because HFRS can disrupt kidney function, infected patients may need dialysis to remove toxins from the blood, according to the CDC. Original source text (English)
HANTAAN virus research reveals how regulatory immune cells protect against kidney damage while potentially delaying viral clearance . New findings highlight a complex relationship between antiviral immunity and tissue injury in hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS), identifying CD8+ regulatory T cells as potential targets for future therapeutic strategies. Hantaan virus infection can cause HFRS, a potentially severe condition characterized by renal injury and immune dysfunction. Although virus-specific CD8+ T cells contribute to viral elimination, their activity can also trigger damaging inflammatory responses. Researchers investigated a subset of CD8+ regulatory T cells restricted by the immune recognition molecule HLA-E, examining their characteristics in patients with HFRS and their function in a mouse model of infection. Blood samples from patients with HFRS revealed significantly reduced levels of these regulatory T cells. The remaining cells exhibited features of terminal differentiation, including increased expression of inhibitory and regulatory markers KLRG-1, NKG2A, CTLA-4, and Fas-L. These changes correlated with disease severity, suggesting that altered immune regulation may contribute to more severe Hantaan virus infection. To investigate the mechanisms underlying these findings, researchers identified two Hantaan virus glycoprotein peptides capable of binding Qa-1, the mouse equivalent of HLA-E. Following peptide immunization and subsequent viral challenge, wild-type mice demonstrated reduced renal immunopathology. However, genetically modified mice with disrupted Qa-1 interactions showed improved viral clearance in the kidneys and spleen but experienced more severe renal tissue damage. The findings demonstrate that regulatory immune responses can protect against inflammatory injury while simultaneously restricting the body’s ability to eliminate infection. Further experiments showed that Qa-1-restricted CD8+ regulatory T cells suppressed the proliferation of antiviral effector T cells through peptide-specific interactions and increased transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) secretion. Disrupting these interactions impaired antigen-specific immune suppression, despite compensatory increases in inhibitory markers among the broader regulatory T cell population. The findings identify the HLA-E/CD8+ regulatory T cell pathway as a potential target for host-directed therapies in HFRS and related viral hemorrhagic fevers. However, therapeutic strategies would need to balance the benefits of limiting inflammatory kidney damage against the potential consequences of delayed viral clearance. SCIENTISTS have revealed how New World hantaviruses recognise a human cell receptor, providing structural insights that could support the development of future antiviral treatments. Hantaviruses are rodent-borne pathogens that can cause severe disease in humans, yet no approved therapeutics are available. New World hantaviruses, including Andes virus (ANDV), use the cell-surface receptor protocadherin-1 (PCDH1) to enter host cells. However, the structural basis of this interaction has remained unclear. The study, published in Nature, describes the structure of PCDH1 bound to the ANDV spike at near-atomic resolution, clarifying how the virus engages its receptor. Revealing the Receptor-Binding Mechanism The researchers found that PCDH1 binds asymmetrically within a deep central pocket formed by four Gn protomers in the viral spike. This recessed binding site helps explain why PCDH1 recognises only intact viral particles or soluble tetramers, rather than isolated components. The findings identify a structural strategy through which hantaviruses can conceal their receptor-binding site within a depression on the viral surface. Understanding this arrangement provides insight into how the virus establishes the interaction needed for cell entry. Viral Loops Shape Recognition Mutagenesis experiments identified three Gn loops that shape the receptor-binding site. The researchers also demonstrated that these structural features can enable PCDH1 binding to be transferred from New World to Old World hantavirus glycoproteins. These findings help define the viral features responsible for receptor recognition and offer a clearer understanding of the structural determinants governing interactions between hantaviruses and host cells. Antibodies Block Hantavirus Entry Structural and functional analyses of four neutralising antibodies revealed two distinct mechanisms for blocking receptor binding. Some antibodies occluded access to the PCDH1-binding site, while others prevented receptor engagement through steric interference. Together, these results explain how antibodies can counter the virus’s recessed receptor-binding strategy. The structural framework established by the study could inform the design of antiviral therapeutics that target the hantavirus spike, addressing an important gap in treatment options for these potentially severe infections. Although the findings provide a foundation for future therapeutic development, further work will be needed to translate these structural insights into effective treatments. Reference Battini L et al.
