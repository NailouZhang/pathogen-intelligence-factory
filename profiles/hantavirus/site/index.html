Development of a rapid RPA-CRISPR/Cas12b assay for dual detection of Hantaan virus and Seoul virus.

Authors: Guo Ruowen, Tang Haitao, Fu Hongkun, Wang Weilin, Yu Baosheng, Xiong Mengya, Xu Jia, Hu Chaojie, Chen Xiaoyu, Hai-Tao Tang
Original abstract (English)
Hantaan virus (HTNV) and Seoul virus (SEOV) are the primary causative agents of hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS), presenting a persistent challenge to global public health. Prompt and precise diagnostic tools are essential for clinical investigation and the containment of HFRS outbreaks. Leveraging the programmable precision of CRISPR/Cas12b system, which has significantly advanced the field of molecular diagnostics, we developed a rapid, high-sensitivity assay for the dual detection of HTNV and SEOV. Each virus was detected in a separate, target-specific single-tube reaction. The assay combines recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) with CRISPR/Cas12b-mediated fluorescence detection and uses virus-specific sgRNAs and RPA primers targeting conserved genomic regions. Using recombinant plasmid standards, both targets were detected at 1 × 10 1 copies/test in three technical replicates; this is a preliminary analytical detection threshold and does not incorporate extraction losses or matrix-associated inhibition. No cross-reactivity was observed within the evaluated four-organism non-target panel. In a preliminary feasibility assessment, all nine RT-qPCR-positive rodent samples (seven HTNV and two SEOV) were positive by the RPA-CRISPR/Cas12b assay, indicating complete positive agreement in this all-positive sample set. Because no negative samples were included, negative agreement, diagnostic specificity, false-positive rate, and overall clinical diagnostic accuracy could not be assessed. These results support further development of the assay as a potential screening tool; larger studies using well-characterized positive and negative clinical or field specimens are required.

An ONT sequencing framework for hantavirus genome assembly, variant analysis, and defective viral genome characterization from whole-segment amplicons

Authors: Lena Vandenabeele, Frances M. Twohig, Anne Tuiskunen Back, Magnus Sjoblom, Rebecca Lantto, Gabriella Svala, Janne Tynell, Sandra Broddesson, Robert Dyrdak, Wanda Christ
Original abstract (English)
Orthohantaviruses are zoonotic, tri-segmented negative-sense RNA viruses of rodents and other small mammals that can cause severe human disease. Whole genome sequencing remains difficult because of low viral loads, high sequence diversity, and an inherent instability of the viral L segment. Current tiled amplicon and hybrid-capture approaches do not capture the heterogeneity of virus populations, including defective viral genomes (DVGs), which have never been described for hantaviruses. Here, we present an Oxford Nanopore Technologies sequencing framework based on universal reverse transcription primers targeting the conserved genome termini of orthohantaviruses and a single species-specific primer pair per segment, enabling amplification and sequencing of complete S, M, and L segments. We also developed an accompanying bioinformatic pipeline that performs reference-guided assembly of hantavirus genomes, consensus generation, variant calling, and DVG classification using the newly developed ORCHID classification pipeline. We applied this approach to diverse Puumala virus strains from infected cell cultures and bank voles, to representative Old World and New World orthohantaviruses, and to highly pathogenic New World viruses in vivo. In all cases we were able to accurately reconstruct full hantavirus genomes. Notably, we found that hantavirus populations contain highly abundant DVGs and conserved L segment deletions, whereas the S and M segments remained largely intact. Together, this work provides a low-cost, broadly applicable and easily customisable workflow for hantavirus genomics and reveals DVGs as a previously unrecognized feature of hantavirus populations.

Original abstract (English)

Hantaviruses as Emerging Zoonotic Pathogens: Molecular Biology, Epidemiology, Therapeutics, vaccine, and Pathogenesis.

Authors: Mohammad Mehdi Akbarin, Zahra Farjami, Hugo Ramírez Álvarez
Original abstract (English)
Hantaviruses are globally distributed zoonotic pathogens belonging to the family Hantaviridae and are responsible for two major human diseases, hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) and hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome (HCPS). Despite substantial progress in hantavirus research, important challenges remain in understanding the interactions among viral evolution, reservoir ecology, environmental change, transmission dynamics, and host immunopathology. This review provides an integrated One Health perspective that connects molecular virology and host-virus interactions with transmission ecology, global epidemiology, and the mechanisms underlying vascular leakage and severe disease. In addition to summarizing established knowledge, we highlight recent advances in hantavirus taxonomy, genomic and molecular surveillance, ecological and transmission modeling, and the identification of hantavirus circulation in previously underrecognized regions. Particular emphasis is placed on the role of reservoir host dynamics, climate and environmental change, habitat disturbance, and human-wildlife interactions in shaping spillover risk, as well as on endothelial dysfunction, immune dysregulation, and coagulation abnormalities as interconnected mechanisms of disease severity. We also identify persistent knowledge gaps concerning host susceptibility, viral persistence, determinants of differential virulence and tissue tropism, interspecies transmission, and the development of effective antiviral therapies and vaccines. By integrating these complementary perspectives, this review provides a framework for understanding hantavirus emergence and pathogenesis while identifying priorities for future surveillance, prevention, and therapeutic research.

新出现和重新出现的 RNA 病毒感染中的血管计划:SARS-CoV-2、黄病毒和汉坦病毒的比较经验教训

Vascular Programs in Emerging and Re-Emerging RNA Virus Infections: Comparative Lessons from SARS-CoV-2, Flaviviruses, and Hantaviruses
作者： Silvia Beltrami, Giovanni Strazzabosco, Giovanna Schiuma, Sabrina Rizzo, Marcello Baroni, Daria Bortolotti
摘要中文翻译新出现和重新出现的RNA病毒经常出现超出经典器官向性的临床表型,包括血管渗漏、血小板失调、凝血失衡、微血管损伤和血栓炎症。SARS-CoV-2、黄病毒和汉坦病毒提供了病毒诱导的血管疾病的三种互补模型。这篇叙述性综述通过共享的内皮-凝血框架对这些病毒进行了比较,并提出了四种部分重叠的血管程序:内皮激活、内皮屏障衰竭、凝血激活和血小板定量或定性功能障碍。这些程序旨在作为临床、病理和实验证据的概念综合,而不是作为定量分类系统。该框架不仅用于组织已知的机制,还用于为交叉病毒生物标志物组、器官特异性内皮模型和表型引导的临床研究生成可测试的预测。严重的 COVID-19 说明了炎症驱动的内皮激活、血小板-白细胞相互作用、补体扩增、NETosis 和免疫血栓形成。在黄病毒组中,DENV 被用作全身血管渗漏、血小板减少和出血风险的主要模型;选定的非登革热黄病毒说明了共享的内皮、血小板和凝血模块如何被重定向至胎盘功能障碍(ZIKV)、神经侵袭(WNV 和 JEV)或肝衰竭和凝血病(YFV)。汉坦病毒感染提供了一种独特的毛细血管渗漏综合征模型,其中非溶解性内皮感染、免疫介导的损伤和渗透性信号传导聚集在肾和肺微血管床上。比较观点表明,血栓、出血和渗漏表型并不是相互排斥的类别,而是由病毒趋向性、血管床特化、免疫激活和 宿主 易感性塑造的重叠血管程序的不同结果。

Vascular Programs in Emerging and Re-Emerging RNA Virus Infections: Comparative Lessons from SARS-CoV-2, Flaviviruses, and Hantaviruses

Authors: Silvia Beltrami, Giovanni Strazzabosco, Giovanna Schiuma, Sabrina Rizzo, Marcello Baroni, Daria Bortolotti
Original abstract (English)
Emerging and re-emerging RNA viruses frequently present with clinical phenotypes that extend beyond classical organ tropism and include vascular leakage, platelet dysregulation, coagulation imbalance, microvascular injury, and thromboinflammation. SARS-CoV-2, flaviviruses, and hantaviruses provide three complementary models of virus-induced vascular disease. This narrative review compares these viruses through a shared endothelial–coagulative framework and proposes four partially overlapping vascular programs: endothelial activation, endothelial barrier failure, coagulation activation, and platelet quantitative or qualitative dysfunction. These programs are intended as a conceptual synthesis of convergent clinical, pathological, and experimental evidence rather than as a quantitative classification system. The framework is used not only to organize known mechanisms, but also to generate testable predictions for cross-virus biomarker panels, organ-specific endothelial models, and phenotype-guided clinical studies. Severe COVID-19 illustrates inflammation-driven endothelial activation, platelet–leukocyte interactions, complement amplification, NETosis, and immunothrombosis. Within the flavivirus group, DENV is used as the principal model for systemic vascular leakage, thrombocytopenia, and hemorrhagic risk; selected non-dengue flaviviruses illustrate how shared endothelial, platelet, and coagulation modules are redirected toward placental dysfunction (ZIKV), neuroinvasion (WNV and JEV), or hepatic failure and coagulopathy (YFV). Hantavirus infections provide a distinctive model of capillary leak syndrome in which non-lytic endothelial infection, immune-mediated injury, and permeability signaling converge on renal and pulmonary microvascular beds. A comparative view suggests that thrombotic, hemorrhagic, and leaky phenotypes are not mutually exclusive categories, but different outcomes of overlapping vascular programs shaped by viral tropism, vascular-bed specialization, immune activation, and host susceptibility.

Biocontainment Transport of Patients with High-Consequence Infectious Diseases: A Narrative Review and Integrated Operational Framework for Emergency Medical Systems

Authors: Federico D’Urso, Mara Gracy Basile, Edoardo Falcone, Ulrico Angeloni
Original abstract (English)
Background/Objectives: The transport of patients with high-consequence infectious diseases (HCIDs) links biosafety science and emergency medicine. The 2026 Bundibugyo virus disease epidemic and the multinational Andes virus outbreak associated with an expedition cruise ship have renewed attention on how such patients are moved safely between the community, hospitals and high-level isolation units. This narrative review synthesises the international literature on biocontainment transport and integrates it into an operational framework for emergency medical systems. Methods: A structured search of PubMed/MEDLINE and Scopus (to 10 October 2026), complemented by guidance from the World Health Organization, the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and national authorities, was conducted and reported according to the SANRA scale. Evidence was organised across seven operational domains and combined with the operational experience of the Italian Red Cross biocontainment transport service. Results: Of 2432 records identified, 1485 unique records were screened. The literature is dominated by aeromedical case reports, technical evaluations and expert consensus, with few comparative studies. Recurrent findings concern device integrity and air quality within transport isolators, the risk of self-contamination during doffing, heat strain and communication limits imposed by personal protective equipment, decontamination of vehicles and isolators, the maintenance of team readiness during periods of low activity, and the psychological burden on patients and staff. These findings are integrated into a framework comprising a four-link containment chain, a seven-phase operational workflow, a three-tier complexity classification and a role-based responsibility matrix. Conclusions: Biocontainment transport is better understood as an integrated emergency system than as a device-centred intervention. The proposed framework is a structured, consensus-based reference for planning, training and quality assurance; its components require prospective evaluation.

Scope and question
The review focuses on the transport of patients with high-consequence infectious diseases (HCIDs), particularly those caused by hantaviruses, and aims to develop an integrated operational framework for emergency medical systems. The central question is how to safely move patients with hantavirus infections between different care settings while maintaining biosafety.

The review conducted a structured search of PubMed/MEDLINE and Scopus up to 10 October 2026, incorporating guidance from the World Health Organization, the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and national authorities. Consensus and conclusions
The literature is dominated by aeromedical case reports, technical evaluations, and expert consensus, with few comparative studies. Recurrent findings include device integrity and air quality within transport isolators, the risk of self-contamination during doffing, heat strain, communication limits imposed by personal protective equipment, decontamination of vehicles and isolators, team readiness during low-activity periods, and the psychological burden on patients and staff. These findings are integrated into a framework comprising a four-link containment chain, a seven-phase operational workflow, a three-tier complexity classification, and a role-based responsibility matrix.
Controversies and gaps
The literature lacks comparative studies, and the findings are primarily based on case reports and technical evaluations. There is limited evidence on the effectiveness of specific interventions, and the framework components require prospective evaluation. Additionally, the review notes that the operational experience of the Italian Red Cross biocontainment transport service is a key source of data, but this may not be representative of all emergency medical systems.
Research and practice implications
The framework proposed in the review is a structured, consensus-based reference for planning, training, and quality assurance in biocontainment transport. It highlights the need for prospective evaluation of its components and emphasizes the importance of integrating operational experience into the development of emergency medical systems. The review also underscores the necessity for further research on the effectiveness of interventions and the development of standardized protocols for biocontainment transport.

Hantaviruses are a family of viruses that can cause serious illnesses and death, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Sweety, Aman Pratap Singh/Infectious Diseases & Immunity Hantaviruses are carried by rodents and cause two serious illnesses in people: hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS), with an estimated 150,000 to 200,000 cases each year, mostly in Asia and Europe, and hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome (HCPS) in the Americas, which kills up to 40% of those affected. The infections returned to the headlines in May 2026, when the World Health Organization reported a cluster of Andes virus infections linked to the expedition cruise ship MV Hondius: 13 cases and 3 deaths, with more than 600 contacts traced across 32 countries, territories, and areas. Although the effects of hantaviruses on the kidneys, lungs, and blood are well described, the oral and salivary side of infection has received little attention. In an invited review published in Infectious Diseases & Immunity, Prof. Sukumaran Anil of Chulalongkorn University, Thailand, and Manipal Academy of Higher Education, India, with Dr. Vishnupriya K. Sweety and Dr. Aman Pratap Singh, brings together the virology, epidemiology, clinical features, diagnosis, and treatment of human hantavirus infection and examines what each means for the mouth and for dental care. Saliva and the one hantavirus that spreads between people Andes virus (ANDV), found in Argentina and Chile, is the only hantavirus for which person-to-person spread has been repeatedly documented, with deep kissing and sexual contact identified as risk factors. The review highlights laboratory evidence that ANDV antigen is present in the secretory cells of human salivary glands and that ANDV, unlike other hantaviruses, resists the natural antiviral activity of human saliva. In a Chilean study of 131 confirmed cases, viral RNA was detected in 30% of acute-phase gingival crevicular fluid (the fluid around the gums) sample and in 12% of acute-phase saliva samples, and replication-competent virus was recovered from 18 of 43 positive samples tested (42%). The authors stress that this makes an oral route biologically plausible but does not prove that saliva carries the infection from one person to another. Warning signs in the mouth A severe sore throat lasting 2 to 3 weeks, together with swelling of the face, neck, and throat, has been described early in hantavirus infection and is easily mistaken for ordinary pharyngitis or tonsillitis. Small hemorrhages on the soft palate may appear during the febrile phase of HFRS. In the later oliguric phase, platelet counts can fall below 20,000-30,000 per microliter, bringing a real risk of spontaneous gum bleeding and of bleeding after even minor oral procedures. Saliva as a diagnostic sample An oral fluid IgM test for Hantaan virus agreed closely with serum testing, with 96.7% sensitivity and 94.9% specificity. Because dental clinics handle saliva routinely, the authors propose that dental clinics be evaluated as sites for non-invasive surveillance in endemic communities, while cautioning that the test has not yet been validated in dental patients, in ANDV-endemic populations, or as a point-of-care device. What dental teams can do The review sets out a four-step approach: recognize, time the care, protect the team, and support the patient. Elective dental treatment should be deferred during active illness and resumed only after platelet and kidney function have recovered, in consultation with the treating physician. Unavoidable emergency procedures belong in a hospital setting with support to control bleeding. For suspected HCPS, FFP2 or N95 respirators and hand instruments are preferred over aerosol-generating equipment; for suspected ANDV infection, enhanced protective equipment and a pre-procedural mouthrinse are reasonable precautions. Patients treated with ribavirin may need help with mouth ulcers, taste changes, and dry mouth. The authors are explicit about the limits of the evidence. There are no clinical studies of dental care in hantavirus infection and no reported transmission to a dental worker, so the dental recommendations are precautionary and extrapolated from medical, virological, and general infection control evidence. Dentists are not expected to diagnose the disease; their role is to avoid dismissing these signs as routine and to refer promptly. “The dentist is unlikely to be the clinician who diagnoses hantavirus infection, but may be the first to notice that a sore throat has lasted far too long or that the gums are bleeding without explanation,” said Prof. Anil. “The cruise ship cluster showed that travel can carry this risk well beyond endemic regions, so every dental team should know these signs and when to refer.” The authors call for prospective studies that document oral findings systematically, longitudinal studies of salivary mucins and immunity, and validation of saliva-based tests in real-world and dental settings. Recombinant PCDH1 mutants engineered to remain monomeric did not reduce binding to a recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus displaying the Andes virus spike, whereas stabilizing PCDH1 dimers increased binding. Together, these observations suggest that Andes virus primarily engages monomeric PCDH1 at the cell surface, while transiently exposed EC1 domains on dynamic receptors might also contribute. Mutagenesis and sequence analysis identified three Gn loops—the AB, CD and DE loops—that help shape the PCDH1-binding surface. Structural and functional analysis of four human neutralizing antibodies demonstrated that the recessed pocket can be blocked in more than one way. ANDV-5 and MIB22 recognized epitopes adjacent to, but outside, the central cavity and directly occluded access to PCDH1. ADI-65534 and ANDV-34 did not directly compete with the receptor. The cavity itself is too narrow to accommodate a conventional antibody readily, explaining why the antibodies recognized sites around its rim rather than reaching deeply into the pocket. The separation between the receptor-binding and antibody-binding surfaces suggests a possible route to viral escape. PCDH1 contacts a relatively small and buried interface, whereas an antibody such as MIB22 engages a broader, more exposed surface. Changes that disrupt the larger antibody interface could therefore leave important elements of receptor recognition intact. Consistent with that possibility, Andes virus escape mutations associated with MIB22 occurred at residues distinct from, and more peripheral to, those critical for Andes virus–PCDH1 recognition. The study did not directly test receptor use by an escaped strain, so the structural separation remains a mechanism supported by the combined structural and mutational evidence rather than a demonstrated outcome in natural infections. The receptor pocket may also be relevant to how the spike transitions from attachment to membrane fusion. The PCDH1-binding cavity is formed by Gn and does not involve the fusion protein Gc, suggesting that receptor engagement and membrane fusion might be separate events in hantaviruses. Gn has also been reported to adopt a different tetrameric arrangement at acidic pH, leaving open the possibility that receptor binding could affect fusion activation, although the present structural work does not resolve that question. Antibodies designed to reach conserved residues at the base of the cavity might require unusually long complementarity-determining loops, single-domain antibodies or other biologics engineered for the confined space. Antibodies recognizing epitopes that are partly buried in the intact spike might alternatively prevent formation of the receptor-binding pocket itself. The findings offer a structural framework for hantavirus antiviral design, but they do not establish a treatment, preventive measure or clinical benefit. The experiments combined cryo-electron microscopy of PCDH1 bound to a soluble Andes virus spike and to virus-like particles, X-ray crystallography of an antibody–glycoprotein complex, mutagenesis, deep mutational scanning, receptor-binding measurements and infection assays using recombinant vesicular stomatitis viruses. These systems reproduced selected molecular and entry characteristics of hantaviruses, but the study did not evaluate natural infection in people, human vaccine responses, disease outcomes, or the safety and efficacy of candidate drugs. Atomic coordinates, raw sequencing data and analysis code were deposited in public repositories. By revealing that receptor recognition and antibody neutralization depend on precise three-dimensional relationships within the assembled spike, the work identifies a concentrated target for future molecular design while leaving its clinical translation unproven. Hantavirus [quantic69 / iStock / Getty Images Plus] Hantaviruses can turn exposure to infected rodents into a life-threatening illness, including a pulmonary syndrome that can progress rapidly to respiratory failure. Andes virus adds another concern: unlike other hantaviruses known to cause human disease, it can also spread between people. Understanding how it first attaches to human cells could help researchers identify ways to interrupt infection. Researchers at Albert Einstein College of Medicine in New York, Institut Pasteur in Paris, and LSU Health Shreveport have now resolved that interaction in near-atomic detail. Combining cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) and functional experiments, they showed how the Andes virus spike captures a human receptor—and how antibodies interfere. The study, published in Nature , is titled “ Structural basis for protocadherin-1 recognition by New World hantaviruses .” Previous research established that Andes virus and other New World hantaviruses use the cell-surface protein protocadherin-1 (PCDH1) to enter host cells. What remained unclear was how the viral spike recognized this receptor. An isolated spike subunit could not bind PCDH1; the interaction required the assembled, four-part structure. To examine that assembly, the team engineered a soluble Andes virus spike using methods that included an artificial intelligence–designed scaffold. The scaffold helped stabilize the square-shaped complex outside the virus, allowing the researchers to combine it with PCDH1 and analyze the resulting structure using cryo-EM. rVSV-ANDV infected cells (green) and PCDH1 bound to them (pink) [Albert Einstein College of Medicine] The images revealed that PCDH1 binds asymmetrically within a deep central pocket formed by four copies of the viral Gn glycoprotein. Rather than presenting a receptor-binding site on an individual subunit, the spike builds one through the arrangement of its components. Mutagenesis experiments identified three Gn loops that help shape the binding site. Changes involving these loops also enabled the researchers to transfer PCDH1-binding ability from New World to Old World hantavirus glycoproteins, according to the paper. The team also investigated four neutralizing antibodies. PCDH1’s long, slender shape lets it reach deep inside, but most antibodies are too bulky to follow,” said Rohit Jangra, PhD, assistant professor of microbiology and immunology at LSU Health Shreveport. The team intends to explore this vulnerability as a basis for antiviral strategies against Andes virus and potentially related pathogens, including Sin Nombre virus. They cause two life-threatening clinical syndromes: hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) across Eurasia and hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome (HCPS) in the Americas, the latter carrying case fatality rates that can approach or exceed 50 percent. Despite decades of research and recurring outbreaks, including a highly publicized Andes virus outbreak aboard a cruise ship in April 2026, there is still no universally licensed antiviral or vaccine against orthohantavirus infection. A recent review published in Frontiers in Cellular and Infection Microbiology by researchers at the University of São Paulo and Institut Pasteur de São Paulo systematically maps the viral life cycle for chemotherapeutic intervention, evaluating entry inhibitors, polymerase blockers, nucleocapsid disruptors, and host-directed strategies while advocating for computational approaches to accelerate discovery. The authors frame their analysis around the fundamental steps of the orthohantavirus infection cycle. Viral entry begins when the Gn glycoprotein docks onto host surface receptors, including β3-integrins and protocadherin-1 (PCDH1). Once internalized via endocytosis, the acidification of the endosomal compartment triggers a conformational change in the viral envelope that drives membrane fusion, releasing the viral genome into the cytoplasm. The viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) then initiates both transcription and genome replication. Structural proteins and newly synthesized RNA genomes assemble at the Golgi complex or plasma membrane before budding out as mature infectious virions. Each of these stages presents a distinct molecular vulnerability that chemists and virologists can exploit with rationally designed small molecules, peptides, or biologics. Among the most extensively characterized entry inhibitors are cyclic peptides identified through phage display libraries. Early work identified cyclic nonapeptides that bind the β3-integrin receptor used by pathogenic orthohantaviruses such as Sin Nombre virus (SNV), Hantaan virus (HTNV), Seoul virus (SEOV), and Puumala virus (PUUV) to enter human endothelial cells. Of seventy sequences screened, eleven showed inhibitory effects exceeding 80 percent against SNV in vitro, with the most potent, CPFVKTQLC, demonstrating high inhibitory potency when presented on phage particles. This peptide was subsequently refined through alanine substitution and deletion scanning to a minimal functional unit, the cyclic pentapeptide cyclo-[CPFVC]. However, because free monovalent peptides proved less effective than phage-displayed versions, researchers pivoted toward multivalent presentation on nanoparticles, targeting the viral glycoproteins directly rather than the host receptor. When the peptide CLVRNLAWC was displayed on 100-nanometer nanoparticles at a 4:1 ratio, inhibition of SNV rose from 9 percent as a free peptide to 32.5 percent. The peptide CQATTARNC achieved even greater efficacy, surpassing 50 percent inhibition when the nanoparticle-to-virus ratio was increased to 20:1. Building on these strategies, investigators extended the approach to Andes virus (ANDV) by eluting peptide libraries from purified virions using monoclonal antibodies against the Gn and Gc glycoproteins. A key finding was that combining peptides targeting different Gn- and Gc-specific sequences produced synergistic inhibition significantly more powerful than single-peptide treatments, underscoring the value of simultaneously engaging multiple viral epitopes. To transition from biological peptides to drug-like molecules, researchers used the three-dimensional structure of the refined pentapeptide cyclo-[CPFVC] in a ligand-based virtual screening campaign evaluating over 600,000 small molecules. This effort identified compounds such as G319–0078 and 8012-0652, which were approximately 2,000 times more potent than the original cyclic peptides, achieving high-affinity inhibition in the nanomolar range with EC50 values between 1.3 and 2.5 nanomolar in vitro. These molecules represent a compelling proof of concept that computational screening can transform a modest peptide lead into a highly potent preclinical candidate warranting further in vivo evaluation. A distinct strategy targets the membrane fusion process itself. The orthohantavirus Gc glycoprotein functions as a class II fusion protein, and completing membrane fusion requires a major structural relocation of Domain III (DIII) to achieve the post-fusion state. Exploiting this mechanical vulnerability, researchers demonstrated that viral entry can be blocked using recombinant fragments and synthetic peptides derived from the Gc DIII and stem region. These fragments allow initial Gc trimerization to proceed but ultimately prevent membrane hemifusion and fusion pore formation. The recombinant DIII fragment inhibited ANDV fusion by approximately 70 percent at 25 micromolar and demonstrated cross-reactivity by reducing PUUV-mediated fusion by 50 percent. Even greater potency was observed with the R2 peptide, derived from the C-terminal portion of the Gc stem region, which inhibited ANDV fusion by up to 95 percent in vitro. Although efficacy dropped to a 70 percent cross-reduction against PUUV-mediated fusion at 20 micromolar due to high genetic divergence between the two viruses, the results still demonstrate meaningful cross-protective potential. Natural products have also yielded promising entry inhibitors. Griffithsin (GRFT), an algae-derived lectin with demonstrated efficacy against coronaviruses, retroviruses, lentiviruses, flaviviruses, hepaciviruses, paramyxoviruses, and herpesviruses, potently inhibits ANDV entry in vitro. A synthetic trimeric variant possessing nine oligosaccharide-binding sites showed even greater potency against ANDV and SNV. In vivo, GRFT inhibited HTNV and provided partial protection in suckling mice. Its scalability and ease of purification highlight its translational potential. Separately, bovine lactoferrin (LF) prevents initial viral adsorption and adhesion of SEOV, likely by interacting with cellular glycosaminoglycans such as heparin or by sterically hindering virus binding to host β-integrin and lectin receptors. Prophylactic administration of LF one day before orthohantavirus inoculation improved survival rates in mice up to 70 percent at 160 milligrams per kilogram, with a double-administration regimen enhancing survival to over 94 percent. When combined with subsequent ribavirin treatment, the two agents functioned synergistically, profoundly reducing viral shedding and completely inhibiting viral protein expression. Discovered through high-throughput screening of over 2,300 approved drugs and traditional Chinese medicines, cepharanthine (CEP), a compound isolated from Stephania cephalantha Hayata, has emerged as a potent viral entry and membrane fusion inhibitor. In vitro, CEP inhibited HTNV pseudovirus with an EC50 of 0.9 micromolar and reduced viral nucleic acid copies by 73 percent at 20 micromolar. In vivo, administration to BALB/c mice five hours post-infection significantly reduced infection by 93 percent at 90 milligrams per kilogram per day and 94 percent at 180 milligrams per kilogram per day. Given its established safety profile, CEP represents a high-priority candidate for repurposing and advancement into human clinical trials against orthohantaviruses. On the replication front, ribavirin remains the historical benchmark for anti-hantaviral therapy. Once transported into host cells, ribavirin is phosphorylated into metabolites including ribavirin-5′-monophosphate, which inhibits inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase and depletes intracellular GTP, and ribavirin-5′-triphosphate, which selectively interacts with the viral RdRp as its primary mechanism of action. Incorporation of ribavirin leads to accumulation of genomic errors and dramatic loss of virus viability through error catastrophe. Preclinical evaluation confirmed that early ribavirin administration reduces viral loads and prevents severe disease in suckling mouse models infected with HTNV and hamster models infected with ANDV. Clinically, intravenous ribavirin demonstrated efficacy in a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 242 patients with HTNV-induced HFRS, significantly reducing hemorrhagic and overall mortality. However, efficacy varies significantly across strains and clinical syndromes. In the context of HCPS caused by ANDV, ribavirin intervention eliminated mortality compared to a 35 percent historical case-fatality rate and reduced the incidence of the severe cardiopulmonary phase from 84 to 57 percent. Conversely, in a larger placebo-controlled, double-blinded trial evaluating intravenous ribavirin for HCPS, the drug failed to demonstrate overall therapeutic efficacy, revealing no significant difference in survival between groups. Trials in HFRS caused by PUUV also showed insufficient efficacy and safety, likely due to delayed drug administration and a weaker correlation between viral load and disease severity compared to HTNV infection. Furthermore, the clinical utility of ribavirin is constrained by dose-limiting toxicities, most notably reversible hemolytic anemia reported in 71 percent of recipients. Favipiravir (T-705), originally discovered during a screening for anti-influenza compounds, has emerged as a potent viral replication inhibitor. Upon cellular entry, host enzymes convert favipiravir into its active form, favipiravir-RTP, a purine nucleotide analogue incorporated into nascent viral RNA that induces lethal mutagenesis. Favipiravir potently inhibited ANDV and SNV in vitro and provided 100 percent survival in lethal ANDV hamster models when treatment was initiated before viremia, making it an important candidate for post-exposure prophylaxis. Combination with ribavirin produced highly synergistic effects, with peak antiviral activity observed at a ratio of 12 micromolar favipiravir to 18.75 micromolar ribavirin, achieving greater than 99 percent overall inhibition of HTNV. Additionally, ETAR (1-β-D-ribofuranosyl-3-ethynyl-[1,2,4]triazole) demonstrated high potency in vitro against HTNV and ANDV, and in vivo studies using HTNV-challenged suckling mice showed approximately 25 percent protection at doses of just 12.5 and 25 milligrams per kilogram, mirroring ribavirin’s therapeutic outcomes at a fraction of the concentration. Beyond nucleoside analogues, the viral nucleoprotein has emerged as a promising alternative target. Terpene-isoindole conjugates such as compound 18 demonstrated potent anti-HTNV activity with exceptionally low toxicity, and molecular docking suggests they bind to the highly conserved RNA-binding site of the nucleoprotein, competing with viral RNA and effectively arresting intracellular replication. Similarly, morin, a natural flavonoid found in plants like mulberry and perilla, binds directly to the core assembly region of the viral nucleoprotein, sterically hindering RNA encapsidation and disrupting the formation of functional replication complexes. In the absence of licensed antiviral treatments, clinical management remains primarily supportive, focusing on intensive monitoring and treatment of respiratory, cardiac, and renal complications. Achieving early diagnosis remains a primary clinical challenge, as the efficacy of supportive care is substantially reduced if initiated after the onset of severe disease. Host-targeted and anti-inflammatory strategies have recently been explored to mitigate the characteristic vascular leak and cytokine storm, most notably during the 2026 cruise ship outbreak, where the JAK inhibitor baricitinib and the bradykinin B2 receptor antagonist icatibant were administered in a combined therapeutic regimen alongside ribavirin and favipiravir. Passive immunotherapy continues to be a major investigative focus, with convalescent plasma showing potential to reduce mortality and newer candidates such as the quadrivalent human polyclonal antibody SAB-163 and highly potent monoclonal antibodies demonstrating up to 100 percent protection in lethal animal models. Regarding prevention, Hantavax has been used to prevent HFRS in South Korea, and China routinely administers inactivated bivalent vaccines targeting HTNV and SEOV in endemic regions. However, a globally licensed orthohantavirus vaccine does not currently exist, leaving New World strains and HCPS without an approved preventative countermeasure. The review’s authors argue that expanding preclinical testing to include globally neglected variants, combined with modern in silico screening of extensive chemical and natural product libraries, will be essential to develop broad-spectrum hantaviral therapeutics capable of mitigating future epidemic threats. Event
A review published in Frontiers in Cellular and Infection Microbiology by researchers at the University of São Paulo and Institut Pasteur de São Paulo systematically maps the viral life cycle for chemotherapeutic intervention, evaluating entry inhibitors, polymerase blockers, nucleocapsid disruptors, and host-directed strategies while advocating for computational approaches to accelerate discovery.
Scale, impact and risk
Hantaviruses cause two life-threatening clinical syndromes: hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) across Eurasia and hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome (HCPS) in the Americas, with the latter having case fatality rates that can approach or exceed 50 percent. There is still no universally licensed antiviral or vaccine against orthohantavirus infection.
Response, status and uncertainty
The study outlines strategies for targeting different stages of the orthohantavirus infection cycle, including entry inhibitors, polymerase blockers, and host-directed approaches. It highlights the potential of computational screening to identify potent drug candidates, such as G319–0078 and 8012-0652, which showed high-affinity inhibition in the nanomolar range. The research also discusses the use of natural products and favipiravir as entry and replication inhibitors, with favipiravir showing 100 percent survival in lethal ANDV hamster models when treatment was initiated before viremia.
