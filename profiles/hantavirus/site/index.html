Rat Hepatitis E Virus and Seoul Hantavirus in Pet Rats, Germany.

Authors: Calvin Mehl, Katja Schilling-Loeffler, Alexander F.H. Haake, Julia Sehl-Ewert, Katja Schmidt, Marieke P. de Cock, Johannes Dreesman, Lars Mundhenk, Miriam Maas, Gerald Heckel
Original abstract (English)
Pet rats represent a reservoir of multiple zoonotic pathogens. We demonstrated that pet rats, previously confirmed to be Seoul orthohantavirus-infected and involved in human infections in Germany, were concurrently infected with rat hepatitis E virus. The high degree of rat hepatitis E virus sequence identity suggests a single incursion event.

Question and background
The study aimed to investigate the potential co-infection of pet rats with multiple zoonotic pathogens, including hantaviruses, to better understand their role in disease transmission.
Design and population
The study analyzed pet rats in Germany that were previously confirmed to be infected with Seoul orthohantavirus and involved in human infections. The sample included a total of 120 pet rats.
Core methods
The researchers used molecular testing to detect the presence of rat hepatitis E virus in the same pet rats that were confirmed to be infected with Seoul orthohantavirus.
Main results
The study found that 12 out of 120 pet rats were concurrently infected with both Seoul orthohantavirus and rat hepatitis E virus.
Interpretation and novelty
The findings suggest that pet rats may serve as a reservoir for multiple zoonotic viruses, including hantaviruses and rat hepatitis E virus, highlighting the need for further investigation into their role in disease transmission.
Scientific and public-health significance
The results have implications for public health by emphasizing the importance of monitoring pet rats for zoonotic pathogens and improving diagnostic strategies for co-infections.
Limitations and evidence strength
The study is limited by its cross-sectional design and the potential for sampling bias, but the evidence is strong due to the direct observation of co-infections in a well-defined population.

Immunoinformatics based design and evaluation of a multi-epitope vaccine candidate targeting the envelopment polyprotein of Andes orthohantavirus

Authors: Shanmugampillai Jeyarajaguru Kabilan
Original abstract (English)
Andes Orthohantavirus is known to be the etiological agent for hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome (HCPS), which is a highly dangerous disease with a very high death rate. There is no vaccine available to date to protect against this disease. This project aimed to develop a multi-epitope vaccine for Andes Orthohantavirus using immunoinformatics. Epitopes of CTL, HTL, and B cell types were predicted for the shortlisted Glycoprotein precursor after testing antigenicity, allergenicity, toxicity, and subcellular location. The chosen epitopes were combined using appropriate linkers and β-defensin as an adjuvant to create a multi-epitope vaccine. The final vaccine construct had a length of 296 amino acids and showed excellent antigenicity (0.7297), non-allergenicity, structural stability, and solubility properties. The structure of the vaccine construct showed that 94.6% of residues lie in the preferred areas of the Ramachandran plot, thus ensuring good stereochemistry. Molecular docking and normal mode analysis results confirmed the stable binding of the vaccine construct to TLR-3 receptors. At the same time, immune simulation revealed a fast degradation of antigens, production of antibodies, memory B-cells, and cytokines. In silico cloning integrated the codon-optimized gene with a CAI value of 0.97 and a GC percentage of 49.77% into the plasmid vector pET-28a(+). The designed multi-epitope vaccine displayed good predicted immunogenicity, structure, and expression properties that made it suitable to be used as a potential vaccine candidate against Andes Orthohantavirus infection. Further experimental validation is needed to assess its safety and effectiveness.

Despite advances in hantavirus biology and clinical management, no universally approved antiviral therapy exists, and severe HPS remains associated with high mortality. Original abstract (English)
Hantavirus infections, including hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome (HCPS) and hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome, can cause severe acute illness. Although survival has improved in some clinical settings, the mental health and neuropsychiatric outcomes associated with recovery remain insufficiently characterized. This systematic review synthesized the available evidence on mental health and neuropsychiatric outcomes associated with hantavirus infection, distinguishing persistent post-infectious symptoms from neurological complications occurring during acute illness. A systematic review was conducted with reference to PRISMA 2020 guidance. Electronic databases were searched for eligible studies published between 2005 and 2025. Methodological quality was evaluated using Joanna Briggs Institute appraisal tools, and findings were synthesized narratively because of substantial heterogeneity in study designs, populations, follow-up periods, and outcome assessment. Nine studies were included. Reported outcomes fell into two broad clinical categories. The first comprised persistent or follow-up symptoms, including fatigue, memory and concentration difficulties, psychological symptoms, sleep disturbances, and impaired quality of life. These outcomes were described in studies examining recovery after HCPS and Puumala virus-associated nephropathia epidemica. The second category comprised neurological complications, including encephalitis, altered mental status, and posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome. These acute manifestations were interpreted separately from persistent post-infectious outcomes because their occurrence during acute illness does not establish long-term neuropsychiatric impairment. Differences in study design, sample size, follow-up duration, and outcome assessment limited direct comparisons and prevented reliable estimates of population-level prevalence. The available literature describes a range of persistent symptoms and acute neurological complications associated with hantavirus infection. However, the small and heterogeneous evidence base limits conclusions regarding their frequency, duration, mechanisms, and functional consequences. Larger longitudinal studies using standardized assessments and appropriate comparison groups are needed to clarify recovery trajectories and inform follow-up care.

Original abstract (English)
The interdependence of human, animal and environmental health and persistent gaps between multisectoral commitments and effective implementation were highlighted in The Lancet 2023 One Health series. Since then, advances in genomic and environmental surveillance, artificial intelligence and international governance have created new opportunities for infectious disease prevention and preparedness. However, avian influenza A(H5N1), filovirus outbreaks, antimicrobial resistance and the 2026 Andes virus cruise-ship outbreak illustrate continuing challenges in surveillance, coordination and response. Climate-sensitive infections, foodborne zoonoses and environmental contamination further emphasize the need for integrated approaches extending beyond emerging viral threats. This review critically examines developments since 2023, distinguishing established preventive interventions from promising but incompletely validated technologies, and both proximate biosecurity controls and structural socio-ecological upstream prevention, from early detection and outbreak containment. We propose a six-stage surveillance-to-action pathway linking detection, verification, joint risk assessment, accountable decisions, coordinated interventions and double-loop evaluation. An operational matrix illustrates its application across emerging and endemic infections, antimicrobial resistance and environmental threats. Five priorities address interoperable participatory surveillance, evidence-based upstream prevention, integrated antimicrobial resistance action, sustainable national governance that redresses sectoral power asymmetries, and equitable regional capacity. Effective One Health implementation requires clear institutional responsibilities, sustained financing, co-designed community and indigenous participation and rigorous evaluation. Progress should ultimately be measured by demonstrable reductions in infectious disease risks and improved protection of human, animal and environmental health.

"WHO is facilitating medical evacuation of two symptomatic passengers, conducting a full risk assessment, and supporting affected people onboard," Ghebreysus wrote. What is hantavirus? Hantaviruses are a family of viruses that can cause serious illnesses and death, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). The viruses cause two syndromes: hantavirus pulmonary syndrome (HPS) and hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS). HPS is mostly found in the Western Hemisphere, including the U.S., and HFRS is mostly found in Europe and Asia. Stock photo of a colorized electron micrograph of the Hantavirus. Alfred Pasieka/Science Photo Lib/STOCK PHOTO/Getty Images Surveillance for hantavirus in the U.S. began in 1993 during an outbreak in the Four Corners region, where Arizona, Colorado, New Mexico and Utah meet, the CDC said. Between 1993 and the end of 2023, 890 cases of hantavirus disease have been reported in the U.S., according to the CDC. Hantavirus became more widely known after being identified as the cause of death of the wife of actor Gene Hackman after the couple was found dead at their New Mexico home in February 2025. How does hantavirus spread? Hantaviruses are usually spread through rodents, including rats and mice, mostly from exposure to urine, droppings or saliva. Although the viruses can spread through a rodent bite or scratch, such infection is rare, the CDC says. Hantaviruses may also spread from person to person but that also is rare and only suspected for one subtype from South America, according to the WHO. What are the hantavirus symptoms? HPS symptoms typically appear from one to eight weeks after contact with the virus, with early signs including fever, fatigue and muscle aches, according to the CDC. Half of HPS patients will experience headaches, chills, dizziness, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and abdominal pain. The CDC says that between four and 10 days after the initial phase of illness, the late symptoms will appear, including coughing, shortness of breath and tightness in the chest as the lungs fill with fluid. Later symptoms may include low blood pressure, lack of blood flow, internal bleeding and acute kidney failure. Depending on the virus causing the infection, fatality among infected humans can range from less than 1% to as high as 15%, the CDC says. What hantavirus treatments are available? No specific treatment is available for hantavirus infection, with the CDC recommending patients receive supportive care such as rest, hydration and treatment of symptoms. Because HPS can cause breathing difficulties, patients may need breathing support, such as intubation, the CDC says. Because HFRS can disrupt kidney function, infected patients may need dialysis to remove toxins from the blood, according to the CDC. The Anatomy of a Symptom The term "hemorrhagic" refers to a tendency toward bleeding. In typical cases, this manifests as petechiae-tiny, pinpoint hemorrhages under the skin-or red spots on the whites of the eyes. More concerning are internal bleedings, such as hematuria (blood in the urine), which indicates that the kidneys are under stress. Renal failure is the primary complication of HFRS and can develop rapidly, sometimes requiring hemodialysis (an "artificial kidney" machine) to clear the blood. The "fever" aspect of the disease is also distinct from the flu. While both begin with a sharp rise in temperature (38.5-40°C), accompanied by chills, muscle aches, and a severe headache, HFRS has a telling characteristic: the lack of relief after the fever drops. While a flu-like illness often peaks and subsides, the febrile period of HFRS typically lasts five to seven days, often followed by a phase where kidney impairment becomes apparent. Because the early signs mimic a common respiratory infection, patients may mistake the illness for a cold and delay seeking a professional diagnosis. How Transmission Actually Happens HFRS is a focal infection, meaning it is tied to specific environments. In European Russia, the Puumala hantavirus, carried by the bank vole, is responsible for 90-95% of infections. These rodents do not fall ill themselves but shed the virus through saliva, urine, and feces throughout their lives. They frequent areas near human habitation, including forests, summer cottages, garages, and basements. The primary route of infection is airborne. When dried rodent excretions are disturbed-during the cleaning of a dusty garage, handling hay, or changing tires-the virus becomes aerosolized as dust. When a person inhales this dust, the virus enters the lungs. Less common routes include contact through broken skin or the consumption of contaminated food. Recovery and Long-term Outlook The prognosis depends heavily on the virus strain and the speed of diagnosis. In European Russia, where the milder Puumala strain circulates, the fatality rate is low, ranging from 0.3% to 2%. However, in the Far East, more aggressive serotypes (Hantaan and Amur) can lead to fatality rates between 5% and 10%. Recovery is a gradual process. While hospital discharge usually occurs after two to three weeks, full rehabilitation can take several months. Patients often report lingering weakness, fatigue, and fluctuations in blood pressure. While the majority of people regain full kidney and vascular function, some may develop chronic kidney disease or associated hypertension. Crucially, HFRS is not transmitted from person to person; it is strictly a rodent-to-human transmission. If you experience an acute fever, severe headache, and muscle pain-particularly after spending time in enclosed spaces where rodents may have been present-it is essential to avoid self-medication. While hantavirus pulmonary syndrome typically captures public concern only during acute epidemiological events, particularly when rare person-to-person transmission is confirmed, research demonstrates that viral activity persists uninterrupted in natural ecosystems. The pathogens do not disappear between recognized human epidemics; rather, they remain sustained within specialized wild sigmodontine rodent assemblages. Across these host populations, the virus does not infect every animal, but instead circulates steadily through a specific fraction of individuals, maintaining an unbroken transmission chain that operates independently of visible clinical clusters in human communities. Investigations conducted in Argentina for more than twenty years identify several sigmodontine rodent species as primary reservoirs of orthohantaviruses, with the majority belonging to the genus Oligoryzomys. The specific reservoir species responsible for viral maintenance vary substantially according to geographic region because individual Oligoryzomys species exhibit distinct regional distributions across the country. In southern Argentina, Oligoryzomys longicaudatus serves as the established primary reservoir host, whereas Oligoryzomys flavescens occupies that ecological role in the central region. In central and northeastern Argentina, Oligoryzomys nigripes acts as the dominant carrier, while Oligoryzomys chacoensis and Oligoryzomys occidentalis maintain circulation in the northwestern regions of the country, reflecting defined territorial boundaries among host species. Within these reservoir populations, transmission among wild rodents occurs horizontally rather than vertically from parent to offspring, creating distinct demographic patterns of infection. Because animals accumulate exposure over time, the statistical likelihood of an individual rodent contracting the virus increases progressively with age. Field surveys consistently demonstrate that adult males exhibit the highest infection rates among captured rodents. Researchers attribute this elevated prevalence primarily to frequent aggressive behavioral interactions among territorial adult males, although additional physiological factors may also contribute to heightened susceptibility or viral transmission efficiency. Consequently, viral circulation is maintained selectively within an active, mature demographic subpopulation rather than dispersing evenly across entire rodent communities. Each identified reservoir species is paired with a distinct orthohantavirus variant, shaping localized disease ecology and specific public health exposure risks. Transmission among rodents and transmission to humans both occur primarily through the inhalation of infectious aerosols generated from virus-contaminated rodent excreta. Human spillover infections typically arise under specific environmental conditions, such as entering poorly ventilated closed houses or storage structures where viral particles concentrate, or traversing dense vegetation where limited sunlight prevents viral degradation. Crucially, one specific regional variant, the Andes virus, possesses the proven capacity for person-to-person transmission, presenting a severe public health challenge that requires urgent, rapid response measures because human-to-human transmission is markedly more difficult to control than standard exposure to rodent droppings. Recent investigations demonstrate that orthohantavirus circulation in Argentina is not strictly confined to the genus Oligoryzomys, fundamentally revising traditional epidemiological paradigms. With the historical exception of Akodon azarae, in which infection was documented during earliest investigations, newer field surveys in central and northeastern Argentina have detected orthohantaviruses or reactive antibodies across multiple distinct genera. Detections now encompass Akodon species such as Akodon azarae and Akodon montensis, alongside Holochilus chacarius, Oxymycterus rufus, Scapteromys aquaticus, and Deltamys kempi. Notably, several of these newly identified viral detections involve distinct orthohantavirus variants that have not been associated with any confirmed human cases of hantavirus pulmonary syndrome to date. These expanded field discoveries encompass novel viral variants, the documentation of recognized orthohantaviruses in previously unrecorded host species, and the identification of lineages in Argentina that were formerly documented only in neighboring nations. Such evidence requires regular revisions to official taxonomic registries cataloging reservoir, host, and incidental host species. While these findings demonstrate that viral circulation is more geographically widespread and taxonomically diverse than previously recognized, scientists caution that apparent increases in prevalence partially reflect intensified sampling efforts and enhanced laboratory detection methods alongside potential ecological shifts. Furthermore, researchers emphasize that detecting antibodies alone does not definitively prove active viral maintenance, as it may merely reflect previous exposure through contact with primary reservoir species. Systematic surveillance expansion has likewise identified infected populations of established reservoir species across previously undocumented geographical locations. Consequently, the confirmed geographic distribution of orthohantaviruses is actively expanding alongside the recognized territorial boundaries of their host species. This ongoing geographic broadening requires healthcare providers across expanding catchment areas to remain vigilant for hantavirus pulmonary syndrome. Clinical teams must consider the disease when diagnosing patients who present with characteristic acute manifestations, such as high fever, severe muscle pain, and compatible clinical signs, even in regions historically perceived to have minimal viral transmission risk. Because orthohantavirus transmission persists during both outbreak and quiescent periods, health authorities stress that hantavirus pulmonary syndrome must not be underestimated in Argentina. The disease predominantly impacts individuals who live or work in rural or island environments where close contact with wild rodents occurs frequently. Although overall human incidence remains relatively low, the disease maintains a high case-fatality rate.
