Misinformation about the recent hantavirus outbreak on a cruise ship.

Authors: Yi-Wei Tang
Original abstract (English)
The recent Andes virus (ANDV) outbreak associated with the cruise ship MV Hondius generated substantial misinformation on social media. This editorial addresses six prominent myths concerning pandemic potential, airborne transmission through ventilation systems, unproven treatments, risks associated with cruise travel, influenza-like transmissibility, and claims that the outbreak was fabricated. Current evidence indicates that, although ANDV can rarely spread between people following close and prolonged contact, its transmission differs fundamentally from that of highly contagious respiratory viruses. The outbreak highlights the need for scientists, public health authorities, healthcare professionals, and the media to communicate evidence rapidly and transparently. Effective responses must control not only emerging infections but also the accompanying spread of misinformation.

<p>Interpretable Machine Learning for Mortality Risk Stratification in Hantavirus Infection: A Global Study Across Hemorrhagic Fever with Renal Syndrome and Hantavirus Cardiopulmonary Syndrome</p>

Authors: Yetunde O Enigbokan, Akeem Adekunle Abiona, Micheal O Ajinaja
Original abstract (English)
Hantavirus infection remains a rare but potentially fatal zoonosis, and early identification of patients at highest risk of death is essential for timely triage and resource allocation. This study developed an interpretable machine learning (ML) framework for mortality risk stratification using a global hantavirus epidemiology dataset. The dataset included both Hemorrhagic Fever with Renal Syndrome (HFRS) and Hantavirus Cardiopulmonary Syndrome (HCPS) cases. The study conducted a retrospective supervised learning analysis on 10,000 patient records, including demographic, clinical, epidemiological, and treatment variables. The binary outcome was mortality. Preprocessing included identifier removal, missing-value handling, categorical and symptom encoding, feature selection, and class-imbalance correction. Logistic regression, random forest, and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) models were trained and compared on a held-out test set using receiver operating characteristic-area under the curve (ROC-AUC), accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and Brier score. Model interpretation was planned using SHapley Additive exPlanations-based feature attribution. The cohort included 8,938 recovered and 1,062 deceased cases (mortality rate: 10.62%), comprising 6,460 HFRS cases (64.6%) and 3,540 HCPS cases (35.4%). Logistic regression achieved the highest discrimination, with an ROC-AUC of 0.858 and the highest recall for mortality detection (0.741), but modest precision (0.321) and weaker calibration (Brier score 0.119). Gradient boosting showed comparable discrimination (AUC 0.857) with better precision (0.511) and calibration (Brier score 0.076). Random forest performed less well for mortality detection, with markedly low recall (0.085). The study trained and compared interpretable classifiers using 10,000 global hantavirus cases representing both HFRS (64.6%) and HCPS (35.4%) presentations, and reported pooled performance as well as syndrome-stratified results. Severity, syndrome type, viral load category, age, and geographic setting were the most informative predictors. Mortality risk in hantavirus infection can be modeled using routine clinical and epidemiological features, but clinical deployment should prioritize sensitivity, calibration, and transparency over accuracy alone. These findings support the use of interpretable ML as a practical framework for early hantavirus risk stratification and external validation in independent cohorts.

Eleven questions on Hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome management in the ICU

Authors: Nicolás Pavez, Paula Fernández, Marcela Ferrés, Jaime Retamal, Luis Felipe Reyes, Eduardo Kattan, Alejandro Bruhn
Original abstract (English)
Hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome (HCPS) due to Andes virus (ANDV) is a fulminant yet potentially reversible cause of respiratory and circulatory failure requiring timely critical care. This article addresses eleven practical questions on ICU management, drawing on clinical experience and research from the Chilean healthcare system. The recommendations cover early recognition, diagnostic workup, haemodynamic monitoring, cautious fluid and vasoactive support, respiratory management, VA-ECMO referral, daily ICU care, recovery, common pitfalls, and therapies with limited evidence. Because no antiviral or immunomodulatory therapy has consistently improved outcomes, survival relies on early suspicion, physiology-guided supportive care, and rapid escalation to ECMO-capable centres when severe cardiopulmonary failure develops.

Three sentenced in Moscow-based $2B telemedicine fraud scheme targeting U.S. insurers The criminal organization ran ghost telemedicine visits, fraudulent prescriptions and remotely controlled pharmacies across more than 75 locations nationwide. Three members of a Moscow-based criminal organization have been sentenced to federal prison for their roles in a nearly $2 billion health care fraud conspiracy that used sham telemedicine visits, fraudulent prescriptions and remotely operated pharmacies to defraud private insurers, with many patients never receiving the medications billed in their names. Anthony Santamaria received 10 years; co-defendants Hershel Tsikman and Hafizullah Ebady received 120 and 97 months, respectively. Nearly 10% of surgeons left clinical practice within an eight-year period, with attrition highest among mid-career surgeons and in subspecialties including oral and maxillofacial surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, and plastic and reconstructive surgery, according to a new study published in the Journal of the American College of Surgeons. Hantaviruses are a group of viruses that naturally infect rodents, sometimes long‐term without apparent illness, and are occasionally transmitted to humans, according to the World Health Organization. "Although many hantavirus species have been identified worldwide, only a limited number are known to cause human disease," the WHO adds. How do you get hantavirus? Hantavirus is primarily spread by rodents through exposure to their urine, droppings or saliva and less commonly through a scratch or bite, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. "While rare, hantavirus may spread between people and can lead to severe respiratory illness and requires careful patient monitoring, support and response," the WHO notes. On May 6, the WHO said the strain of hantavirus in the latest outbreak has been confirmed through laboratory testing as Andes virus, a strain found in South America that is believed to spread person-to-person. Does hantavirus exist in the United States? While hantavirus cases have been previously reported in the U.S. − especially the four corners region of Utah, Arizona, Colorado and New Mexico − this strain doesn't cause human-to-human transmission, according to infectious disease expert and Texas Christian University professor Dr. Nikhil Bhayani. Is there a vaccine for hantavirus? There is no specific treatment that cures hantavirus diseases, according to the WHO, but early supportive medical care is "key to improve survival." This care includes clinical monitoring and managing any respiratory, cardiac or other complications, the organization says. What is the hantavirus mortality rate? The death rate from hantavirus depends on the specific disease it causes. For example, it can cause hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome, "a severe respiratory illness, with a case fatality rate up to 50%," according to the WHO. For hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome, which is more common with infections in Europe and Asia, death rates range from less than 1% to 15%, depending on the viral strain's severity, the organization adds. Original source text (English)
A small outbreak of a rare hantavirus on a cruise ship earlier this year captured worldwide attention and sparked concerns about a possible new pandemic. But according to a new University of Maryland School of Medicine (UMSOM) commentary published in JAMA Internal Medicine , the outbreak posed little risk to the general public and offers important lessons about how health officials, scientists, and the media should communicate during emerging disease events. The commentary examines an outbreak of Andes virus, a rare hantavirus found in South America, aboard the cruise ship MV Hondius . The outbreak left three people dead and there were 13 infections among passengers and crew. While the event prompted extensive media coverage, the authors argue that the virus's limited human-to-human transmission ability made a larger outbreak unlikely. Seeing a deadly virus spread among passengers on a cruise ship naturally raised concerns because it reminded many people of the early days of COVID-19. But not every dangerous virus has the capacity to become a pandemic. It's important that public health responses and public messaging are guided by evidence rather than fear." Daniel J. Morgan, MD, MS, Professor of Epidemiology & Public Health at UMSOM and lead author of the commentary Hantaviruses are rare diseases that usually spread to humans through contact with rodents or their droppings, urine, or saliva. In the United States, roughly 30 cases are reported annually. Andes virus is unique among hantaviruses because it can spread from person to person through close contact. However, outbreaks have historically remained small and self-limited. In the commentary, the authors describe how public health officials followed established practices aboard the ship by identifying infected individuals, tracing contacts, and monitoring those who may have been exposed. Health authorities, including the World Health Organization, assessed the risk to the global population as low. "The outbreak appears to have been managed appropriately, and the limited transmission seen with Andes virus makes it very different from highly contagious viruses such as SARS-CoV-2 or measles," Dr. Morgan said. "The public deserves clear explanations about what scientists know, what they don't know, and how much risk a situation actually poses." According to the authors, the recent outbreak illustrates the value of strong international public health networks and expert guidance, particularly as human interactions with wildlife, environmental changes, and global travel continue to increase opportunities for emerging infectious diseases. "We live in a world where new infectious disease threats will continue to emerge," Dr. Morgan said. "The key is ensuring that we have responsive public health institutions, independent scientific research, and trusted experts who can help the public understand which threats require their concern, and those that don't." The authors said that maintaining public engagement in infectious disease issues is valuable, but scientific evidence must be communicated carefully and accurately. They emphasize that society needs experts, institutions, and media organizations capable of helping the public distinguish between rare outbreaks with limited pandemic potential and emerging viruses that could pose a more serious global threat. "International public health collaboration remains critical to preventing and managing outbreaks," said co-author Tara Palmore, MD, Professor of Medicine at UMSOM and a faculty member at the School's Institute of Human Virology. "Continued investment in the development of vaccines and therapeutics for emerging infectious diseases must remain a global research priority, while clear, timely communication from public health experts and the media is essential to keep the public informed about infectious disease risks."
