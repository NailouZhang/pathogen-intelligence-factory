A bivalent Lassa virus mRNA vaccine protects guinea pigs from lethal infection with Lassa virus

A bivalent Lassa virus mRNA vaccine protects guinea pigs from lethal infection with Lassa virus

Authors: Mei Hashizume, Takeshi Saito, Ryotaro Nouda, Takashi Matsuzaki, Shintaro Yamada, Gavriella Siman-Tov, Daiki Kobayashi, Christine Click, Kirsten E. Littlefield, Emily Schugardt
Original abstract (English)
Abstract: Lassa virus (LASV), the causative agent of the life-threatening febrile disease, Lassa fever, remains a major public health concern in West Africa, where hundreds of thousands of infections occur annually. However, no licensed vaccine is currently available, highlighting the urgent need for effective preventive measures. We previously demonstrated that an mRNA-lipid nanoparticle (LNP) vaccine expressing LASV glycoprotein precursor (GPC; LASgpc-mRNA-LNP) conferred protection against lethal infection with a recombinant lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus expressing LASV GPC in mice. In this study, we extended our previous work with the aim of evaluating the efficacy of an mRNA-LNP vaccine expressing LASV nucleoprotein (NP; LASnp-mRNA-LNP) and a bivalent vaccine combining LASgpc-mRNA-LNP and LASnp-mRNA-LNP. Using a lethal CBA/N mouse model of infection with ML29, an LASV live-attenuated vaccine candidate encoding LASV GPC and NP, we found that LASgpc-mRNA-LNP and LASnp-mRNA-LNP each conferred protection in the absence of detectable neutralizing antibody responses. We identified LASV GPC- and NP-derived CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell epitope peptides in CBA/N mice and demonstrated that vaccination induced robust antigen-specific T-cell responses. Importantly, the bivalent vaccine provided complete protection against lethal challenge with doses at which monovalent vaccines failed to protect all mice, suggesting a potential dose-sparing benefit of the bivalent formulation. Furthermore, the bivalent vaccine fully protected guinea pigs against lethal challenge with authentic LASV. Together, these findings establish LASV NP, in addition to GPC, as a protective antigen in the mRNA vaccine platform and provide a rationale for developing bivalent mRNA-LNP vaccines to improve protection against Lassa fever. A transgenic Syrian hamster model for New World hemorrhagic fever mammarenavirus infection

Authors: Samantha R. Wasson, Yanan Liu, Minghao Li, Kevin W. Bailey, Jonna B. Westover, Julio Landinez-Aponte, Arnaud J. Van Wettere, Venkatraman Siddharthan, Christopher J. Davies, Tracy R. Daniels‐Wells
Original abstract (English)
Mammarenaviruses are endemic in rodent populations worldwide, and their zoonotic transmission can lead to a severe, life-threatening hemorrhagic fever (HF) syndrome. In the Americas, five New World mammarenaviruses (NWMs) are known to cause HF. As vaccines and antiviral therapy options are limited, these viruses pose a significant public health risk and a threat to national security. Research into NWM pathogenesis and the development of effective countermeasures is restricted by the shortage of small-animal models that faithfully reproduce human disease. The pathogenic NWMs use human transferrin receptor 1 (hTfR1) as the receptor for cellular entry. Here, we describe the development of an hTfR1 knock-in golden Syrian hamster line and its susceptibility to Junín virus (JUNV) infection. This transgenic hamster infection model supports TfR1-mediated entry as an essential step in JUNV pathogenesis and provides a novel small-animal model to advance the development of promising host receptor-directed therapies and direct-acting antivirals to improve outcomes in patients with severe disease caused by pathogenic NWMs.

Question and background
This study aimed to assess the influence of malaria co-infection on Lassa fever (LF) case detection in health facilities in Ondo State, Nigeria, where both diseases are endemic.
Design and population
A convergent mixed-methods design was used, combining retrospective review of 463 LF and malaria records from 2021 to 2023 across 10 purposively selected facilities in five high-burden LGAs, alongside nine key-informant interviews.
Core methods
Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and logistic regression, while qualitative findings were analyzed thematically and integrated through a joint display.
Main results
Of the 463 records, 297 (64.1%) had positive results for both Lassa fever and malaria. Malaria positivity was associated with missed LF suspicion (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 5.30, 95% CI 2.13–13.19; p < 0.001).
Interpretation and novelty
The study suggests that malaria positivity may mask LF rather than establish an independent cause of mortality, highlighting the need for parallel assessment in co-infection scenarios.
Scientific and public-health significance
These findings inform the development of diagnostic algorithms and surveillance strategies to improve LF detection in areas where malaria is prevalent.
Limitations and evidence strength
The study is limited by its reliance on retrospective records and interviews, which may introduce recall bias. The evidence is moderate in strength due to these limitations.

Assessment of Edo People Attitude Towards Lassa Fever Diseases Prevention Campaign Programme

Original abstract (English)
This study examines the attitudes of Edo people in Owan West Local Government Area (LGA) towards Lassa fever prevention campaigns, particularly those broadcast by KU 92.7 FM. Guided by Behaviour Theory and the Health Belief Model, the research explores how awareness and cultural beliefs influence health behaviours. Using a survey method, 398 respondents were selected through stratified and random sampling from a projected population of 112,503. Findings reveal a high level of awareness of Lassa fever, largely due to efforts by health organizations such as the NCDC, Edo State Ministry of Health, and Irrua Specialist Hospital. However, awareness does not consistently lead to preventive action, highlighting gaps in behavioural change. Trust in health professionals was found to enhance campaign effectiveness, though some skepticism persists due to misinformation and cultural beliefs. Radio emerged as a vital communication tool in rural areas, while urban populations relied more on television and social media. Cultural practices, including reliance on herbal remedies and spiritual interpretations of illness, continue to hinder adoption of preventive measures in rural communities. The study concludes that while awareness is widespread, behavioural change remains uneven. It recommends continuous public health messaging and the inclusion of traditional rulers, religious leaders, and community elders in campaign design to improve acceptance and effectiveness. Tailoring communication strategies to local contexts is essential for fostering sustained health behaviour change.

The study, published open access in Discover Viruses, reports no laboratory or clinical testing; every finding is a prediction generated by bioinformatics servers, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation and immune modelling. The authors, Akeem Babatunde Sikiru, Ibrahim Shuaibu Harande and Nurulfiza Bint Mat Isa, describe the construct as a computationally validated candidate that now requires experimental validation before any claims about real-world performance can be made. The rationale for the work rests on the epidemiology of Lassa fever. The disease is endemic in Nigeria, Sierra Leone, Liberia, Guinea and Benin, with epidemiological estimates suggesting between 100,000 and 300,000 infections and approximately 5,000 deaths each year, figures the authors note are widely considered underestimates because of limited surveillance in rural, resource-constrained settings. Around 80 percent of infections are estimated to be asymptomatic or mild, but the remaining cases can progress to severe multisystem disease. The case-fatality rate among hospitalised patients is frequently cited at around 15 percent and can exceed that during major outbreaks. In pregnancy, particularly the third trimester, infection is associated with maternal mortality above 30 percent and foetal or neonatal loss in more than 80 percent of cases. No licensed vaccine exists against Lassa virus, and the authors argue that traditional development is difficult for a Biosafety Level 4 pathogen, since working with live virus requires expensive high-containment facilities. Candidate vaccines are in development, including rVSVΔG-LASV-GPC, the DNA vaccine INO-4500 and the inactivated rabies virus platform LASSARAB, but the pipeline still lacks a licensed product. The World Health Organization has designated Lassa fever a priority pathogen in its Research and Development Blueprint. The study targeted the viral L protein, the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase encoded by the L segment, selected for its high expression during replication, its role as a source of T-cell epitopes in arenaviruses and its higher conservation across strains compared with surface glycoproteins. The design workflow began with the complete genomic sequence of the Lassa virus L segment from the Josiah strain, retrieved from GenBank under accession number NC_004297.1. The L protein spans 2,218 amino acids. Linear B-cell epitopes were predicted with BepiPred 2.0, discontinuous epitopes with ElliPro, cytotoxic T-lymphocyte epitopes with NetMHCpan 4.1 against 27 common HLA class I alleles, and helper T-lymphocyte epitopes with NetMHCIIpan 4.0 against 27 HLA class II alleles. Predicted epitopes then passed through sequential filtering for antigenicity with VaxiJen 2.0, immunogenicity with the IEDB Class I predictor, allergenicity with AllerTop 2.0, toxicity with ToxinPred, conservation across 50 LASV strains using Clustal Omega, and human homology screening with BLASTp against the human proteome. Selected epitopes were assembled into a chimeric construct using defined linkers: an EAAAK rigid linker connecting the N-terminal adjuvant, the 50S ribosomal protein L7/L12 from Mycobacterium tuberculosis, GPGPG flexible linkers between helper T-cell epitopes, AAY linkers between cytotoxic T-cell epitopes and KK linkers between B-cell epitopes. A 6×His tag was added at the C-terminus for purification, and the final sequence was checked for internal stop codons and frameshifts. Physicochemical characterisation with ExPASy ProtParam yielded a molecular weight of 24.38 kilodaltons, a theoretical isoelectric point of 11.82 indicating a strongly basic protein, and a grand average of hydropathicity of −1.70, signifying a highly hydrophilic character that the authors consider favourable for solubility. The same analyses flagged significant stability concerns. The instability index of 72.57 classifies the protein as unstable, and the low aliphatic index of 18.10 suggests it is likely to be thermolabile, which the authors acknowledge would complicate cold-chain storage in endemic regions. They frame this as an engineering problem addressable through stabilising excipients, lyophilisation or site-directed mutagenesis, rather than a fatal flaw. On safety-related predictions, the construct scored 0.385 for antigenicity with VaxiJen and was predicted to be non-allergenic, an assessment the authors base on its lack of cysteine residues and glycine content of 18.18 percent, below the 20 percent threshold sometimes associated with allergenicity. Solubility on overexpression in Escherichia coli was predicted with the Protein-Sol server. To probe whether the construct could engage an innate immune receptor, the team docked the vaccine model against the human TLR4/MD2 complex using ClusPro 2.0 after preparing structures in UCSF Chimera. The docking analysis identified hotspot residues on the vaccine, including R15, R10 and R18, contributing electrostatic and hydrogen-bond interactions with TLR4, with the vaccine chain contributing approximately 9.5 kilocalories to electrostatic stability. A 100-nanosecond molecular dynamics simulation in GROMACS with the CHARMM36m force field showed the complex reaching equilibrium after roughly 30 nanoseconds, with backbone RMSD stabilising around 2.56 angstroms, a radius of gyration stabilising at 4.13 nanometres, an average of approximately 150 hydrogen bonds over the final 60 nanoseconds, and a persistent salt bridge between TLR4-Lys42 and Vaccine-Asp67 at a distance below 1.0 nanometre. These are simulated stability metrics, not measurements of a physical molecule. Immune simulation with the C-IMMSIM server, run for 1,050 steps over a simulated 350 days with a three-dose prime-boost schedule at weeks 0, 4 and 8, predicted a primary IgM response followed by class-switched IgG, activation and clonal expansion of both CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell populations, a Th1-polarised cytokine profile dominated by IFN-γ and IL-2, and a lasting memory T-cell population. Population coverage analysis with the IEDB tool predicted combined coverage of 95.30 percent for the West African population, spanning Benin, Ghana, Guinea, Liberia, Mali, Nigeria and Sierra Leone, compared with 92.10 percent globally. Gene Ontology enrichment analysis with g:Profiler additionally associated the construct with nucleic acid binding and transcription factor activity terms, leading the authors to hypothesise, without experimental support, that the strongly basic construct might possess intrinsic self-adjuvanting properties. The authors are explicit that the study’s central limitation is its computational nature: in silico predictions, however sophisticated, remain hypotheses requiring rigorous experimental validation. The construct’s predicted instability, its reliance on predicted epitope immunogenicity and the absence of any antibody binding, T-cell activation or protection data mean the candidate sits at the earliest stage of the development pathway. What the study delivers is a rationally designed sequence, a documented filtering pipeline and a set of testable predictions, including the Th1-oriented immune profile and the self-adjuvancy hypothesis, that the authors argue warrant advancement into preclinical evaluation and in vivo testing as part of the broader effort to develop a Lassa fever vaccine. Review maps interconnected drivers of Lassa fever in Nigeria - Bioengineer.org

News Brief
A systematic review published on October 26, 2026, by Bioengineer.org maps the interconnected drivers of Lassa fever in Nigeria. The review, which analyzed 38 eligible studies from January 2000 through March 2025, highlights human behaviors, healthcare conditions, reservoir rodents (particularly Mastomys natalensis), and environmental changes as key factors contributing to persistent transmission. Nigeria accounts for approximately 70% of the global Lassa fever burden, with the disease being endemic in multiple states. The review identifies perennial hotspots in Edo, Ondo, Ebonyi, and Plateau states, along with seasonal peaks at the end of the rainy season. It reports thousands of deaths during annual outbreaks, underscoring the severity of the disease. The findings suggest that Lassa fever risk is shaped by multiple determinants across human, animal, and environmental systems, and that controlling the disease requires an integrated One Health approach. The authors recommend strengthened infection prevention and control, improved diagnostics, community-based rodent-proofing, and coordinated surveillance across humans, animals, and environments. However, the review does not test these measures in clinical trials or demonstrate their effectiveness in reducing deaths. Its contribution is to consolidate available evidence and provide a rationale for comprehensive prevention strategies.

Among human risk factors, the studies most consistently reported open food storage, occupational exposure, poor household hygiene and nosocomial transmission in facilities without adequate infection prevention and control. The latter finding points to the role of health-care settings in allowing person-to-person transmission under deficient containment conditions. Animal evidence focused on the peri-domestic ecology of Mastomys natalensis, the rodent implicated as a reservoir of Lassa virus, with rodent density strongly correlated with human exposure. Environmental conditions—including poor housing, inadequate waste management, deforestation, climate variability and peri-urban expansion—were identified as contextual amplifiers of risk rather than isolated causes. The review’s evidence therefore supports an interconnected model in which human practices, reservoir populations and environmental change jointly shape transmission. The review also describes thousands of deaths during annual outbreaks, indicating that transmission has persisted despite the disease’s established endemicity. Because the evidence came from heterogeneous studies conducted across different locations and domains, the synthesis does not quantify a single population-wide effect or establish that every reported factor carries the same degree of importance. Instead, it shows that Lassa fever risk is shaped by several simultaneously operating determinants across human, animal and environmental systems. The authors conclude that controlling Lassa fever requires an integrated One Health approach rather than reliance on any one intervention. They call for strengthened infection prevention and control, improved diagnostics, community-based rodent-proofing and sanitation programmes, coordinated surveillance across humans, animals and environments, culturally tailored risk communication and sustained domestic financing. The review does not test these measures in a clinical trial, nor does it demonstrate that the combined package will reduce deaths. Its contribution is to consolidate the available empirical evidence and characterize persistent endemicity as the outcome of converging determinants, providing a rationale for coordinated surveillance and prevention across Nigeria.
