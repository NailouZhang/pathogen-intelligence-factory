Lassa fever and Argentine hemorrhagic fever treatment in guinea pigs using broad-spectrum cap-dependent endonuclease inhibitors

Authors: Junki Maruyama, Satoshi Taniguchi, Takeshi Saito, Shintaro Yamada, Rachel A. Reyna, Ruchi Paroha, Daiki Kobayashi, Gavriella Siman-Tov, Christine Click, Kirsten Littlefield
Original abstract (English)
ABSTRACT The class Bunyaviricetes encompasses several highly pathogenic viruses that cause lethal hemorrhagic fevers. Due to their limited prevention and treatment options and high pathogenicity, these viruses require handling in biosafety level-4 facilities. Within the bunyaviruses, arenaviruses are particularly notable for their pathogenicity and ability to cause severe hemorrhagic disease in humans. The cap-dependent endonuclease (CEN) is a unique and crucial enzyme involved in the replication cycle of these viruses. As humans do not possess a similar enzyme, CEN represents an ideal target for antiviral drug development with reduced risk of side effects. Recently, we identified a promising CEN inhibitor (CENi) demonstrating potent inhibition of virus replication. In this manuscript, we demonstrate the successful therapeutic efficacy of CENis against Lassa fever and Argentine hemorrhagic fever virus infections in guinea pig models of lethal hemorrhagic fever. In addition, we identified several CENis with antiviral activity against other highly pathogenic arenaviruses. These findings further support the potential of CENis as therapeutic agents for arenavirus infections that cause severe and often lethal hemorrhagic fever. Collectively, our results suggest that CENis are promising candidates for pan-arenavirus therapy and may also have broader utility against other CEN-containing viruses for which no approved antiviral treatments currently exist. IMPORTANCE Arenaviruses, such as Lassa virus and Junin virus, cause severe hemorrhagic fevers in humans and are associated with high mortality rates and limited treatment options. Because of their pathogenicity and potential for outbreaks, these viruses represent an important global health threat. The viral cap-dependent endonuclease (CEN) plays a critical role in arenavirus replication by enabling the cap-snatching process required for viral mRNA synthesis. Notably, mammalian cells lack a comparable enzyme, making CEN an attractive target for antiviral drug development. In this study, we demonstrate that inhibitors targeting the arenavirus CEN effectively suppress viral replication and provide therapeutic protection in animal models of lethal Lassa virus and Junin virus infections. We also identify compounds with antiviral activity against multiple highly pathogenic arenaviruses. These findings establish CEN inhibition as a promising strategy for the development of broad-spectrum antivirals against arenaviruses that cause life-threatening hemorrhagic fevers.

Biophysical Characterization of K33A Mutation to Stable Signal Peptide Reveals Insights into Lassa Virus Fusion Mechanism

Authors: Shane D. Collins, Idowu Moses Falomo, Lucian Q. Cook, Lia R. Goldstein, Jinwoo Lee
Original abstract (English)
Abstract Lassa virus (LASV), a member of the arenavirus family, utilizes low-pH-dependent membrane fusion to enter host cells and elicit infection. LASV is a class I fusion virus, and thus its fusion protein, GP2, is functionally similar to other class I fusion proteins in that it follows the six-helix bundle fusion mechanism. LASV fusion is atypical in that it requires another viral protein, stable signal peptide (SSP), that does not undergo fusion itself but is required for the fusion process. However, the exact function of SSP in fusion is unknown. We aimed to understand the role of SSP in the fusion process by examining the structural changes that occur within SSP during the fusion process. We found that SSP undergoes two distinct structural changes; the first at pH 6 that is not associated with fusion, the second at below pH 5 that is associated with fusion activity. Furthermore, we found a mutation to a key residue of SSP, K33A, does not undergo these two structural changes; in fact, K33A is seen to undergo a different conformational change at pH 6. We believe this distinct conformation change is due to the loss of favorable interactions between the lysine at the end of the transmembrane helix of SSP. This disruption of favorable interaction then causes downstream effects to the SSP structure in the event of lowering pH. The difference in conformational changes between K33A and wildtype (WT) SSP is likely why K33A abrogates the fusion activity of GP2. We conclude that the conformational changes undergone by SSP are crucial for LASV fusion activity.

Scope and question
Lassa fever (LF), an acute viral haemorrhagic disease caused by Lassa virus (LASV), remains a significant public health concern in West Africa.
Evidence base and method
This review synthesizes available evidence on the occupational risk of Lassa virus exposure among abattoir workers and examines the broader public health implications, with emphasis on strengthening surveillance, biosafety, and integrated One Health interventions within abattoir settings.
Consensus and conclusions
Abattoirs, characterized by abundant organic waste and inadequate sanitation, offer ideal conditions for rodents like Mastomys natalensis to thrive and contaminate surfaces and meat products. Abattoir workers face a severe, under-recognized occupational health hazard due to routine and unprotected exposure to animal tissues, blood, and bioaerosols. Infected workers, serving as an entry point, can inadvertently act as vectors, introducing the virus into households and communities, fueling outbreaks.

This is a narrative review that consolidates existing findings and literature on the epidemiology of Lassa fever, its virology and transmission pathways, the link between abattoirs and zoonotic disease exposure, rodent activity and biosecurity in abattoirs, and public health implications.

The current Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo is a stark reminder of how serious these threats can become when they spread undetected and gain ground. CDC-Trained Disease Detectives on the Frontlines At the center of many of these responses are CDC-trained field epidemiologists on the frontlines when dangerous outbreaks strike. These disease detectives are part of a global corps trained through CDC's landmark Field Epidemiology Training Program (FETP). More than 25,000 disease detectives have been trained through CDC-supported FETPs since 1980 in more than 100 countries around the world, where they are positioned as a first line of defense against deadly disease threats. They are often the first on the ground when outbreaks happen—identifying threats, investigating cases in real time, tracking how diseases spread, identifying those at risk, and helping stop outbreaks before they escalate. Working at the very heart of outbreak response, these experts help stop dangerous outbreaks in their tracks and help keep them from ever reaching U.S. communities. How CDC Helped Stop a Deadly Virus Before It Spread View LargerDownload In 2025, CDC's global health security experts and Senegalese health officials rapidly contained a deadly outbreak of Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever, stopping it at the source before it could cross borders. In January 2025, a deadly virus surfaced in Senegal's Kaffrine Region. Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever—a tick-borne disease that can also spread through infected animals and between humans—had sickened five people and killed one. With a high fatality rate and multiple pathways for transmission, even a small cluster can quickly become something much larger. Stopping the virus meant understanding not only who had become sick, but where they had been, who they had come into contact with, and whether the disease was also circulating among animals. CDC's Rapid Response Within hours, CDC's global health experts and Senegalese health officials sprang into action, activating Senegal's Emergency Operations Center and launching a coordinated response. Among those responding were CDC-trained disease detectives, working alongside veterinarians, doctors, and anthropologists to trace how the virus was moving between people, animals, and affected communities. Together, the teams identified and followed 51 potential contacts and tested more than 70 human and animal samples. They treated livestock, disinfected contaminated areas, and worked across human and animal health to identify possible exposures and interrupt transmission. It was the kind of response CDC's Field Epidemiology Training Program is designed to make possible: trained disease detectives already on the ground, able to move quickly when a dangerous threat emerges, and work across disciplines to stop it. Global Action. Local Impact. Thanks to these efforts, the outbreak was contained in just two weeks—with only five cases and no wider spread. The virus never moved beyond the affected area—protecting communities and livelihoods in Senegal and reducing the risk that a deadly outbreak could spread across borders cause significant human and economic harm and ultimately threaten Americans at home. Stopping Lassa Fever in Its Tracks View LargerDownload In 2024, when an American man returned to Iowa from Liberia unknowingly infected with Lassa fever, CDC experts acted fast to safeguard Americans from the threat, containing it before it could spread. In October 2024, an American man returned to Iowa after a trip to Liberia. Unknowingly, he carried Lassa fever, a potentially deadly virus found in parts of West Africa. In the days after his arrival, he developed symptoms and visited three healthcare facilities, using two ambulances and emergency services along the way. Despite efforts to treat him, tragically, he died. The case highlighted just how quickly a health threat overseas can become a threat here at home—and the critical role CDC's global health expertise plays in protecting communities around the world and in the United States. CDC's Rapid Response CDC's ability to respond in Iowa drew on 16 years of work in Liberia, where Lassa fever remains a persistent challenge. Working in partnership, CDC's global health teams and subject matter experts in Atlanta were able to provide Iowa health officials with the knowledge and guidance needed to diagnose the disease and prevent further spread. Over the years, CDC teams in Liberia have assessed health facilities, trained local staff, and worked with Liberia's Ministry of Health and the National Public Health Institute of Liberia to strengthen disease detection and response. CDC experts have conducted active case searches, strengthened case tracking and genetic sequencing, and trained frontline health workers to recognize and respond to diseases like Lassa fever. Those tools weren't just helping Liberia. When Lassa fever surfaced in Iowa, CDC's domestic and international teams mobilized quickly to support the state—providing laboratory confirmation, supporting the epidemiologic investigation and contact tracing, offering infection-prevention guidance, and helping protect healthcare workers who had been exposed. Global Action. Local Impact. Thanks to this rapid response, there was no additional spread in the United States beyond the single imported case. By strengthening the systems and expertise needed to confront diseases like Lassa fever where they occur, CDC is better prepared when those same threats reach U.S. shores—helping protect communities overseas and Americans here at home. Containing Ethiopia's First Marburg Outbreak Before It Spread View LargerDownload In 2025, global health experts, including CDC-trained disease detectives partnered with Ethiopian health officials to detect and contain Ethiopia's very first Marburg virus outbreak, before it could reach the United States. In November 2025, Ethiopia confronted a troubling historic episode: its first confirmed outbreak of Marburg virus. The deadly hemorrhagic fever quickly appeared across communities in the South Ethiopia and Sidama regions. By early December, 14 cases had been confirmed and nine people had died. With no previous Marburg outbreak experience to draw from, the challenge was immediate: find those at risk, interrupt transmission, and keep the virus from reaching more communities—or crossing Ethiopia's borders. CDC's Rapid Response From the earliest days of the outbreak, CDC experts worked alongside Ethiopia's Ministry of Health. At the center of that effort were 17 CDC-trained Ethiopian field epidemiologists who deployed into affected communities to investigate cases, trace contacts, and track the virus in real time. CDC experts and Ethiopian field epidemiologists strengthened laboratory testing and surveillance and reinforced infection prevention and control in health facilities. As new information came in, these teams used the data to guide the response—helping health officials see where the greatest risks remained and where action was needed most. CDC experts also looked beyond the immediate outbreak. They helped strengthen border health measures including in Addis Ababa international airport, a major hub with multiple flights to the United States. They advised on bat ecology to reduce the risk of future transmission from animals to people, and also worked with neighboring countries to assess their readiness should the virus spread farther. Global Action. Local Impact. Within one month, transmission was interrupted, and six weeks later, the outbreak was fully contained—with just 14 confirmed cases. Ultimately, the virus remained confined to two southern regions, never reached the capital, Addis Ababa, and never crossed an international border. As such, CDC's experts helped stop Ethiopia's first Marburg outbreak in its tracks before it could spread farther, threaten neighboring countries, or become a threat to the United States. Stopping an Unexplained Neurological Illness Outbreak View LargerDownload In 2023, CDC's global health security experts and country partners identified the source of a mystery neurological illness, Guillain-Barré syndrome, in Guatemala and quickly contained the spread before it could cross borders. In late 2023, a cluster of severe neurological illness began appearing along Guatemala's Pan-American Highway. Patients were rapidly developing Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS), a rare neurological disorder that can cause progressive muscle weakness and paralysis, potentially leading to respiratory failure. With cases appearing along one of Central America's major travel and trade corridors linking the region to the U.S., finding the answer quickly mattered well beyond the affected communities. CDC's Rapid Response CDC country office experts worked alongside Field Epidemiology Training Program graduates, Guatemala's Ministries of Health and Agriculture, and regional partners to find the source. Two CDC Epidemic Intelligence Service officers were deployed to strengthen the investigation and help determine what the cases had in common. The laboratory offered another critical clue. Using advanced genomic sequencing to analyze patient and environmental samples, investigators detected Campylobacter, a common foodborne bacterium. Combined with epidemiologic findings linking illness to uncooked or poorly cooked foods, the evidence pointed to this foodborne bacterial contamination as the likely trigger. Together, the field investigation and laboratory evidence gave health officials what they needed to understand the outbreak—and act. Global Action. Local Impact. Thanks to the rapid investigation, within 30 days, health officials had identified the likely cause, and within 90 days, the outbreak was contained. In all, 87 cases were identified, but the outbreak remained localized with no cross-border spread. By finding the likely trigger and stopping the outbreak quickly, CDC and country partners protected communities while reducing the risk that an unexplained health threat along a major travel and trade corridor could spread farther—including toward the United States. When someone died without a clear diagnosis, an important warning could disappear from the surveillance system even while the disease continued circulating in the community. Using this novel approach, investigators uncovered 11 Lassa fever infections and one dengue infection that had previously gone unrecognized. The Nigeria Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (NCDC) has issued an advisory urging Nigerians to take preventive measures against Lassa fever ahead of its peak season, which typically runs from November to April. The advisory emphasizes the importance of storing food in covered containers, maintaining cleanliness, and blocking rodent entry points to reduce the risk of transmission. This recommendation follows a national health security press briefing held on September 9, 2026, in Abuja, where the director-general, Dr Jide Idris, highlighted the need for preparedness before the health system faces increased pressure. The 2026 After-Action Review identified several challenges, including delayed care-seeking, prolonged self-treatment for suspected malaria, and persistent rodent exposure, which contribute to the spread of the disease. The NCDC's Accelerated Readiness Plan focuses on early detection, rapid investigation, and infection prevention, with additional resources allocated to high-risk areas. The plan also incorporates a 'Turn a Community Orange' approach to drive preventive behavior and early reporting, under a One Health framework that involves collaboration across health, environment, agriculture, livestock, and local government sectors. Idris urged states to fund preparedness efforts before cases rise, emphasizing the gap between theoretical capacity and functional readiness in healthcare systems.

What the 2026 review found Delayed care-seeking and prolonged self-treatment for suspected malaria Continued infections among healthcare workers, especially under pressure Poor sanitation, unsafe food storage and persistent rodent exposure Delayed release of state resources and limited state ownership of response A gap between capacity on paper and capacity that actually functions Idris illustrated the last gap bluntly, saying in remarks reported by The Cable that owning a dialysis machine does not mean dialysis is available without trained personnel, consumables and maintenance. An After-Action Review of the 2026 response, conducted with states and partners, produced the findings. The Accelerated Readiness Plan The NCDC's plan concentrates on early detection and reporting, rapid investigation and laboratory testing, infection prevention and control, availability of medicines and protective equipment, and ready treatment centres, with extra resources for areas of highest documented burden. In selected high-risk communities the agency will intensify its Turn a Community Orange approach to drive preventive behaviour and early reporting, under a One Health framework spanning health, environment, agriculture, livestock and local government. Idris pressed states to fund preparedness before cases rise: "Preparedness is most effective when we act before the pressure on our health system rises," he said, urging functional surveillance, clear laboratory referral pathways and stocked treatment facilities. For healthcare workers, the agency's message is shorter: think Lassa, protect, report, refer. Household actions before the peak The NCDC advises storing rice, garri, beans and maize in covered containers, never drying food on bare ground, reducing household clutter, using safe rodent-control measures and avoiding bare-handed contact with rodents or their fluids. Anyone with fever that persists after malaria treatment should seek medical care promptly rather than continue self-medicating. Representatives of the environment, livestock and agriculture ministries backed the briefing with commitments on sanitation, bush-meat safety and farmer awareness on grain storage.
