Strengthening diagnostic capacity for viral hemorrhagic fevers in Forest Guinea: advances in Lassa fever case detection.

Authors: Fara Raymond Koundouno, Youssouf Sidibe, Saa Lucien Millimono, Kékoura Ifono, Julia Hinzmann, Hugo Soubrier, Karifa Kourouma, Tamba Elie Millimouno, Fernand M'Bemba Tolno, Faya Moriba Kamano
Original abstract (English)
Viral hemorrhagic fevers (VHFs) remain a major public health threat in resource-limited settings. The 2014-2016 Ebola epidemic exposed critical gaps in laboratory preparedness and diagnostic capacity in most-at-risk countries, including Guinea. In order to implement sustainable in-country laboratory capacity for early detection and rapid response to VHF outbreaks, a decentralized diagnostics laboratory strengthening program started in the forested region of Guinea in Guéckédou (2017) and N'Zérékoré (2021). Between 2017 and 2024, a total of 4683 samples from suspected VHF cases were tested across the two laboratories. Of those, 47 (1%) were identified as positive for VHFs by molecular testing, including the 2021 Ebola virus disease resurgence and Marburg virus disease emergence samples, for which the laboratories supported rapid outbreak detection and response. Notably, 30 Lassa fever cases were laboratory-confirmed with a case fatality rate of 67.9%. Of those, 50.0% and 27.6% of the cases originating from the Guéckédou and N'Zérékoré prefecture, respectively. Prior to this program, Lassa fever detection in Guinea remained anecdotic. One case of yellow fever and one of dengue were also identified by molecular assays. The establishment of locally embedded laboratory capacity enhanced VHFs preparedness, surveillance, and response in Guinea, supporting Lassa fever epidemiology and health-system resilience.

Lassa Virus—A Persistent and Evolving Threat

Authors: Meng Hu, Emily K. Mantlo, Cheng Huang
Original abstract (English)
Lassa virus (LASV), the most important human pathogen within the Arenaviridae family, causes Lassa fever (LF), an acute hemorrhagic fever disease endemic in West Africa with high case fatality rates, particularly in pregnant women. Currently, there are no licensed vaccines or antivirals approved for clinical use against LASV infection. Despite its significant impact on public health, important knowledge gaps remain regarding LASV disease burden, transmission dynamics, pathogenesis, and virus−host interactions, impeding effective disease control and development of therapeutic interventions. The emergence of new lineages and the remarkable genetic diversity of LASV poses substantial challenges to accurate and prompt diagnosis, epidemiological surveillance, and the development of broad-spectrum medical countermeasures. Here, we synthesize current knowledge on the disease burden of LF, recent outbreaks, the geographic distribution and genetic diversity of LASV, infection and transmission in natural reservoirs and other susceptible hosts, emerging evidence suggesting possible sexual transmission, viral pathogenesis, host immune responses, diagnostic advances and challenges, and ongoing preclinical and clinical efforts to develop effective vaccines and antivirals.

Safety first, science second: Healthcare workers' conditional trust and willingness to participate in Lassa fever vaccine trials in Nigeria

Authors: Zubairu Iliyasu, Muhammad Sani Abubakar, Bilkisu Z. Iliyasu, Nafisa S. Nass, Aliyu T. Mande, Iliyasu Z, Abubakar MS, Iliyasu BZ, Nass NS, Mande AT.
Original abstract (English)
<h4>Background</h4>Lassa fever poses a persistent occupational risk to healthcare workers (HCWs) in Nigeria. While HCWs are pivotal to vaccine trial success, their decision-making in settings shaped by historical research controversies remains poorly understood. This study explored the conditions under which HCWs at Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital (AKTH), northern Nigeria, considered participation in Lassa fever vaccine trials acceptable.<h4>Methods</h4>We conducted a descriptive qualitative study using semi-structured in-depth interviews with 20 physicians, nurses, and midwives at AKTH between September and October 2025. Participants were recruited through purposive and snowball sampling. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed using reflexive thematic analysis. Reporting followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).<h4>Results</h4>HCWs did not frame participation as a simple decision to accept or refuse vaccine trials. Instead, they described a pattern of cautious conditionality, whereby willingness depended on explicit safety, ethical, and institutional conditions being met. Decisions were shaped by perceptions of occupational risk, scientific uncertainty, professional responsibility, institutional trust, and the enduring influence of the 1996 Pfizer meningitis trial in Kano. Transparent safety information, robust informed consent, credible ethical oversight, fair compensation for research-related harm, and institutional accountability emerged as essential conditions for participation.<h4>Conclusions</h4>Within the distinctive historical and institutional context of AKTH, HCWs approached Lassa fever vaccine trials as ethically discerning evaluators rather than passive research participants. These findings highlight actionable features for the ethical design and implementation of Lassa fever vaccine trials in endemic settings.

Question and background
The study aimed to understand the conditions under which healthcare workers (HCWs) in Nigeria considered participation in Lassa fever vaccine trials acceptable, given the persistent occupational risk posed by Lassa fever and the historical context of research controversies in the region.
Design and population
A qualitative study was conducted using semi-structured in-depth interviews with 20 HCWs at Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital (AKTH) in northern Nigeria, recruited through purposive and snowball sampling, between September and October 2025.
Core methods
Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed using reflexive thematic analysis, with reporting following the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).
Main results
HCWs described a pattern of cautious conditionality, where willingness to participate in vaccine trials depended on explicit safety, ethical, and institutional conditions being met, including transparent safety information, robust informed consent, credible ethical oversight, fair compensation for research-related harm, and institutional accountability.
Interpretation and novelty
The findings highlight the need for transparent and ethical practices in vaccine trials to address HCWs' concerns and ensure their participation, particularly in the context of historical research controversies.
Scientific and public-health significance
These findings can inform the ethical design and implementation of Lassa fever vaccine trials in endemic settings, emphasizing the importance of addressing HCWs' concerns to ensure their participation and the success of vaccine trials.
Limitations and evidence strength
The study's limitations include its qualitative nature and the small sample size, which may limit generalizability. The findings are specific to the context of AKTH and may not be directly applicable to other settings.

Environmental Drivers and Spatial Patterns of Lassa Fever Cases in Nigeria: A GIS-Based Approach to Dynamic Susceptibility Mapping

Original abstract (English)
Lassa fever is a major yet persistently neglected viral hemorrhagic disease in West Africa, with an estimated 100,000-300,000 infections and approximately 5,000 deaths reported annually across the region. Case-fatality rates are typically above 15% among hospitalized patients and may approach 50% during epidemic periods, with Nigeria accounting for the largest reported burden of confirmed cases. This study presents a pilot multi-layer GIS framework for the spatial recording, visualization, and analysis of hemorrhagic fever infections in Nigeria, focusing primarily on Lassa fever. The framework integrates epidemiological observations with environmental information to move beyond conventional static disease maps toward dynamic assessment of disease susceptibility. Confirmed Lassa fever cases recorded between 2023 and 2025 were combined with weather variables obtained from Open-Meteo, CHIRPS precipitation data, land-use information, lithological characteristics, and elevation datasets. The datasets were temporally aggregated and statistically analyzed, while multiple GIS layers were used to identify and visualize recurring spatial patterns. Ondo, Edo, Bauchi, and Taraba consistently emerged as prominent hotspot states during the study period. Weekly temporal aggregation was adopted to correspond with the reporting frequency of official epidemic surveillance, enabling short-term environmental conditions to be examined in relation to confirmed cases. The findings indicate a pronounced seasonal pattern, with increased Lassa fever activity occurring predominantly during dry periods characterized by minimal rainfall, fewer precipitation hours, and reduced humidity, particularly between December and March. Of the 28 environmental variables examined, precipitation- and humidity-related indicators demonstrated the strongest inverse associations with weekly confirmed cases, with Spearman correlation coefficients reaching {rho} =-0.75. Air temperature showed the strongest and most consistent positive association, reaching {rho} = 0.633. These findings demonstrate the potential of integrating epidemiological surveillance and environmental GIS layers to characterize spatial and temporal patterns of Lassa fever and provide a foundation for more dynamic susceptibility mapping and environmental risk assessment in Nigeria.

Original abstract (English)
BACKGROUND: Viral infections associated with hemorrhagic fevers (VaHFs) cause millions of cases each year across a broad geography with severity ranging from asymptomatic cases to clinical disease with a high case fatality rate. Commonalities in pathophysiology across VaHFs include vascular damage and plasma leakage with the possibility of hemorrhage, organ failure, shock, and death. These changes are seen on autopsy, and evidence of plasma leakage can be readily detected on ultrasound. METHODS: We conducted a systematic review to assess ultrasound findings in VaHFs and their ability to predict severe disease (PROSPERO CRD42024620862). PubMed was searched through May 2025. Any articles presenting original, patient-level findings on ultrasound in VaHFs with serologic and/or molecular confirmation were included. Case series and duplicative datasets were excluded. A QUADAS-2 tool was used to assess bias. Study level characteristics and patient-level data on the ultrasound findings were extracted. The crude proportions of ultrasound findings were reported, and weighted meta-analysis using a random-effects model was performed and displayed in forest plots when the data allowed. Sensitivity, specificity, and positive/negative likelihood ratios for severe disease were calculated and visualized using a random effects model in SROC plots when the data allowed. RESULTS: In total, 457 unique publications were screened for inclusion, and 56 publications comprising 6979 participants were included in the review, including 44 publications (6189 participants) on dengue, six (494 participants) on Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever, three (134 participants) on hantavirus disease, two (116 participants) on yellow fever, and one (46 participants) on Lassa fever. Participants were most often recruited while acutely sick during periods of high transmission at tertiary care centers. Ultrasound findings included gallbladder wall thickening, ascites, pleural effusions and abnormal lung ultrasound, pericardial effusions, abdominal lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, abnormal pancreatic ultrasound, and hyperechoic kidneys or liver. Pericardial effusions and several other findings were associated with severe disease. CONCLUSIONS: Common pathophysiology leading to plasma leakage results in sonographic signs across viral infections associated with hemorrhagic fevers, and some findings can help predict severe disease. Important limitations included reliance on a single database and possibility of missing manuscripts from local journals, the small number of publications on non-dengue VaHFs, patient recruitment overwhelmingly from the in-hospital setting at tertiary referral centers during high-transmission periods, high degree of heterogeneity, and bias in the timing domain among the studies on dengue. We first propose ultrasound findings be incorporated into new definitions of severity for VaHFs and then introduce an Ultrasound-based assessment of Plasma leakage Severity (UPS) as a hypothesis generating framework to help guide future, prospective research. We discuss some virus and organ-specific considerations and conclude with detailed considerations for future research.

She recalls the recent discharge of a critically ill newborn who survived after weeks of intensive care. “There were moments when we thought the baby might not survive. Watching the child leave the hospital with the parents was one of the happiest moments for the entire team.” Those moments help offset the emotional strain of losing patients who arrive too late for treatment to reverse severe complications, and the exhaustion that follows physically demanding days. Health workers spend long hours wearing full personal protective equipment (PPE), often emerging drenched in sweat after attending to patients. “We cannot afford to let our guard down,” Dr Isaac says. “Lassa fever does not discriminate. If infection prevention measures are not followed strictly, healthcare workers become vulnerable too.” Stepping up disease response Support from the Nigeria Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (NCDC)⁠ and humanitarian partners, including ALIMA (The Alliance for International Medical Action), has strengthened infection prevention and control in Ondo, but clinicians say improved supplies of laboratory reagents, PPE and medicines such as the antiviral ribavirin are essential if treatment centres are to keep pace with the outbreak. While hospitals remain at the centre of the response, Ondo State has increasingly recognised that controlling Lassa fever begins long before patients reach an isolation ward. One important investment has been in the strengthening of diagnostic capacity. In partnership with the Institute of Human Virology Nigeria (IHVN), the state upgraded its Public Health Laboratory in Akure to complement testing at FMC Owo. The expanded laboratory network has shortened the time needed to confirm suspected cases, allowing treatment to begin earlier while improving surveillance. For Ondo State Commissioner for Health, Dr Banji Ajaka, preventing deaths depends as much on public awareness as on the quality of hospital care. He has repeatedly urged residents not to dismiss persistent fever as “ordinary malaria” and has encouraged people to report early to designated treatment centres, where diagnosis and treatment are provided free of charge. According to the commissioner, every delay increases the risk of severe complications and makes outbreak control more difficult. The state has also expanded community engagement with support from UNICEF Nigeria⁠. Through its Risk Communication and Community Engagement (RCCE) programme, health educators have taken prevention messages directly to markets, schools, churches and other public spaces across Ondo’s 18 local government areas. Rather than relying solely on radio announcements, officials speak directly with traders and residents about proper food storage, environmental sanitation and the importance of seeking medical care if fever persists after malaria treatment. The objective is to replace fear and misinformation with practical knowledge that can prevent infections and encourage earlier diagnosis. Disease in a changing world Understanding why outbreaks are becoming less predictable has also become a priority. According to Associate Professor Olayinka Osuolale, an environmental management and toxicology expert at Elizade University, Ilara-Mokin, Ondo State, Lassa fever is increasingly being shaped by changes in the environment. Moderate rainfall encourages vegetation growth and increases food availability for rodents, while flooding destroys burrows and drives infected animals into homes and food stores, increasing opportunities for transmission. Osuolale believes climate forecasting should become part of Nigeria’s disease surveillance system, allowing health authorities to anticipate periods of increased risk and prepare hospitals, laboratories and response teams before outbreaks intensify. His recommendations reflect the growing adoption of a One Health approach, which recognises that human, animal and environmental health are closely interconnected. Better housing, safer food storage, improved sanitation and effective waste management, he argues, are as important to preventing Lassa fever as clinical treatment. New tools called for Even with stronger surveillance and improved preparedness, experts agree that a long-term solution will require new, additional tools. Several Lassa fever vaccine candidates are currently advancing through clinical development with support from the Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations (CEPI)⁠ and international research partners. Although no vaccine has yet been licensed for public use, researchers hope a successful candidate will eventually be able to protect health workers and people living in endemic communities. Until then, early diagnosis, prompt treatment, robust surveillance and community engagement remain the most effective defences. For Fatoyinbo, the lesson is deeply personal. Recovering from Lassa fever has transformed both his outlook and his sense of purpose. “I am grateful for life,” he says. “Every day I became stronger, I became more thankful.” He now believes survivors have an important role to play in changing public perceptions of the disease. “People should not stigmatise anyone with Lassa fever,” he says. “Fear is one reason some people delay going to hospital. They need to know that recovery is possible if they seek help early.”

The latest cases were reported in Ondo, Bauchi, Edo and Benue States, adding to a pattern of transmission that has repeatedly affected several states and communities across the country. For health experts, however, the statistics tell only part of the story. Behind the numbers are patients whose early symptoms can be mistaken for malaria, families who may delay hospital visits, healthcare workers exposed to infection and communities living in environments where rodents can thrive. When a familiar fever hides a dangerous disease For Amina Peter’s family, Lassa fever did not initially look like a life-threatening disease. When her fever persisted for several days, her relatives assumed she had malaria. They purchased over-the-counter medication and waited for her condition to improve. Instead, her weakness became increasingly severe. It was only then that the family took her to a hospital, where further testing was carried out. Her experience illustrates one of the central challenges in Nigeria’s response to Lassa fever: the disease can begin with symptoms that resemble common illnesses, particularly malaria, potentially allowing patients to remain at home until their condition deteriorates. Dr Eno Onen, a community physician at University College Hospital, Ibadan, said early symptoms of Lassa fever were frequently mild and could easily be mistaken for malaria. He stressed the importance of maintaining a high level of clinical suspicion, particularly in states and communities where Lassa fever transmission is known to occur. “My advice is this: if you experience fever lasting more than 48 hours that does not respond to malaria treatment, don’t manage it at home.” Onen said persistent fever should prompt people to seek medical attention, especially where there has been possible exposure to rodents or contact with someone suspected of having the disease. The warning is significant because untreated Lassa fever can progress from nonspecific symptoms such as fever, headache, weakness and stomach discomfort to much more serious complications. In severe cases, patients may develop bleeding, facial swelling, breathing difficulties, confusion and seizures. The disease can ultimately become fatal. How Lassa fever enters homes Lassa fever is a viral haemorrhagic disease caused by the Lassa virus. Its principal reservoir is the multimammate rat, which can shed the virus through urine, droppings and other biological materials. Humans can become infected through contact with contaminated food, water, household surfaces and environments. According to Onen, rodent-to-human transmission accounts for the overwhelming majority of infections. “Together, these rodent-to-human routes account for an estimated 90 percent to 95 per cent of human Lassa infections,” he said. The remaining infections can occur through contact with the blood, urine, saliva and other body fluids of infected people. This form of transmission presents a particular concern in healthcare environments, where inadequate infection prevention and control measures can expose health workers and other patients. Environment plays a major role Experts increasingly argue that Nigeria’s Lassa fever challenge cannot be separated from changes in the environment. Dr Ekpereonne Esu, Associate Director of Cochrane Nigeria, linked part of the continuing transmission to ecological disruption in affected communities. Deforestation, biodiversity loss and changing rainfall patterns, he said, can influence rodent populations and behaviour, potentially increasing contact between infected animals and humans. Seasonal rainfall can also affect where multimammate rats seek food and shelter. When environmental conditions become less favourable in their natural habitats, rodents may move closer to human settlements and homes. “Deforestation adds another dimension because expanding farmland into forested areas can create favourable conditions for rodents while simultaneously reducing natural habitats and disrupting ecological balance. “Biodiversity loss, caused by human activities, reduces natural predators that ordinarily control rat populations, allowing the rodents to multiply and spread infections more easily,” Esu said. Housing conditions can further increase the risk. Cracks and gaps in walls, leaking roofs, poorly fitted doors and inadequate waste management can give rodents easy access to houses. Once inside, they can contaminate food, water and household surfaces. The TETFund-sponsored Mega Research Project on Lassa fever also identified poor housing, rodent infestation, food contamination, leaking roofs and poorly fitted doors as significant environmental risk factors. These findings reinforce the growing importance of a One Health approach to Lassa fever—one that recognises the links between human health, animal populations and the environment. 23 states affected NCDC data showed that by epidemiological week 33, confirmed Lassa fever infections had been recorded in 23 states and 117 local government areas. Although transmission remains concentrated in particular parts of the country, the geographical spread demonstrates that the disease remains a national public-health concern. Bauchi, Ondo, Taraba, Benue and Edo accounted for 87 per cent of cumulative confirmed cases by week 33. The weekly figures have not followed a consistent upward pattern. Four new cases were recorded in week 32, compared with 17 in week 31, before the number increased again to 14 in week 33. Similarly, week 30 recorded 17 cases after 20 cases in week 29, while 25 cases were recorded in week 28 following 14 cases in week 27. Such fluctuations can make the outbreak appear less threatening during weeks when fewer cases are recorded. But health experts warn that a temporary decline does not mean transmission has stopped. As long as new infections continue to emerge, the disease remains active and requires surveillance and prevention. Why the fatality rate matters The 24.4 per cent case fatality rate reported by week 33 is particularly concerning. It suggests that significant challenges remain in the early recognition, diagnosis and management of severe disease, even as Nigeria continues to strengthen surveillance and outbreak-response systems. Younger adults are among the groups frequently affected. Recent NCDC reports identified people aged 21 to 30 as the predominant affected age group, while research findings point to substantial exposure among adults aged 20 to 40. Researchers have associated this pattern partly with farming and outdoor activities, which can increase contact with rodents and potentially contaminated environments. Food storage practices can also contribute to exposure when food is kept in places accessible to rodents. Searching for answers among survivors While prevention and treatment remain immediate priorities, Nigerian researchers are also looking for answers in people who survive the disease. At Abubakar Tafawa Balewa University Teaching Hospital in Bauchi, scientists are studying Lassa fever survivors to understand why some infected people recover while others develop severe disease or die. Prof. Alash’le Abimiku, Executive Director of the International Research Centre of Excellence and Institute of Human Virology Nigeria, said the research could contribute to the development of an effective vaccine. “We want to study survivors and understand what made them survive,” Abimiku said. Researchers are examining survivors’ immune responses while also studying different Lassa virus lineages circulating in Nigeria. The research programme goes beyond laboratory investigations. It includes community engagement, clinician training, improved diagnosis and laboratory studies, reflecting the need to connect scientific findings with practical public-health measures. Abimiku also noted that Lassa fever was no longer strictly seasonal, with infections now being recorded throughout the year. The shift strengthens the argument for continuous surveillance and prevention rather than relying primarily on seasonal responses. During the presentation of research findings, TETFund Executive Secretary Sonny Echono highlighted the need to accelerate the search for solutions, including vaccine development. “What is the shortest route toward developing a vaccine? How can we ensure that the burden on our people is reduced?” Echono asked during the presentation of research findings. Saturday, September 19, 2026 Follow Us on Google Search Home National Columns Business China News Politics & Power Opinion Entertainment The Sun TV Sporting Sun The Sun Foundation NCDC’s alarm over Lassa fever 16 September 2026 12:18 am WAT By The Sun Follow Us on Google The Nigeria Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (NCDC) has raised the alarm over the approaching peak season for Lassa fever, warning states and local governments in the country to adopt proactive measures against the disease. The centre expressed concern over delayed presentation of patients at health facilities, infections among healthcare workers, poor sanitation and food storage, exposure to rodents and inadequate outbreak preparedness by some states, as major challenges in confronting the menace. Director-General of NCDC, Dr. Jide Idris, who raised the alarm at a press briefing on Nigeria’s preparedness for the 2026/2027 Lassa fever season, identified Ondo, Bauchi, Taraba, Edo and Benue as the five high burden states for Lassa fever in the country, stressing that they account for 86 per cent of cases so far in 2026. Idris noted that the disease occurs throughout the year, with tendency to increase during the dry season, particularly between November and April, hence the plans by the NCDC to accelerate readiness within the period before the yearly fever peak period of the epidemic. He called on healthcare workers, state governments, communities and all Nigerians to do their part in preventing infections, reducing deaths and strengthening preparedness for Lassa fever. The alarm by the NCDC DG is timely and in order. Lassa fever has become an annual infection in the country, with huge costs on the health of the people and national economy. By December 2024, NCDC had recorded 1,154 confirmed cases of Lassa fever out of the 9,492 suspected cases and 190 deaths. From December 30, 2024 to January 12, 2025, 143 confirmed cases of Lassa fever out of 484 suspected cases had been recorded across seven states and 32 local government areas in the country. Within the period, NCDC also reported 22 deaths, resulting in a Case Fatality Rate (CFR) of 15.4 per cent, within the period. According to the centre, the deaths occurred in Ondo (6), Edo (5), Bauchi (2), Taraba (6), Ebonyi (2), and Gombe (1). The case fatality rate has consistently remained high, prompting the need for preemptive measures against the disease. Lassa fever remains endemic in Nigeria, posing a significant public health risk across all states. The degree of occurrence poses serious challenge to the authorities and the people. Lassa fever, also known as Lassa hemorrhagic fever, according to experts, is a type of viral hemorrhagic fever caused by the Lassa virus. Many victims of the virus do not develop symptoms. When symptoms occur, they typically include fever, weakness, headaches, vomiting, and muscle pains. Less commonly, there may be bleeding from the mouth or gastrointestinal tract. The risk of death once infected is about one per cent and frequently occurs within two weeks of the onset of symptoms. The disease is usually initially spread to people via contact with the urine or feces of an infected multimammate mouse. Other rodents can also act as carriers of the virus. Spread can then occur via direct contact between people. Diagnosis based on symptoms is difficult, except by laboratory testing. Tackling the disease demands proactive actions by the government and the people. The assurance by the NCDC of rolling out an accelerated plan that would strengthen the entire response chain, from prevention and early detection to diagnosis, referral and treatment, is therefore commendable. Let the NCDC prioritise early detection, prompt reporting and investigation, faster transportation and testing of samples, and timely transmission of laboratory results. Treatment centres in high-risk locations should also be prepared to receive patients, with essential medicines, laboratory supplies and protective equipment made available where required. To check transmission and spread of the disease, the NCDC should intensify the campaign on environmental sanitation, waste management and safer food storage, while working with environmental health, agriculture and livestock authorities. Adequate enlightenment programmes on the dangers of the disease are needed. Let the people and communities be carried along in the enlightenment efforts. Lassa fever spreads in poor hygienic environment which breeds rats and other dangerous rodents. Members of the public have a role to play in preventing the occurrence of the disease. Families and households need to keep food such as rice, garri, beans and maize in covered containers, block openings through which rodents enter homes, reduce hiding places and dispose of refuse properly. Apart from proper storage of food items, the people should keep their environment clean and avoid drying food stuff outside, on the ground or roadside. They should discourage bush burning and deforestation. People with fever or persistent symptoms, particularly those living in or travelling to affected areas, should seek medical attention promptly and desist from self-medication.
