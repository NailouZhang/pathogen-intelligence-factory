Hemagglutinin and fusion proteins precede measles virus ribonucleoproteins and marshal cell-to-cell spread in airway epithelium.

Authors: Stephanie E. Clark, Justin W. Kaufman, Kalpana Yadav, Roberto Cattaneo, Brajesh K. Singh, Patrick L. Sinn
Original abstract (English)
ABSTRACT Measles virus (MeV) is the most contagious human respiratory pathogen. To understand how it spreads in the airways, we infected well-differentiated primary cultures of human airway epithelial cells (HAE). We discovered that the MeV attachment protein (hemagglutinin, H), the fusion (F) protein, and the assembly organizer protein (matrix, M) reach newly infected cells ahead of, and independently from, the replicative complex proteins. To characterize the mechanisms of MeV spread, we generated a replication-deficient adenoviral vector expressing the MeV M, F, and H proteins in a doxycycline-inducible manner. This viral vector formed infectious centers that preserved epithelial function like those from MeV infection. To further dissect this process, we generated Ad vectors expressing individual MeV proteins, termed Ad-M, Ad-F, and Ad-H. Transduction of HAE with single, double, and triple Ad vector combinations revealed that Ad-F and Ad-H are necessary and sufficient to form MeV-like infectious centers. MeV enters and spreads within airway epithelia via H protein binding to adherens junction protein, nectin-4. To disrupt spread, we introduced the point mutation HY543A, a mutation previously described to inhibit nectin-4 binding, in the Ad-H vector. Co-transduction of Ad-F and Ad-HY543A did not result in MeV-like infectious center formation. Finally, we assessed whether the MeV-related paramyxovirus Nipah (NiV) attachment and fusion proteins (G and F, respectively) support cell-to-cell spread. Co-transduction of HAE with Ad-NiV-G and Ad-NiV-F did not result in infectious center formation, suggesting that decoupling of the membrane fusion apparatus function from replication is a unique feature of the MeV envelope glycoproteins. IMPORTANCE Measles virus is a highly contagious pathogen. We characterized a mechanism by which measles virus spreads rapidly through the cells that line the human airway in an ex vivo model. We show that the proteins of the viral membrane fusion apparatus, hemagglutinin (H) and fusion (F), move independently from and reach newly infected cells before the proteins of the replicative complex. Thus, viral protein spread is assembled and arranged in order (i.e., marshaled). Using adenovirus-based vectors, we show that co-expression of H and F proteins promotes rapid sequential spread to multiple cells and the formation of infectious centers. Further elucidation of the mechanism of measles virus amplification and cell-to-cell spread within the lung may help explain its highly contagious nature. Measles virus is a highly contagious pathogen.

Interpreting changes in reported case fatality in endemic zoonoses: an analytical framework and ecological analysis of Lassa fever surveillance in Nigeria, 2017-26

Authors: David G. Simons
Original abstract (English)
BackgroundNigerias reported Lassa fever case fatality ratio (CFR) rose from 18.9% at week 23 of 2025 to 25.0% in 2026, prompting concerns over failing clinical care. Because surveillance artefacts can also alter CFRs, candidate mechanisms that published data can resolve were identified and evaluated. MethodsThis ecological study extracted weekly state-level counts from 474 Nigeria Centre for Disease Control and Prevention situation reports (2017-2026). Per a pre-specified plan, years were compared at phase-matched points, year-on-year CFR changes were decomposed into within-state and between-state components, and tipping-point ascertainment shifts calculated. Weekly incident and cumulative published totals were compared post hoc. FindingsOf 11 candidate mechanisms, 2 could be evaluated fully and 5 in part. On weekly incident counts, 2026 did not differ from 2025 at the phase-matched midpoint (+0.3 percentage points, 95% CI -5.0 to 7.6); within-state (-1.1, 95% CI -4.7 to 3.0) and between-state (+0.5, -2.0 to 2.9) components spanned zero. Conversely, the published cumulative CFR was 5.4 to 6.2 percentage points higher because 70 deaths were added retrospectively by week 36 outside weekly reports. Reconciling cumulative figures would require 279 missed survivors (6% of suspected cases not confirmed). InterpretationThe 2026 CFR rise came from deaths added to cumulative totals outside the weekly counts, and published data cannot attribute it to care. Endemic zoonotic CFRs cannot serve as clinical outcome measures unless testing volumes, deaths added late, and post-mortem sampling are published alongside case counts. The data that would separate these explanations (specimens tested, the share of cases sampled at or after death, the share with a known outcome, and deaths added late by week of confirmation) are collected but not published. Publishing them would let progress towards national CFR targets be tracked and would support planning of Lassa fever vaccine trials.

This systematic review examines the human immunological response to NiV infection and its interplay with pathogenesis, discusses the mechanisms of NiV transmission from bats to humans and among humans, and reviews the current landscape of NiV vaccine development. NiV has evolved multiple strategies to evade innate immunity, and an effective immune response (involving both robust interferon activity and adaptive responses) is critical for host survival. Major transmission pathways include bat-to-human (e.g. via contaminated date palm sap), animal-to-human (e.g. via pigs or horses), and close-contact spread, which have been implicated in several outbreaks. Finally, I summarize progress in NiV vaccine research: several vaccine candidates (e.g., viral-vectored, subunit, and mRNA-based vaccines) have demonstrated protective immunity in preclinical models and are in Phase 1 trials. Given the rarity of outbreaks, innovative regulatory strategies are being pursued to expedite vaccine availability. Overall, strengthening our understanding of NiV immunity and transmission, alongside advancing vaccines, is pivotal to reduce the threat of this deadly virus.

Original abstract (English)
Emerging and re-emerging infectious diseases (EIDs and REIDs) continue to pose a major threat to human and animal health globally. The close interactions among wildlife, livestock, and humans especially in areas where natural habitats are disturbed create ideal conditions for diseases to spill over from animals to humans. Changes in land use, deforestation, climate change, urban expansion, and global travel have contributed both to the rise in new infections and the return of old ones. Outbreaks of Nipah virus in Malaysia, Ebola in West Africa, and COVID-19 highlight the large-scale epidemics caused by human–wildlife contact. Ecological pressure and increasing movement of people and goods have facilitated the cross-species transmission of pathogens. In India, outbreaks of diseases like Lumpy Skin Disease, avian influenza, and Nipah virus have affected both public health and the livestock sector, causing significant economic losses. The One Health approach—linking human, animal, and environmental health—is now essential for controlling disease risk. Strategies like improved surveillance, better diagnostics, vaccine development, responsible use of antibiotics, and restoration of ecological buffer zones are essential in mitigating the risk of disease. This review emphasizes the need for coordinated action across disciplines to reduce future outbreaks and improve preparedness for zoonotic and environmentally driven infectious diseases.

Singapore experienced a significant increase in air pollution, with the one-hour PM2.5 reading reaching 284 micrograms per cubic metre. The National Environment Agency (NEA) warned that the 24-hour Pollutant Standards Index (PSI) could rise to the Very Unhealthy range. In response, the Government distributed N95 masks to vulnerable groups, including seniors, CHAS cardholders, pregnant women, children aged seven to 16, and those with medical conditions. The one-hour PM2.5 reading in the west surged to 284 micrograms per cubic metre at 2pm, while the 24-hour Pollutant Standards Index (PSI) remained in the unhealthy range across the island. The National Environment Agency (NEA) has warned that the 24-hour PSI could climb into the Very Unhealthy range over the coming day. In response, the Government has brought forward the distribution of N95 masks to vulnerable Singaporeans, with supplies being rolled out from 14 October. Eligible groups include seniors from the Pioneer and Merdeka Generations, CHAS Blue and Orange cardholders, pregnant women, children aged seven to 16, and people with medical conditions such as asthma. Masks are being distributed through community clubs, selected public transport hubs, Social Service Offices and other designated venues, although availability will vary by location. If you or someone in your household is eligible, check HazeMaskGoWhere for collection points and live stock updates before heading out. Each eligible person can collect a pack containing two N95 masks, subject to availability. For everyone else, it’s a good time to rethink outdoor plans. NEA advises healthy individuals to reduce prolonged or strenuous outdoor exercise when the 24-hour PSI is unhealthy, and avoid it if conditions become very unhealthy. Children, pregnant women and older adults should minimise outdoor activity in these conditions, while people with chronic heart or lung conditions should take extra precautions. Keep an eye on the latest NEA haze updates and hourly PM2.5 readings, as conditions can change throughout the day. Image credit: Health Hub | Website September 2026 If Singapore suddenly starts looking a little hazy, there's a reason. The Government has been stepping up preparations for a potential haze episode, with agencies reviewing their plans as dry and hot conditions across the region increase the risk of fires—and potentially, smoke drifting our way. That doesn't mean Singapore is definitely heading for a full-blown haze crisis. Whether smoke actually reaches us depends on where fires are burning, how intense they are, wind direction and rainfall. But with conditions looking less than ideal, it's a good time to know what to look out for. So, What's Causing The Haze Risk? Much of the concern comes from fires in parts of Indonesia, particularly Sumatra and Kalimantan. Hotter and drier conditions linked to El Niño are making it easier for fires to start and spread, while the prevailing winds can potentially carry smoke towards Singapore. Singapore's Government Haze Task Force, led by the National Environment Agency, has been reviewing its preparations and has plans in place for different levels of haze. How Will You Know If The Haze Is Bad? Keep an eye on Singapore's Pollutant Standards Index (PSI) and, particularly, the one-hour PM2.5 readings. The PSI gives you a broader picture of air quality, while PM2.5 measures tiny particles that can penetrate deep into the lungs. If you're deciding whether to head out for a run, take the kids to the playground or spend the afternoon outside, the PM2.5 reading is particularly useful. As a general guide, a PSI of 101 or above is considered unhealthy. Image credit: Health Hub | Website What Does Haze Do To Your Health? Even relatively mild haze can cause irritated eyes, a scratchy throat, coughing and breathing discomfort. As the air quality worsens, the risks increase, particularly for children, older people, pregnant women and those with existing heart or lung conditions. If you develop significant breathing difficulties, chest pain or other worrying symptoms, seek medical attention. How Can You Protect Yourself? The good news is there are some very easy things you can do if the air quality deteriorates: Check the air quality: Use the myENV app or NEA's official updates rather than relying on how hazy the sky looks. Limit outdoor exercise: If the PSI rises into the unhealthy range, swap your outdoor run for an indoor workout. Keep windows and doors closed: Particularly when outdoor air quality is poor. Use an air purifier: A purifier with an appropriate filter can help reduce indoor particulate pollution. Wear an N95 if needed: If you have to spend time outdoors during unhealthy haze, a properly fitted N95 mask can help reduce exposure to fine particles. Look after kids and vulnerable family members: Consider moving outdoor play and activities indoors when air quality worsens. Should You Be Worried? Not yet, but it's worth being prepared. The Government is monitoring the situation closely, and Singapore has an established system for responding if haze levels deteriorate. So, rather than panic-buying every air purifier in Singapore, just keep an eye on the readings, dig out those N95s if you already have them and maybe have a few indoor activities ready to go.
