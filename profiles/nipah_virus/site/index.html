Nanobodies targeting a conserved henipavirus G glycoprotein receptor-binding region potently protect against Nipah virus infection.

Authors: Mengmeng Sun, Qizan Gong, Yanfeng Yao, Haotian Sun, Fangxu Li, Li Chen, Cheng Peng, Baoyue Zhang, Ge Gao, Rui Gong
Original abstract (English)
Nipah virus (NiV) and Hendra virus (HeV) are highly lethal zoonotic henipaviruses with pandemic potential, yet no approved antiviral therapeutics are currently available. Here, we identified four nanobodies targeting the viral attachment glycoprotein (G) with strong neutralizing activity. All candidates potently neutralized NiV-Malaysia (NiV M ) and NiV-Bangladesh (NiV B ) in authentic virus assays, with IC 50 values in the low ng/mL range (2.50-18.3 ng/mL). 2E2 shows the most potent activity, with an IC 50 of 2.50 ng/mL. These candidates cross-neutralized HeV in pseudovirus assays at levels comparable to those against NiV, yet authentic HeV neutralization was reduced with the most potent clone reaching an IC50 of 160 ng/mL and the remaining clones showing variable potency. In Syrian hamsters challenged with 1000 LD 50 NiV M , prophylactic administration of 1C3, 2E2, or 5A3 provides complete protection. Notably, 2E2 confers superior therapeutic protection (66.7% survival) when administered 24 h post-infection. Additionally, 2E2 provides 50% prophylactic protection against lethal HeV. Alanine-scanning mutagenesis indicated that the nanobodies recognize a conserved region within the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of G, with distinct sensitivities to individual residue substitutions. Notably, 2E2 exhibited minimal sensitivity to the tested NiV G mutations and retained neutralizing activity against the m102.4 escape-associated substitutions NiV G-V507I and HeV G-D582N. These findings identify 2E2 as a candidate RBD-targeting nanobody with potent prophylactic and therapeutic efficacy against NiV and cross-neutralizing activity against HeV, warranting further evaluation as a countermeasure for NiV outbreaks.

A Synthetic Consensus Chiroptera IFN-β Induces IFN-I Signaling Across Diverse Bat Taxa

Authors: Julianne Bullock, Brown Bulloch, Anthony McBain, Tom Hill, Aaron Carmody, Yi He, Lucas De Sousa Paula, Paul A. Beare, Craig Martens, Arthur Wickenhagen
Original abstract (English)
Bat-associated viruses consistently spill over from bats into humans and other animals. Many bat-associated viruses, including Marburg virus and Nipah virus, cause severe disease in humans but their presentation is subclinical in bats. Advancements in tools and resources to study bat innate antiviral immune responses, including the type I IFN (IFN-I) pathway, are needed to evaluate the mechanisms that underlie differential infection outcomes between reservoir bats and spillover hosts. Commercially available IFN-Is are not bat-specific, and conspecific IFN-Is are impractical to produce for the over 1,500 described bat species. Here, we design and validate a bat order-level consensus IFN (Chiroptera IFN-β) that is functional in bat cells from six diverse bat families, including representatives from both suborders. Using Jamaican fruit bat cells, we demonstrate that Chiroptera IFN-β induces an IFN-stimulated gene repertoire comparable to conspecific IFN-β. This valuable resource can accelerate the capacity of the field to study bat innate antiviral responses and illuminate differences between bats and humans.

DeepMolVir: Learning Virtual Virus Representations for Sequence-Aware Virus–Inhibitor Activity Prediction

Authors: Pengxuan Ren, Fengyu Cai, Mengyu Cheng, Ye Zhao, Shihang Wang, Yunze Zhang, Lu Liu, Qian Zheng, Xianglei Zhang
Original abstract (English)
Abstract The growing threat of emerging and drug-resistant viruses demands efficient approaches for predicting virus–inhibitor interactions. However, most existing computational models treat viruses as categorical identifiers and fail to capture sequence-level biological information. Here, we assembled a data set of 50,711 compound–virus activity pairs spanning 178 viral species and explored protein language models as a strategy for viral representation learning. Using embeddings generated by ESMC-600M, we introduce a virtual virus representation in which viruses are encoded as sequence-derived biological entities rather than as taxonomic labels. News Brief
The World Health Organization (WHO), the Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations (CEPI), and PATH have collaborated to develop a standardized Nipah disease severity score (DSS) to enhance outbreak preparedness and vaccine research. This initiative was announced on 30 October 2026, with a stakeholder consultation held in Kathmandu, Nepal, from 11 to 13 August 2026. The DSS aims to address the high case fatality ratios of Nipah virus infection and improve the prediction of severe disease outcomes. The collaboration focuses on the WHO South-East Asia Region, which includes countries such as Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Myanmar, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Thailand, and Timor-Leste. The development of the DSS is part of the NiVOTriPP project, which seeks to create a flexible and adaptive clinical trial protocol for evaluating Nipah vaccine efficacy in the event of large outbreaks. The status of the initiative is under investigation as the scoring system is being refined and validated. This effort is critical for strengthening public-private partnerships and promoting research collaboration in the region.

Recognising the growing threat posed by the virus and its emergence in countries of the WHO South-East Asia Region, WHO South-East Asia Regional Office (SEARO) designated Nipah virus as a regional priority pathogen in 2018. To strengthen regional preparedness and response, WHO subsequently developed the South-East Asia Regional Strategy for the Prevention and Control of Nipah Virus Infection 2023–2030, which provides a comprehensive framework for prevention, preparedness, detection, response, and resilience against Nipah virus infection across the Region. Addressing a critical evidence gap Although Nipah virus infection continues to be associated with high case fatality ratios, the ability to accurately predict which patients are at greatest risk of severe disease and poor outcomes remains limited. Standardised and validated disease severity scores could help address this challenge. Recognising the importance of developing a standardised Nipah disease severity score (DSS), a critical tool for outbreak response, clinical research, and vaccine evaluation, WHO SEARO collaborated with the Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations (CEPI) and PATH to convene a stakeholder consultation under the CEPI’s Nipah Vaccine Outbreak Trial Planning and Preparedness (NiVOTriPP) Project. WHO, CEPI and PATH convened a stakeholder consultation in Kathmandu, Nepal, from 11 to 13 August to advance the development of a Nipah disease severity score. (Photo credit: CEPI) The NiVOTriPP project was initiated to develop a scenario-specific, flexible, and adaptive clinical trial protocol for evaluation of Nipah vaccine efficacy (VE) in case of a potentially large outbreak (i.e., Nipah-X). While the current epidemiology of Nipah virus disease makes it practically infeasible to evaluate VE, any change in epidemiology leading to larger outbreaks may allow conduct of such trials. NiVOTriPP is set up to prepare for such a potential scenario. The DSS is being developed in parallel with the protocol under NiVOTriPP, and its primary purpose is to support vaccine evaluation in clinical trials by enabling the standardised and consistent measurement of disease severity during Nipah virus outbreaks. The consultation also responded to calls for strengthening public-private partnerships in the development of medical countermeasures and for promoting research collaboration as outlined in the implementation framework of the WHO South-East Asia Regional Strategy for the Prevention and Control of Nipah Virus Infection 2023–2030. Bringing together multidisciplinary expertise The consultation brought together international and regional experts in Nipah virus surveillance, outbreak response, vaccine trial design in outbreak settings, clinical management, biostatistics and disease severity score development. The primary objective of the consultation was to obtain expert input on the factors to be included in the scoring system and the methodological approaches for possible derivation, refinement, and validation. During the three-day meeting, participants reviewed the available evidence and identified the most relevant clinical signs, symptoms, and other factors for inclusion in the DSS. Experts also reviewed methodological approaches for score development, refinement, and validation. The consultation brought together multidisciplinary experts from across the WHO South-East Asia Region and beyond. (Photo credit: CEPI) Next steps An initial draft DSS was developed based on the discussions and consensus reached during the consultation. Further review and refinement will be undertaken through a series of technical working group activities during the next phase of the project. WHO SEARO, CEPI, and PATH will continue to partner on refining and validating the draft DSS. In addition to its primary intended use as a standardised, evidence-based tool for estimating vaccine efficacy in forthcoming studies, the DSS has the potential to support therapeutic research and improve clinical management of Nipah virus disease across the region. The model incorporates memory effects from past infection states, which classical integer-order models do not account for. The study highlights that reducing the fractional order delayed epidemic peaks and prolonged infection duration, with peak infection levels lowered by over 40% in some scenarios. Birds showed a monotone decline across all fractional orders, though slower for smaller values. The authors emphasize the importance of early interventions to manage outbreaks, as optimal control strategies significantly reduced infected human and pig populations. However, the model is theoretical and not based on specific outbreak data, relying on existing literature for parameter values. The findings suggest that coordinated, early interventions targeting both human and animal populations can shape whether a zoonotic spillover event fades out or persists.

The study, published as open access research in Discover Analytics on 13 July 2026 by Sunday Oluwafemi Gbodogbe and Adedapo Chris Loyinmi, formulates the model using the Caputo fractional derivative, an operator that incorporates memory effects arising from past infection states, which classical integer-order models do not account for. The motivation rests on the epidemiology of Nipah virus, a zoonotic pathogen the World Health Organization has classified as a priority pathogen requiring urgent research attention. Bats of the genus Pteropus serve as the primary natural reservoir, pigs act as intermediate hosts, and humans acquire infection through contaminated fruit, exposure to bat saliva or urine, contact with infected pigs, or human-to-human transmission in healthcare and family settings. No approved vaccine or targeted antiviral treatment exists, and patient management relies primarily on supportive care, with antiviral agents such as ribavirin showing limited effectiveness. The model divides human and pig populations into susceptible, infected, treated, and recovered compartments, while the bird population is partitioned into susceptible and infected classes. Transmission occurs through standard-incidence forces of infection that explicitly represent cross-species pathways: birds transmit to pigs and humans, pigs transmit to humans and within their own population, and infected humans contribute to further human spread. Birds act as the primary reservoir in the model’s biological assumptions, pigs serve as intermediate hosts capable of passing infection to humans, and treatment reduces disease burden without conferring immediate immunity. A uniform fractional order is assumed across all compartments for analytical consistency. The authors establish that solutions remain nonnegative and bounded within a biologically feasible region, prove existence and uniqueness of solutions via a fixed-point argument, and derive the disease-free equilibrium where all infected and treated compartments vanish. Using the next-generation matrix method, the team computed the basic reproduction number as the maximum of three host-specific thresholds, one each for humans, pigs, and birds. Because the underlying matrix is upper triangular, its eigenvalues fall on the diagonal, yielding a closed-form expression in which transmission coefficients and contact modification parameters appear in the numerator and treatment, natural mortality, and disease-induced mortality rates in the denominator. Sensitivity analysis shows that transmission rates and contact modification parameters carry positive sensitivity indices of one, making them the dominant drivers of disease spread, while treatment and removal rates reduce transmission. The stability analysis follows the fractional-order criterion that an equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable only when every eigenvalue of the linearized system satisfies an argument condition tied to the fractional order. The disease-free equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable when the reproduction number is below one and unstable when it exceeds one, with a Lyapunov-based argument extending this to global stability within the feasible region. When the reproduction number surpasses one, the authors show that a unique endemic equilibrium exists and can be locally stable under the same eigenvalue condition. Numerical simulations using a predictor-corrector Adams-Bashforth-Moulton scheme with a time step of 0.1 reveal a striking role for memory. Across scenarios testing fractional orders of 0.8, 0.9, and the classical integer-order case of 1, decreasing the fractional order significantly delayed epidemic peaks and prolonged infection duration, with the strongest sensitivity appearing in the human and pig populations. The abstract reports that reducing the fractional order lowered peak infection levels by more than 40 percent in some scenarios, while the classical case exhibited faster epidemic growth and quicker decay. The bird population showed monotone decline across all orders, though with markedly slower decline for smaller fractional values. Applying Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle yields a Hamiltonian, an adjoint system, and explicit characterizations of the optimal controls in terms of costate variables and infection forces. The objective functional balances the epidemiological burden of infection in each host population against quadratic costs of implementing each control. The simulation results carry a consistent practical message. Under optimal control, infected human and pig populations rapidly decline to near-zero levels in both disease-free and endemic settings, whereas uncontrolled dynamics show pronounced epidemic peaks followed by slow decay. Susceptible human and pig populations are better preserved under control, treated compartments attain substantially lower peaks, and phase portraits show controlled trajectories contracting toward the disease-free equilibrium while uncontrolled trajectories exhibit large persistent excursions. The optimal controls are strongest at early times and decrease thereafter, a front-loaded structure the authors interpret as evidence that early aggressive intervention is most effective. The authors frame the work as filling a gap in which existing models either focused on single-host dynamics without memory effects or applied fractional-order models to single populations without cross-species spillover mechanisms. They caution that the model is general rather than fitted to specific outbreak data: parameter values are taken from existing literature on Nipah virus dynamics or chosen to be biologically reasonable where empirical estimates are unavailable, no empirical datasets were generated or analyzed, and future work is expected to incorporate spatial heterogeneity, stochastic effects, and data-driven parameter estimation. Within those limits, the study argues that memory effects and coordinated, early interventions targeting both human and animal populations jointly shape whether a zoonotic spillover event fades out or persists. Nipah virus infection is a 'zoonotic disease' that spreads from animals to humans. The animals that carry the virus in nature are large bats called flying foxes (genus Pteropus). Humans become infected through intermediate animals like pigs, or by consuming fruits or date palm sap contaminated with bat saliva or urine. With the note that it is 'rare,' human-to-human transmission through close contact with infected individuals has also been reported. Progression of Symptoms It begins with symptoms that are difficult to distinguish from a cold or influenza, such as fever, headache, muscle pain, vomiting, and sore throat. Subsequently, neurological symptoms such as dizziness, drowsiness, and impaired consciousness appear, and if it becomes severe, it leads to acute encephalitis. According to commentary in a medical journal, severe cases can progress to coma within 24 to 48 hours of the onset of encephalitis or seizures. Incubation Period While there is a slight difference in the lower limit between the FORTH article, which states 'an average of about 4 to 14 days,' and the medical journal, which states '3 to 14 days,' both note that 'there are reports of up to 45 days.' In other words, the troublesome aspect of this disease is that it may manifest some time after returning home. Classification in Japan In Japan, it is designated as a 'Category IV Infectious Disease' under the Infectious Diseases Control Law, and doctors who diagnose it are obligated to report it to the public health center immediately. However, there has never been a report of Nipah virus infection within Japan to date. It is reported that many flying foxes inhabit the affected areas. Kerala is a 'regular area' for Nipah Since the first outbreak in Kerala in 2018 (23 cases, 91% fatality rate), outbreaks have continued almost every year, including 2019, 2021, 2023, 2024, and 2025. Meanwhile, officials with the Office of the Health Minister said that in the wake of allegations about expired drugs being given to patients, a probe has become imperative. The minister's reply kicked up a political row in the state as the LDF government had often touted Nipah management as exemplary, which brought down the case fatality rate (CFR) from 91% to 33% in the recent outbreaks reported in 2023 and 2025. In his reply to a submission in the Kerala Assembly, the minister, quoting figures from Kozhikode Medical College, made serious statements about administering Remdesivir. According to his reply, KMSCL (Kerala Medical Services Corporation Limited) had last bought Remdesivir in June 2021, and these medicines had an expiry date of May 30, 2023. These medicines were used in 2023, 2024 and 2025 (102 vials in 2023, 34 vials in 2024 and 10 in 2025). The minister said that the committee will look into whether Nipah patients died because of using expired medicine and whether other patients complained of any side effects. Former health experts associated with Nipah management in Keralam as well as doctors who had taken Remdesivir as a prophylactic while treating Nipah patients said that the minister's statement is problematic in many ways. ADVERTISEMENT Remdesivir was procured from multiple agencies by the state government in 2021 to manage the second wave of COVID-19. "These batches had different expiry dates -- 2023 and 2025. Also, the KMSCL has a default system which flags expired medicines before it is supplied. It's not just patients, but several doctors had also used this medicine as a preventive, and it is highly improbable that none of them would check the expiry date," Dr Anish T S, former Nodal Officer for the Kerala One Health Centre for Nipah Research and Resilience, told Onmanorama. It's a broad-spectrum antiviral which has been considered a safe drug. Between 2023 and 2026, 6 Nipah patients were given this medicine and all of them survived. Officials said that the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) insists on an expiration date on a drug product, but does not explicitly mandate a manufacturing (MFG) date on the label. Male and female mice were exposed to cigarette smoke extract at a lower or higher dose for 12 weeks. Muraleedharan | Photo: Mathrubhumi Thiruvananthapuram: Health Minister K Muraleedharan on Friday stated that the Kerala government would take further action upon receiving a report from an expert committee investigating allegations that expired doses of Remdesivir were administered to Nipah patients in the state in previous years. The probe follows a submission by Congress MLA Mathew Kuzhalnadan in the State Assembly on Thursday. Replying to the submission, the minister noted that Remdesivir procured in 2021 carried an expiry date of May 2023, yet reports suggest it might have been administered to patients admitted to Kozhikode Medical College until 2025. Speaking to reporters, Muraleedharan clarified that the information presented to the Assembly was sourced from official reports provided by Health Department authorities, emphasizing that he would refrain from making further comments until the expert panel submits its findings. He assured that the government would investigate whether the deaths of Nipah patients were linked to the administration of expired medication, and whether other patients who received the drug suffered any adverse side effects. "There are several doubts regarding the medicine, including whether it was administered after its expiry date. We will investigate and take a decision," he said. The minister clarified that the investigation focuses strictly on whether expired doses were administered to patients, rather than any concerns regarding the inherent quality or efficacy of Remdesivir. Recalling the procurement of the antiviral during a prior Nipah outbreak after the UDF government assumed power, he noted that initial attempts to source the drug from various parts of the country failed because available stocks in Mumbai had already expired. Subsequently, the Kerala Medical Services Corporation Limited (KMSCL) informed the government that valid stock was available in Bahrain, after which it was airlifted within 48 hours. He highlighted that patients who received the medicine within its validity period recovered, reiterating that the issue centers on expiry management rather than the drug's therapeutic capability. Muraleedharan stated that an expert panel headed by Dr Aravind would conduct an impartial investigation without preconceived conclusions. The panel's mandate includes auditing how the drug was procured and distributed, examining the role of intermediaries, and reviewing the operational functioning of KMSCL. The government will also look into whether any private agents were involved in the procurement chain. Additionally, the Kozhikode Medical College superintendent informed the minister that while the Remdesivir packaging listed an expiry date, it omitted the manufacturing date—a standard requirement on pharmaceutical packaging. This discrepancy will also be investigated. The government is further reviewing reports that the medicine supplied to patients was labeled for clinical trial use, though an authoritative report on this aspect is awaited. "Further decisions on the issues would be taken after receiving the expert committee's findings," Muraleedharan said. He added that concerns regarding the involvement of foreign pharmaceutical firms in the state's procurement procedures would be thoroughly examined. Procurement records and mortality timeline Detailed procurement records reveal that KMSCL last acquired 3,000 vials of Remdesivir via its Karunya scheme on June 3, 2021, at a cost of ₹42 lakh, carrying an official expiry date of May 30, 2023. Data provided by Kozhikode Medical College Hospital indicates that 102 vials were utilised during 2023–24, 34 vials during 2024–25, 10 vials during 2025–26, and 50 vials during 2026–27. However, the 50 vials used during 2026–27 were freshly procured from Bahrain on June 12, 2026, specifically to treat a Nipah patient. These timelines have raised strong suspicions that the Remdesivir administered at the medical college throughout 2023, 2024, and 2025 had already passed its expiration date. Consequently, instructions have been issued to the Directorate of Medical Education (DME) and the Directorate of Health Services (DHS) to compile comprehensive records of all patients who received Remdesivir across state health facilities during these periods. The state recorded six Nipah cases in 2023, resulting in two fatalities. In 2024, two cases were reported, both of which were fatal. In 2025, two out of three reported Nipah patients succumbed to the infection. In 2026, one Nipah case was reported, and the patient successfully recovered. Nipah virus is a zoonotic pathogen capable of transmission between animals and humans. Fruit bats (flying foxes) serve as the primary natural reservoir for the virus, which can also infect swine and human populations. Hunting Invisible Viruses: Threats to Humans Lurking in the Animal Kingdom ResearchSpectrum JournalVU News Illustration by Marija Benetytė Viruses are the most abundant biological entities on Earth. Scientists estimate that more than 99% of them remain undescribed. Some of these invisible pathogens live in wild animals, and often neither cause serious illness nor pose a major threat to humans. Occasionally, however, viruses cross the species barrier and give rise to new diseases. One such example is the Nipah virus outbreak in Malaysia. The virus passed from fruit bats to pigs and subsequently infected humans, claiming more than 100 lives. To understand how such viruses emerge and evolve, Dr Milda Norkienė, a researcher at the Life Sciences Center of Vilnius University, and her colleagues are studying the diversity and evolution of polyomaviruses found in animals, as well as their potential threat to human health. Viruses that live among us Viruses are often associated with disease, but most of them are not actually dangerous to humans. ‘There is an enormous diversity of viruses in nature, and we encounter them constantly without even noticing. For example, when we eat plants, we also ingest plant viruses, while the bacteria living in our gut are infected by viruses of their own – bacteriophages. Yet this doesn’t mean that all of them are dangerous to us. Most are highly ‘selective’ and can reproduce only in a particular host,’ said VU researcher Dr Norkienė. The same is true of polyomaviruses. Many of us were probably infected with at least one of them in childhood without ever realising it, as the infection usually causes no symptoms. These viruses can remain in the body for many years and become active only under exceptional circumstances. Although the polyomavirus family is relatively small, research into these viruses has profoundly influenced biology and medicine. By analysing how they take over cellular machinery and force cells to copy viral genetic material, researchers have gained a better understanding of cell division, DNA replication, and even the development of certain tumours. This is why the search for new polyomaviruses continues to this day. ‘Polyomaviruses are found in a wide range of animals, from mammals to fish. Although few people have heard of them, scientists have already identified 16 polyomavirus species associated with humans, most of them only in the past two decades,’ explained Dr Norkienė, whose research focuses on these viruses. Most of these viruses are harmless to humans. However, when the immune system is weakened or a person is taking immunosuppressive medication, some polyomaviruses can reactivate and cause serious health problems. For instance, the BK virus is one of the polyomaviruses found in humans. Most people become infected with it in childhood, but the infection generally produces no symptoms. Following a kidney transplant, however, the virus can cause inflammation and damage the transplanted organ. The Merkel cell polyomavirus, meanwhile, is associated with a rare but highly aggressive form of skin cancer. Thus, it is important for researchers not only to discover new viruses but also to understand the circumstances under which they become dangerous. Discovering a new virus from a barely visible trace When we think of new virus discoveries, we tend to picture sophisticated laboratory equipment or major scientific breakthroughs. Yet the first step is often far less spectacular. It usually begins in the laboratory, with researchers analysing animal samples. The first indication of a potentially new virus may be nothing more than a barely visible DNA band on a gel. At first glance, such a band may seem unremarkable, but it can provide the first clue to a previously unknown virus. When examining animal samples, researchers look for genetic traces of viruses. Any positive result from an animal species that has not previously been studied immediately raises a question: is this a known virus, or something that no one has ever described before? This is where the real detective work begins. DNA gel electrophoresis. The first lane on the left contains a DNA size marker; the second shows a positive signal – a DNA fragment; while the third and fourth show negative results. Photo credit: doctoral researcher Monika Repšytė. ‘Is it really a virus? Could it be laboratory contamination? Does the virus actually live in this animal?’ These are just some of the questions that researchers must answer. They reconstruct the virus’s entire genetic sequence, compare it with data stored in international databases, and check whether the same virus has ever been detected before. A single finding is generally not enough: researchers need to establish that it is not merely a chance occurrence. That is why they need to detect a similar virus in more than one animal of the same species and, ideally, across different geographical locations. Even then, however, there are usually more questions than answers. One of the most important is whether the scientists have identified a genuinely new virus or simply one that had previously gone unnoticed. New or just newly discovered? News of a newly discovered virus often sounds alarming. However, ‘new’ does not necessarily mean ‘recently emerged’. More often, it means something quite different: researchers have detected and comprehensively described the virus for the first time. In fact, most viruses being discovered today are not new to nature – they are new only to science. Many of the polyomaviruses now being identified in animals have probably been around for thousands, if not millions, of years, but have simply never been studied before. Nevertheless, new discoveries sometimes raise more complex questions. If a virus causes unusually severe disease in its host, researchers begin to consider whether it has truly been adapted to that species for a long time. In such cases, they seek to answer one crucial question: could the virus have recently crossed over into a new host? A European hamster in its natural habitat Could a virus cross the species barrier? ‘Our immune system has evolved to cope with constant exposure to viruses, but sometimes the slightest vulnerability is all a virus needs. Some viruses become a threat when they cross the species barrier – that is, when they are transmitted from animals to humans. The diseases they cause are known as zoonoses. Others, including polyomaviruses, are often already present in our bodies and may lie dormant and undetected for years, until a weakened immune system gives them an opportunity to reactivate and cause disease,’ explained VU researcher Dr Norkienė. More than 70% of emerging infectious diseases in humans are zoonoses – diseases caused by pathogens transmitted from animals. This is why virologists ask not only ‘What virus have we found?’ but also ‘Could it adapt to a new host?’ How, then, can scientists test something that has not yet occurred in nature? To do so, they use pseudoviruses – safe surrogates that mimic real viruses. Their viral genetic material is replaced with a fluorescent marker, which means that they cannot replicate or cause disease, even though their outer structure resembles that of a real virus. If a pseudovirus enters a cell, researchers can detect it using a specialised microscope. In other words, they can observe the initial interactions between the virus and the cell and safely investigate whether the virus could theoretically take the first step towards infecting a new host. Of course, this does not necessarily mean that the virus will infect a new species in nature. Many factors influence whether this happens – from the host’s immune system to environmental conditions. Such experiments can nevertheless provide initial evidence for or against the hypothesis that a virus may be capable of adapting to a new host. The multifunctional Agno protein of a rodent polyomavirus is shown in green within the host cell; cell nuclei are stained blue and cell membranes red. Photo credit: Emilija Vasiliūnaitė. The specific antibodies used in this study were developed at the Department of Immunology of the Life Sciences Center, Vilnius University Experiments of this kind help scientists better understand how viruses recognise cells and which factors determine their ability to infect different hosts. Although this technology remains relatively uncommon worldwide, Vilnius University researchers are among those using polyomavirus pseudoviruses in their studies. In collaboration with Dr Christopher B. Buck, a virologist at the US National Cancer Institute, they have developed a rodent polyomavirus pseudovirus technology, enabling safe investigation of how viruses might adapt to new hosts. When even brewer’s yeast can recognise a viral protein Sometimes the most fascinating discoveries emerge where they are least expected: during one experiment, researchers found that the major capsid protein of a polyomavirus triggered a strong stress response even in brewer’s yeast. Brewer’s yeast is one of the simplest eukaryotic organisms, therefore, the finding was particularly surprising. As the yeast cells synthesised the polyomavirus protein, they began to cluster into clumps visible to the naked eye – a mechanism that helps them protect themselves against adverse conditions. Flocculating yeast. Such unexpected observations shed light on how viruses affect living cells and why some of them are so successful at adapting to very different organisms. Why look for these viruses now? The search for new viruses is driven by more than scientific curiosity. Climate change, human mobility, growing contact between animals and humans, changing ecosystems, and the increasing number of people with impaired immune systems are all creating new opportunities for viruses to spread. The more we know today about the viruses circulating in nature, the better prepared we will be to face tomorrow’s challenges. The Vilnius University polyomavirus research group, funded by the Research Council of Lithuania, has already identified several new polyomaviruses that infect animals, although none have been found to pose a threat to humans. Most viruses discovered today are unlikely ever to harm us. Yet this is precisely why it is important to study them before they ever have the chance to become a threat. Scientists do not know how many such viruses remain hidden in nature. However, each new discovery brings us closer to understanding the invisible world that surrounds us. Share FacebookLinkedInX

Govt has ordered a detailed probe into the incident.Replying to a submission by Mathew Kuzhalnadan, Muraleedharan said a medical board headed by Dr Aravind Reghukumar, head of infectious diseases at govt medical college hospital, Thiruvananthapuram, would investigate whether the use of expired remdesivir had contributed to Nipah deaths or caused adverse effects in other patients.He said the directorate of medical education (DME) and the directorate of health services (DHS) had been directed to collect details of patients who received remdesivir during the period. The drug, administered through intravenous infusion, was supplied to healthcare institutions by Kerala Medical Services Corporation Ltd (KMSCL) to meet emergency treatment requirements.The medicine was last procured on June 3, 2021, through KMSCL’s Karunya division.
