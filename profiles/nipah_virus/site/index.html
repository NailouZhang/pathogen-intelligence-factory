Structural Aspects of Nipah Virus Glycoproteins using Predictive Protein Modeling

Authors: Urmi Roy
Original abstract (English)
Nipah is a virus borne zoonotic disease that mostly occurs in Southeast Asian countries. Nipah virus (NiV) infection is potentially lethal as it can cause fatal encephalitis, and as of now, there are no vaccines or therapeutic treatments for this disease. The highly pathogenic NiV belongs to the Paramyxovirus family and the natural host of NiV is fruit bats of the Pteropodidae family. Though NiV has not been detected in the US, rather recently the Camp Hill virus (CHV) in shrew species was identified in Camp Hill, Alabama. This virus is characteristically similar to Nipah, and belongs to the henipavirus genus that include the Nipah and Hendra viruses. NiV has two surface glycoproteins namely the attachment glycoprotein (NiVG) and the fusion glycoprotein (NiVF). Being associated with host cell attachment and fusion of viral membrane, these glycoproteins tend to facilitate the viral entry to the host cell. The present paper describes the structures of the aforesaid viral glycoproteins based on the available database of protein structures and examines if they have an evolutionary link to other viral glycoproteins. Certain similar glycoproteins found in different countries are also explored. The structure-function relationship for these two NiV glycoproteins are determined using time-based molecular dynamics (MD) simulation. The interfacial ligand-receptor interactions between NiV proteins with human receptor and monoclonal antibody, Fab 66, are examined. A primary motivation for these structural studies is that the results might potentially contribute to future therapeutic developments and drug designs to effectively combat NiV.

Computational identification of multi-targeted bioactive phytocompounds as candidate drug molecules against Nipah virus infection

Authors: Ovi Sharma, Md. Feroj Ahmed, Dipu Sharma, Tasfia Noor, Md. Nurul Haque Mollah, Feroj Ahmed
Original abstract (English)
Nipah Virus (NiV) is a highly virulent zoonotic virus that leads to severe encephalitis and respiratory diseases with mortality rates estimated at 40–75%, sometimes up to 100% in specific outbreaks. NiV has outbreaks in South and Southeast Asia, particularly in Bangladesh, India, Philippines, Malaysia, Singapore, and so on. The WHO classifies NiV as a priority pathogen due to its high fatality rate, lack of approved antiviral drugs or vaccines, and pandemic potential spread by human-to-human transmission. Therefore, identifying novel therapeutic agents against NiV remains a critical global health priority now. Natural phytochemicals have gained increasing attention due to their diverse biological activities and, favorable safety profiles. In this study, we investigated 2271 unique phytocompounds as drug candidates induced from 261 medicinal plants having high antiviral activities through molecular docking interaction with the NiV G glycoprotein, F fusion protein, and L protein. Phytocompounds with significant binding affinity (< − 7.0 kcal/mol) were further assessed by, drug-likeness screening, ADME/T Profiling, network pharmacology, protein–ligand interaction analysis, density functional theory (DFT) calculations, and 100 ns MD simulation to investigate structural stability and interaction behavior. Docking results revealed strong binding affinity of several phytochemicals toward NiV proteins. Among which Absinthin, Cycloepiatalantin, and Diosgenin showed the most favorable interactions results. ADME/T analysis suggested high absorption, low predicted toxicity, and effective blood–brain barrier penetration. DFT analysis indicated a favorable electronic interaction potential of the phytochemicals. MD simulations suggested stable protein–ligand complexes supported by the following analysis, RMSD, RMSF, SASA, RoG, MM-PBSA, DCCM, and PCA. Overall, these findings suggest that these Phytocompounds may serve as potential candidates for further experimental validation as antiviral agents against NiV. Using large-scale Phytocompounds screening, this study identified candidate compounds with predicted bioactivity capable of interacting with NiV proteins, while helping to reduce the cost, time, and labor associated with conventional wet-laboratory experiments.

Original abstract (English)
The Hendra virus (HeV; Henipavirus hendraense) and Nipah virus (NiV; Henipavirus nipahense), members of the genus Henipavirus, are high-consequence pathogens that have caused multiple zoonotic outbreaks with high mortality. Frequently, pigs or horses have acted as bridging hosts between bats, the reservoir hosts, and people. We used pseudotyped viruses with the fusion (F) and glycoprotein (G) envelope proteins of the NiV and Ghana virus (GhV; Henipavirus ghanaense) in neutralization tests (PVNTs) to screen serum samples from 138 apparently healthy horses in northwest Nigeria. The tree had long been home to a colony of straw-coloured fruit bats, many of which were injured in the fall. Two children, aged 9 and 12, saw a business opportunity. The bats soon ended up at the roadside stall of a Yoruba traditional medicine ingredient trader who prefers to be called “Iya Kabirat.” For 50 years, she has sold assorted barks, herbs, animal carcasses, skins, and other materials used by herbalists for spiritual healing. She buys each bat from the children for 500 naira ($0.36), and sells it to customers for 700 naira ($0.50). “I was born in this business,” says Kabirat. “Many herbalists regularly come here to buy them. Across western Nigeria, bats are incorporated into traditional medicine for spiritual healing. The recipes entail charring their flesh and grinding it into a fine powder. The country is home to roughly 100 bat species, making up one-third of the 266 species found in sub-Saharan Africa. But these statistics are lost on locals who depend on bats for their meat. In neighbouring urban hubs such as Lagos, Calabar and Port Harcourt, bats are considered a delicacy and are sold openly by bushmeat vendors. In Ilorin, however, they are sought-after ingredients in traditional medicine. Often reservoirs for severe infectious diseases, bats can transmit pathogens to humans, raising concerns among researchers about the potential for zoonotic disease transmission. A 2023 study examined 304 straw-coloured fruit bats (Eidolon helvum) captured for human consumption in Makurdi, Nigeria. Researchers found antibodies indicating exposure to henipavirus lineage GH-M74a, Nipah virus, and bat influenza H17N10. Bat meat contains protein and essential nutrients such as iron and B vitamins, says Uju Onuorah, a registered dietitian-nutritionist and research associate at Nutrition Drive for Healthy Diet Initiative. “[But, they] are also known reservoirs for several viruses and pathogens that can be transmitted to humans, particularly during hunting, slaughtering, and preparation.” From hunting and handling to preparation, every step of the bat meat trade poses a risk of exposure to zoonotic diseases.
