经典机器学习与深度学习回归模型在尼帕病毒 基因组编码子序列分析中的比较研究

Comparative Analysis of Classical Machine Learning and Deep Learning Regression Models for Codon-Level k-mer Composition Analysis of the Nipah Virus Genome
作者： Asim Abid1, Muhammad Bilal1, Shafiq Ur Rehman Khan
摘要中文翻译摘要 尼帕病毒(尼帕病毒)是一种高度致病性的动物源性副粘病毒,与其他新兴病原体相比,其完整的基因组序列资源相对有限。本研究展示了从NCBI核苷酸数据库中检索到的93条完整的尼帕病毒 基因组序列的编码子(3-mer)水平的k-mer组成分析。基因组被编码为一个包含64个元素的媒介,每个元素代表64种三核苷酸组合的出现次数。对特征集进行了描述性统计分析,并计算了各特征之间的协方差和皮尔逊相关系数,结果显示该编码子计数特征集内部存在极高的成对共线性。通过基于相关性的特征选择过程(|r| > 0.99),将特征空间从64个减少到几乎独立的5个编码子计数(AAA、TAG、TCG、CGC和CCG)。我们考虑了七种回归算法,涵盖三种学习范式(经典模型:线性回归、K近邻、支持向量机/支持向量回归;集成模型:随机森林;深度序列模型:长短期记忆网络和简单循环神经网络;以及决策树基线模型),并以编码子CCG的数量作为回归目标,其余四个编码子计数作为预测变量进行比较。所有模型均采用70/20/10的训练/验证/测试划分方式进行训练,并通过决定系数(R2)、均方根误差(RMSE)和平均绝对误差(MAE)进行比较。决策树回归表现最佳(R2 = 0.9945),其次是线性回归(R2 = 0.9908)、随机森林(R2 = 0.9892)、LSTM(R2 = 0.9731)和SimpleRNN(R2 = 0.9718),而KNN(R2 = 0.8038)和SVM/SVR(R2 = 0.1038,经过特征缩放)表现较差。这些结果在考虑了编码子使用的大规模组成约束、基于实例和边界回归器对未缩放、共线性小样本数据的敏感性,以及深度序列架构在非常小的表格样本伪序列上的有限优势后进行了评估。本文以透明的方式展示了流程结果、统计结果和比较建模结果,同时列出了数据集的局限性,为未来基于编码子组成的尼帕病毒计算研究提供了基准。

Comparative Analysis of Classical Machine Learning and Deep Learning Regression Models for Codon-Level k-mer Composition Analysis of the Nipah Virus Genome

Authors: Asim Abid1, Muhammad Bilal1, Shafiq Ur Rehman Khan
Original abstract (English)
Abstract The Nipah virus (NiV) is highly virulent zoonotic paramyxovirus, where a complete genome sequence resource is relatively limited compared to other emerging pathogens. In this study, the codon level (3-mer) k-mer composition analysis of 93 complete Nipah virus genome sequences retrieved from the NCBI Nucleotide database was presented. The genomes were encoded into a vector of 64 elements representing the number of appearances of each of the 64 three-nucleotide combinations. The feature set was subjected to descriptive statistics, covariance and Pearson correlation between each feature, which showed very high degrees of pairwise collinearity in the set of codon-count features. Using a correlation-based feature-selection procedure (|r| > 0.99), the feature space was reduced from 64 to 5 almost independent codon counts (AAA, TAG, TCG, CGC and CCG). We considered seven regression algorithms across three learning paradigms (classical models: Linear Regression, K-Nearest Neighbors, Support Vector Machine/SVR, an ensemble model: Random Forest, deep sequential models: Long Short-Term Memory network and Simple Recurrent Neural Network, and a Decision Tree baseline) and compared their performance using the number of codons CCG as a regression target and the remaining four codon counts as predictors. The 70/20/10 train/validation/test split was used to train all models, and they were compared using the coefficient of determination (R2), root mean squared error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE). Decision Tree Regression achieved the best performance (R2 = 0.9945), followed by Linear Regression (R2 = 0.9908), Random Forest (R2 = 0.9892), LSTM (R2 = 0.9731), and SimpleRNN (R2 = 0.9718), while KNN (R2 = 0.8038) and SVM/SVR (R2 = 0.1038 with feature scaling) underperformed. The results are evaluated with the knowledge of the large compositional constraints on codon usage over a set of Nipah virus genomes, the known sensitivity of instance- and margin-based regressors to unscaled, collinear small-sample data, and the limited benefit of deep sequential architectures on a pseudo-sequence of a very small tabular sample (no temporal dimension). This paper presents in a transparent manner the pipeline results, statistical results and comparative modeling results, along with the limitations of the dataset, as a baseline for future codon-composition based computational studies of Nipah virus.

Geometrical study of DNA sequences of New World hantavirus and Nipah virus using the ATG genomic walk method

Authors: Guennadi Kouzaev
Original abstract (English)
Viral genome analysis is essential for characterizing genetic variation and evolutionary patterns, while complementary computational approaches may provide additional insights beyond conventional sequence-based methods. In this paper, the complete RNAs of New World hantavirus and Nipah virus are analyzed using a DNA walk method that traces the distribution of ATG triplets along sequences. It provides visual one-dimensional models (ATG trajectories) of genomes on a plane and quantitative estimates based on: (i) point-defined relative values of the divergence of ATG trajectories, (ii) relative global-defined standard deviation of ATG triplet numbers in a studied set of sequences, and (iii) relative global-defined standard deviation of fractal dimension values of inter-ATG distances in the same sequence set. These calculated parameters for the two species mentioned are compared with those of previously studied viruses, allowing them to be arranged in the following order based on their increasing sensitivity to variations in ATG distribution: Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2, Middle East Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus, Nipah, Ebola, and dengue viruses, which is in good accordance with their observed and recognized mutability. The method verified in this paper can be used as a supplementary pre-screening qualitative and quantitative tool for conventional sequencing analysis.

RT-qPCR和IgM/IgG 血清学在诊断中的时间融合:关键叙述性综述及可解释人工智能框架的提出

作者： Singh Aryan Joseph, Maria Agbo-Inedu Ihotu Owoicho, Ajit Pal Singh
摘要中文翻译尼帕病毒感染是一种在南亚和东南亚地区具有高后果性的人兽共患病,其实验室确诊依赖于两个检测方法家族,它们在疾病过程中提供相反方向的信息。逆转录定量聚合酶链反应(RT-qPCR)在病毒核酸仍能在血液和咽部检测到时表现最佳,而免疫球蛋白M和免疫球蛋白G的酶联免疫吸附试验(ELISA)则在核酸消失后变得具有信息量。目前的实践将这些检测视为在单一时间点获得的独立二元结果,忽略了定量数值、样本来源以及每个样本采集时的疾病天数。本综述批判性地评估了这两种检测方法家族的纵向结构是否可以联合利用,并探讨了在暴发条件下使用此类模型之前,可解释人工智能需要提供哪些要素。同时,评估了描述分子和血清学标志物时间行为的人类临床研究,以及在缺乏充分参考标准的情况下关于诊断准确性的方法学文献,以及关于时间与多模态融合和临床决策支持中解释方法的计算文献。证据一致表明,在尼帕病毒感染中存在一个有序的过渡过程,其中病毒核酸从血液中的清除先于病毒特异性免疫球蛋白G的出现,且在初次就诊时检测到病毒血症而免疫球蛋白G缺失的组合具有不良预后意义。然而,这些观察结果的信心仍受到样本量非常小、幸存者过度代表、缺乏国际核酸标准以及证据地理集中于孟加拉国和喀拉拉邦等因素的限制。未发现针对该感染的临床预测模型,该模型能够随时间融合分子和血清学结果。为此,提出了一种概念性的动态框架,该框架区分了已建立的时间关系和假设的收益,明确了适用的评估和治理要求,并确定了前瞻性连续采样队列研究作为证据的关键限制步骤,而非模型架构。

Temporal Fusion of RT-qPCR and IgM/IgG Serology for Nipah Virus Diagnosis: A Critical Narrative Review and a Proposed Explainable Artificial Intelligence Framework

Authors: Singh Aryan Joseph, Maria Agbo-Inedu Ihotu Owoicho, Ajit Pal Singh
Original abstract (English)
Nipah virus infection is a high-consequence zoonosis of South and South-East Asia for which laboratory confirmation rests on two assay families whose informativeness moves in opposite directions across the course of illness. Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction performs best while viral nucleic acid remains detectable in blood and the oropharynx, whereas enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays for immunoglobulin M and immunoglobulin G become informative as nucleic acid disappears. Prevailing practice interprets these assays as independent dichotomous results obtained at a single moment, discarding quantitative magnitude, specimen provenance and the day of illness on which each specimen was taken. This review critically evaluates whether the longitudinal structure of the two assay families can be exploited jointly, and what explainable artificial intelligence would need to deliver before such a model could be used in outbreak conditions. Human clinical studies describing the temporal behaviour of molecular and serological markers were appraised alongside the methodological literature on diagnostic accuracy without an adequate reference standard, and the computational literature on temporal and multimodal fusion and on explanation methods in clinical decision support. The evidence consistently indicates an ordered transition in which clearance of viral nucleic acid from blood precedes the appearance of virus-specific immunoglobulin G, and that the combination of detectable viraemia with absent immunoglobulin G at presentation carries adverse prognostic weight. Confidence in these observations is nevertheless constrained by very small samples, over-representation of the minority who survive, the absence of an international nucleic-acid standard, and geographical concentration of evidence in Bangladesh and Kerala. No clinical prediction model that fuses molecular and serological results over time was identified for this infection. A conceptual dynamic framework is developed that separates established temporal relationships from hypothesised gains, specifies the evaluation and governance requirements that would apply, and identifies prospective serial-sampling cohorts as the rate-limiting evidential step rather than model architecture.

The first Nipah outbreak was recorded in 1998 after the virus spread among pig farmers in Malaysia. The virus is named after the village where it was discovered. Outbreaks are rare but Nipah has been listed by the World Health Organization (WHO) -- alongside Ebola, Zika and Covid-19 -- as one of several diseases deserving of priority research for their potential to cause a global epidemic. Nipah usually spreads to humans from animals or through contaminated food, but it can also be transmitted directly between people. Fruit bats are the natural carriers of the virus and have been identified as the most likely cause of subsequent outbreaks. Symptoms include intense fever, vomiting and a respiratory infection, but severe cases can involve seizures and brain inflammation that results in a coma. There is no vaccine for Nipah. Patients have a mortality rate of between 40 and 75 percent depending on the public health response to the virus, the WHO says. What has happened during previous outbreaks? The first Nipah outbreak killed more than 100 people in Malaysia and prompted the culling of one million pigs in an effort to contain the virus. It also spread to Singapore, with 11 cases and one death among slaughterhouse workers who came into contact with pigs imported from Malaysia. Since then, the disease has mainly been recorded in Bangladesh and India, with both countries reporting their first outbreaks in 2001. Bangladesh has borne the brunt in recent years, with more than 100 people dying of Nipah since 2001. Two early outbreaks in India killed more than 50 people before they were brought under control. The southern state of Kerala has recorded two deaths from Nipah and four other confirmed cases since last month. Authorities there have closed some schools and instituted mass testing. This marks Kerala's fourth recorded spate of Nipah cases in five years. The virus killed 17 people during the first instance in 2018. The state has managed to stamp out previous outbreaks within a matter of weeks through widespread testing and strict isolation of those in contact with patients. Are animal-to-human viruses becoming more frequent? Having first appeared thousands of years ago, zoonoses -- diseases that can be transmitted from animals to humans -- have multiplied over the past 20 to 30 years. By occupying increasingly large areas of the planet, experts say, humans also contribute to disruption of the ecosystem and increase the likelihood of random virus mutations that are transmissible to humans. Industrial farming increases the risk of pathogens spreading between animals while deforestation heightens contact between wildlife, domestic animals and humans. By mixing more, species will transmit their viruses more, which will promote the emergence of new diseases potentially transmissible to humans. Climate change will push many animals to flee their ecosystems for more livable lands, a study published by the scientific journal Nature warned in 2022. Donate Home » New EU project aims to create first breathable treatment for Nipah virus Share this on News New EU project aims to create first breathable treatment for Nipah virus A new EU-funded project that aims to develop the world’s first breathable treatment for Nipah virus infection has officially launched this month (1 September 2026). 30/09/2026 Over the next six years, the Horizon-funded initiative, HENIPROTECT, will bring together 18 partners across eight countries to tackle one of the most concerning emerging infectious diseases. What is Nipah virus? Nipah virus is a highly dangerous virus carried by fruit bats, which can host the virus without becoming ill themselves. These bats are widespread across parts of South-East Asia and Australia. While the virus usually spreads from animals to humans, it can also pass between people. This raises important concerns about possible future pandemics and preparing for them. Outbreaks tend to occur in rural or farming areas. Symptoms include fever, headache, muscle pain, cough and difficulty breathing. The infection can be severe and lead to death in up to 75 out of every 100 people who catch it. The World Health Organization (WHO) has classified Nipah virus as a ‘priority infectious disease’ and included it in its ‘Blueprint list’. This list highlights infectious diseases that pose the greatest risk to global health because they have the potential to cause major outbreaks or epidemics, and we currently lack adequate vaccines, cures, or treatments to stop them. Currently, there are no licensed preventions or treatments available – but the HENIPROTECT project aims to change this. What are the aims of HENIPROTECT? Supporting the European Union’s priorities to strengthen health security and prepare for potential future pandemics, HENIPROTECT aims to develop a new antibody treatment for the Nipah virus delivered by inhalation. This means it is delivered straight to the lungs by breathing it in. Antibodies are proteins the body makes naturally to fight off infections. When a virus or bacteria enters the body, antibodies recognise it and stop it from causing harm. An antibody treatment provides ready-made antibodies instead of waiting for the body to make its own. What will the project do? The project will: Look at an antibody (called Nip35) that targets the Nipah virus and look at how it can be used in humans Combine two antibodies that work in different ways for better protection against the virus Test giving the antibody through breathing it in (inhalation) using mesh-nebulisation so it goes directly to the lungs where the virus infects Set up manufacturing to produce the treatment to regulatory standards (this is known as Good Manufacturing Practice or GMP) Run a human clinical trial in healthy volunteers to test safety Develop a toolbox to counter emerging variants of the virus. The European Lung Foundation (ELF) will lead public-facing activities in the project and will be responsible for translating the scientific results into lay language for patients, carers, and the general public through various communication platforms. The European Respiratory Society (ERS) will lead scientific dissemination and overall communication planning. Through Lungs Europe, ELF and ERS will lead advocacy efforts, working to ensure Nipah virus remains on the EU health agenda. Overall, the project will not only deliver a crucial treatment for Nipah virus, but also establish a valuable blueprint for responding rapidly to future respiratory viral threats, in particular to other viruses of the same Henipavirus genus, like highly pathogenic Hendra virus. He immediately thought of the original Nipah virus outbreak in Malaysia, where bats displaced by deforestation ended up in pig farms, and Argentine hemorrhagic fever, tied to pampas converted to corn. Dove's response: "they're seeing those things, but they're not seeing them as the majority." Racaniello added that Nipah also involved an El Niño event on top of forest clearance — "it's never one thing." The paper's most consequential conclusion is negative: the results "do not support the idea that one-size-fits-all ecological interventions would be broadly protective against epidemic and pandemic threats." Diseases transmitted by arthropods to humans respond more strongly to ecosystem drivers and drying; directly transmitted zoonoses like Ebola and MERS show little evidence of shared drivers at all — possibly because the data are sparse, possibly because their ecologies differ fundamentally. Racaniello offered a concrete illustration of how the diagnostic gaps compound. In a DRC Ebola outbreak, patients who reached a clinic tested negative for the specific ebolavirus a test was designed for, were sent home, and infected their families. John Dye, who has field experience with Bundibugyo virus, told him early Ebola and Lassa overlap so much in symptoms they cannot be distinguished clinically. In a Lassa-endemic area, a negative Ebola test sends the patient home. Mingarelli raised a data question the paper doesn't answer: the maps show Canada, Russia, North Africa, Spain, and Portugal essentially blank, despite known outbreaks in those regions. "It's probably either a reporting problem or they didn't include those data." Myotis bats: 139 species, one genus, a 42-year lifespan in a body that fits in your hand The second paper, published in the same journal on 26 August 2026, turns from outbreak maps to bat genomes. Myotis is the largest bat genus — 139 species across six continents, emerging 33 million years ago. Within the genus, lifespan ranges sixfold: Myotis brandtii reaches 42 years, while the shortest-lived species makes it to seven. Those extremes diverged only 10.6 million years ago. "That is very, very abnormal," Mingarelli said. Bats break the standard rule that lifespan scales with body size. "You've got these little bats flying around living for 42 years. That doesn't fit the curve." The team, with co-first authors Juan Vazquez and Elise Lauterbur and co-corresponding authors including UC Berkeley's Peter Sudmant and David Enard, produced haplotype-resolved, chromosome-scale genome assemblies for eight Myotis species. Mingarelli said she had never seen a bat paper attempt haplotype-resolved assembly before, and called the results "some of the most contiguous mammalian assemblies" at nearly 99% complete, with 20,869 protein-coding genes. All eight species carry 44 chromosomes — remarkable across 33 million years of divergence — yet harbor six to eight thousand structural variants per genome relative to the group. PKR: the antiviral protein that bats duplicated — and learned to live with The functional centerpiece is PKR, or protein kinase R. It's an interferon-stimulated gene that senses double-stranded RNA produced during virus infection and shuts down protein synthesis. Racaniello explained why every virus on Earth cares about it: every virus genome carries some antagonist to PKR "because if you don't, you're out of business." Myotis species carry one, two, or three copies. PKR2 is the ancestral copy; PKR1 originated from a single duplication at the root of the genus; a third copy appears only in Myotis californicus. The duplicated and unduplicated haplotypes have been segregating for tens of millions of years. The team's functional work knocked out PKR in HeLa cells, then reintroduced one, two, or three bat PKR variants. The results were precise: PKR1 and PKR2 together produce an intermediate translation shutdown, with no synergy and no inhibition. PKR1 forms homodimers, but PKR1 plus PKR2 does not form heterodimers — the protein-protein interface has diverged. Both together restrict vesicular stomatitis virus and Sindbis virus to a similar extent as single copies. The effects are additive, not synergistic. Then the tradeoff: at high doses, PKR is toxic to cells. The authors suggest this toxicity may explain why duplication is rare in other mammals. Bats have apparently resolved it. How, exactly, remains unidentified. The genomic surprise: DNA viruses drive sequence change, RNA viruses drive copy number The paper's most striking finding came from a reference set of 5,527 manually curated virus-interacting proteins, or VIPs — host proteins with at least one experimentally verified physical interaction with a viral protein, RNA, or DNA. In Myotis bats, VIPs are enriched for positive selection. The enrichment strengthens when restricted to VIPs with experimental evidence of pro- or antiviral fitness effects. The pattern holds across 47 publicly available non-Myotis bat genomes. But split by virus type, the result is counterintuitive. Bat VIP adaptation is driven by selection on DNA-virus-interacting proteins — with no genome-wide enrichment for RNA-virus VIPs. In humans, the pattern is reversed: adaptation is driven by RNA VIPs. Other mammalian clades show a mix. Racaniello called the bat result "a big surprise." Mingarelli offered a hypothesis: Myotis are not reservoirs of the RNA viruses people associate with bats, so perhaps they carry more DNA viruses — "but we don't know." Dove distilled the two strategies into a memorable frame: "Maybe when you're adapting to DNA viruses, you need to change the protein and when you're adapting to RNA viruses, you just need more." The copy-number analysis confirmed the complementary pattern. VIP genes were more likely to have undergone expansions or contractions on at least one Myotis branch — and this was driven only by copy-number changes in RNA-virus VIPs. Bats adapt to DNA viruses through sequence changes and to RNA viruses through copy-number changes. Humans do the opposite. "Other mammalian orders show a mix of adaptation in both RNA and DNA VIPs," Dove said. "None show the absence of genomewide enrichment in RNA VIPs that they've just found in the myotis." Cancer resistance emerges as a byproduct The longevity analysis modeled body size and lifespan evolution across a supertree of a thousand mammals. Bats show some of the largest and most rapid lifespan changes among mammals, largely independent of body size. Bats experience a 40% greater increase in lifespan per 1% increase in body size than non-bat mammals. Myotis showed some of the fastest lifespan increases relative to their most recent ancestor. The cancer analysis found that 4%, 5.4%, and 20% of protein-coding genes — depending on the measure — have at least one region under positive or negative selection in cancer-related processes, enriched for immunity, cancer, and aging pathways. Among positively selected genes, most Nearctic Myotis lineages were enriched for cancer hallmark pathways, and the lineages with the greatest lifespan showed the strongest enrichment. Myotis lucifugus, the longest-lived, showed over-representation of DNA double-strand break repair pathways. The functional test applied neocarzinostatin, a radiomimetic agent that induces double-strand breaks, to fibroblast lines from four bat species. At low doses, M. lucifugus was the only species showing sensitivity after 24 hours — its cells died. At high doses, it showed the highest apoptosis and greatest viability drop of all bats tested. This matches the pattern seen in elephants, naked mole rats, and bowhead whales: longevity associated with increased clearance of damaged cells rather than repair. Mingarelli wished the study had included human or mouse cells in parallel: "it would be really nice to see compared to another mammal because I think it would be even more striking." The RNA-seq results filled in the mechanism. After 6 hours of neocarzinostatin treatment, cells upregulated cell cycle arrest and cell death genes while downregulating division, growth, and DNA repair pathways. At 18 hours, cell cycle downregulation continued and stress response genes came up. The strongest pathway enrichment in DNA-damaged cells was the innate immune response — and those differentially expressed genes were enriched among DNA-virus VIPs specifically. The authors' interpretation ties it all together: selection on the innate immune response may drive antagonistic pleiotropy in aging-associated traits. The antiviral adaptations that let bats tolerate viruses also gave them longevity and cancer resistance. Racaniello quoted the paper's conclusion: "Traits such as cancer risk, cellular homeostasis, and antiviral response have evolved in tandem due to pleiotropic selection at coinciding points in the evolutionary history of bats." Dove summarized the hypothesis: "bats have been assaulted with viruses. They've evolved. Evolution has provided them with strong innate immune systems. And at the same time, those also gave them longevity and anti-cancer properties." The paper frames the connection as a single chain, from viral pressure through immune adaptation to its two byproducts: Peto's paradox and the elephant model Mingarelli explained Peto's paradox: within species, cancer risk scales with body size and lifespan, but across species there's no correlation. Elephants carry 20 copies of TP53 — 40 alleles — versus one copy in humans. In vitro, elephant cells exposed to DNA damage undergo apoptosis significantly faster than human or other mammalian cells: they clear the cell rather than attempt repair. The energetic cost of that strategy in an elephant-sized body is enormous. Mingarelli questioned whether making a new cell is really cheaper than repairing one. Whales and naked mole rats use similar strategies. Mingarelli's central caveat runs through both discussions: "bat is not equal to bat" — a phrase her lab uses constantly. The eight species assembled in this study are all North American; the Old World bats — Rousettus, Hipposideros, Rhinolophus — are the primary reservoirs for the most lethal human pathogens, including filoviruses and beta-coronaviruses. What the Myotis findings mean for bat immunology broadly is unresolved. The species carrying the viruses that most worry humanity are the least studied. Dove added a caution of his own on cancer: "to be clear, death bats almost certainly do get cancer. We just haven't seen it yet. ROME, Italy – Dr. Saowapak Hinjoy, President of the PREZODE General Assembly, presented Thailand’s experience at the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO). She advocated for integrating human, animal, and environmental data to support joint risk assessments and coordinated action plans to prevent infectious diseases at their source. At the same conference, FAO recognized Thailand’s “One Health for Safe Pork” initiative as a Good Practice and Innovation in One Health in Agrifood Systems. Since her election as President of the PREZODE General Assembly in December 2025, Dr. Saowapak, Senior Veterinary Expert at the Department of Disease Control of the Ministry of Public Health, has linked global collaboration with practical implementation in Thailand to prevent zoonotic and emerging infectious diseases through the One Health approach. Most recently, Dr. Saowapak served as a speaker and panelist at the FAO Global Conference for Actions on One Health in Agrifood Systems, held from 21 to 23 September 2026 at FAO Headquarters in Rome, Italy. During the session “Beyond Data: The One Health Decision Challenge in a Complex World,” she emphasized that sharing data alone is insufficient. Turning data into action requires all sectors to develop a shared understanding, set common priorities, and establish a joint action plan. Collaboration among PREZODE, PREACTS, and ASAMCO demonstrates a practical pathway from global frameworks to local action. PREZODE sets the overall direction for prevention, PREACTS translates the framework into implementation, and ASAMCO applies and tests the approach in local settings. During the “From Science to Action” session, Dr. Saowapak presented a case study from Nan Province in northern Thailand, where the risk of Streptococcus suis was assessed across the entire pork value chain, including farms, slaughterhouses, markets, food preparation, and consumers. The team translated the findings into community-level interventions and policies to improve slaughterhouse standards. The approach emphasized practical, safer alternatives and engaged young people to communicate health information through short, accessible videos for the public. Dr. Saowapak also contributed to discussions on strengthening the wildlife and environmental health workforce. She highlighted surveillance for Nipah virus, Plasmodium knowlesi, and Herpes B virus as examples of how wildlife and environmental data can help identify health risks before human cases occur. She proposed stronger links between FTP-WEBE, the public health Field Epidemiology Training Program (FETP), and FETPV for the animal health sector. During the conference, Thailand’s Department of Disease Control, the Department of Disease Control Foundation, and ASAMCO/PREZODE partners received FAO recognition for Good Practices and Innovations in One Health in Agrifood Systems for the “One Health for Safe Pork: A Community-Based Innovation to Prevent Streptococcus suis in a Hmong Village in Northern Thailand.” The initiative was implemented in Ban Nam Tuang, Mae Charim District, Nan Province. This achievement resulted from collaboration among public health and livestock authorities, hospitals, schools, local government organizations, community leaders, young people, and academic institutions. Representing the team, Dr. Saowapak received the FAO Certificate of Recognition on September 22, 2026. This recognition shows how One Health can begin with a real community health challenge and develop into practical, effective prevention measures by combining scientific evidence, cultural understanding, and meaningful local participation.
