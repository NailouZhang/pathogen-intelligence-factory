Avian cholera causes mass mortality at penguin mega-colonies amid H5N1 HPAI outbreaks in Antarctica

Authors: Matteo Iervolino, Anne Günther, Leonard Schuele, Florencia Soto, Lonneke M. E. Leijten, Theo M. Bestebroer, Adam Coerper, Alice Reade, Ben Wallis, Simeon Lisovski
Original abstract (English)
Abstract The characterization of pathogens affecting Antarctic wildlife has been historically limited and sporadic. With the introduction of H5N1 high pathogenicity avian influenza virus (HPAIV) in Antarctica in the austral summer 2023/2024, concerns about the health of Antarctic seabirds and mammals prompted immediate surveillance efforts. In March 2024, we visited the Danger Islands archipelago, that hosts two mega-colonies of Adélie penguins, and observed an unusual mortality estimated in thousands of Adélie penguins and other species. Swabs and tissues were collected for molecular detection of infectious agents from 49 carcasses, and additional tissues for histology from a selection of 9 carcasses. We detected Pasteurella multocida DNA in 46 of 49 individuals, and diagnosed avian cholera as the cause of death of most of these animals. By shotgun sequencing, we retrieved the first genomic sequences of the P. multocida strain from this region, and the phylogenetic analysis showed a close relation with strains from other locations retrieved during the same expedition and with strains previously reported in the Southern Ocean area. This study confirms avian cholera as a relevant cause of mortality in the Antarctic, and overall highlights the importance of considering this disease in the differential diagnoses during mortality events in Antarctica.

Surveillance of zoonotic avian influenza viruses H5N1 and H9N2 in selected live bird markets in Northern Nigeria: public health implications

Authors: Olawuyi Abraham Kayode, Uwem Okon Edet, Orole Olugbenga Olukayode, Mkpuma Nicodemus Nnabuike, Junaid Suraj, Olawuyi Grace Foluke, Olabode Mayowa Peter, Meseko Clement Adebajo, Kayode Abraham Olawuyi
Original abstract (English)
Avian influenza viruses (AIVs), including highly pathogenic H5N1 and low pathogenic H9N2, represent significant zoonotic threats. Both subtypes have been repeatedly introduced into Nigeria since their initial detection in West Africa in 2006 and 2017, respectively. This study aimed to investigate the current genetic diversity and spillover potential of these viruses through active surveillance in live bird markets (LBMs) in Hadejia and Kano, Nigeria, from March to November 2022. We sampled 219 birds, collecting two swabs per bird (438 swab specimens). A positive bird was defined as having at least one positive swab. At the bird level, highly pathogenic H5N1 was detected in 3/125 (2.4%) apparently healthy guinea fowl at Sabon Gari LBM in Kano. Notably, low pathogenic H9N2 was detected at a substantially higher prevalence, 10/94 (10.6%) in chickens at Hadejia LBM. Overall, 13/219 (5.9%) birds were positive for either H5N1 or H9N2. Phylogenetic and selection pressure analysis confirmed that the H9N2 viruses belong to the G1 lineage and harbor key mammalian adaptation signatures in the hemagglutinin (HA) gene, including S143T, S145T, and S121T, which are associated with increased affinity for human-like α2–6 sialic acid receptors. Phylogenetic analysis placed the H5N1 strain within clade 2.3.4.4b, showing close genetic relationships with viruses responsible for the 2021–2022 Nigerian epidemic and subsequent outbreaks in Niger in early 2023. H5N1 and H9N2 were detected in different Nigerian live bird markets. Although these findings do not provide evidence of current co-circulation or reassortment, the detection of both subtypes within the broader LBM system, together with putative mammalian adaptation markers in H9N2, highlights a potential reassortment risk should these viruses later co-circulate within the same market and co-infect the same host. Continued genomic surveillance is therefore warranted to detect future viral convergence and emergent reassortant strains and to inform pandemic preparedness and control strategies.

A qualitative study exploring poultry farmers’ barriers and motivations to seasonal human influenza vaccine uptake as a preventive public health measure because of avian influenza

Authors: Berg1 EJvd, E. Warmelink1, Loenen2 Tv, K.V.C. Wevers-de Boer1, E. J. van den Berg1, T. van Loenen2
Original abstract (English)
Abstract Background: The global spread of highly pathogenic avian influenza (H5N1) has increased concerns about reassortment between avian and human influenza viruses and the potential emergence of novel pandemic influenza strains. Annual seasonal influenza vaccination can reduce this risk and is recommended by the Dutch government and the World Health Organization (WHO) for risk groups, including poultry farmers. This study explored Dutch poultry farmers’ perceptions regarding this vaccination and identified motivations and barriers influencing their willingness to be vaccinated. Methods: This qualitative study involved semistructured interviews with twelve poultry farmers in the Netherlands. Interviews were conducted online via Microsoft Teams between June and October 2024. The interview guide was based on the attitude‒social influence‒self-efficacy (ASE) model. Interviews were transcribed verbatim and analysed using thematic analysis. Results: Attitudes towards vaccination differed both generally and regarding seasonal influenza vaccination due to avian influenza risk. Approximately half considered vaccination a reasonable preventive measure. The main motivation for vaccination was preventing reassortment between avian and human influenza viruses and thereby reducing the risk of a future pandemic. Participants also viewed vaccination as a sign of societal responsibility and a way to contribute positively to the public image of the poultry sector. Important barriers included diminished trust in the government, perceived regulatory burden within the poultry sector, and perceived limited benefits of vaccination. Participants emphasized that vaccination should remain voluntary. Participants generally perceived their personal risk of avian influenza infection as low, while acknowledging potentially severe consequences for public health if a novel influenza virus were to emerge. Trusted sources of information included veterinarians, general practitioners, poultry advisers, and agricultural organizations. Accessibility was considered essential: local, flexible in timing, and free of charge. Conclusions: Acceptance of seasonal influenza vaccination among poultry farmers is influenced by a combination of societal responsibility, perceived risk, trust in the government, and practical considerations. Vaccination strategies should emphasize the societal benefits of vaccination and the potentially serious public health consequences of the emergence of a novel influenza virus. Improving vaccine uptake among poultry farmers requires engagement of trusted intermediaries and vaccination programs that are accessible and tailored to farmers’ practical needs.

Avian influenza A virus polymerase gene leads to temperature-resistant genome replication

Authors: Stephanie L. Williams, Sebastian M. Gygli, Jacob C. Bjorgen, Haitian Fan, Munashe Mudiwa, Lex Matthews, Yong-Li Xiao, Ashley Freeman, Sharon F. Legaspi, Li Qi
Original abstract (English)
ABSTRACT Influenza A viruses (IAV) pose a persistent pandemic threat. Successful cross-species transmission from waterfowl to mammals requires host adaptation for efficient replication and spread. Avian IAVs are adapted to the temperature of the avian gut, approximately 41°C, while human IAVs replicate at 33°C in the upper respiratory tract. PB1, the catalytic core of the viral heterotrimeric RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp), is essential for viral RNA synthesis. Three pandemic IAV strains (1918, 1957, and 1968) carried an avian-origin PB1 gene, suggesting a selective advantage. Fever, a critical antiviral response, elevates body temperature to 38°C or higher and can restrict viral replication. We hypothesized that avian PB1 may allow IAV replication at febrile temperatures. Using a mini-replicon system, we demonstrate that a polymerase of a seasonal human H1N1 IAV fails to replicate viral RNA efficiently at 40°C, whereas substitution of the PB1 with that from the 1918, 1957, and 1968 pandemic viruses, or a low-pathogenic avian H2N1 virus, restores RNA synthesis. Chimeric seasonal human H1N1 viruses expressing avian or 1918 PB1 replicate at 40°C in MDCK cells, unlike wild-type H1N1. Activity assays with purified chimeric IAV polymerases further suggest that avian-origin PB1 may intrinsically enhance polymerase activity at elevated temperatures. Together, these data show that avian-origin PB1 enables IAV replication at febrile temperatures, highlighting its role in zoonotic emergence and host adaptation. Surveillance of avian-origin PB1 genes in IAVs circulating in mammals may help identify strains with pandemic potential and inform the development of countermeasures. IMPORTANCE Influenza A viruses of avian origin remain a persistent threat to human health. The 1918, 1957, and 1968 pandemic influenza viruses each acquired an avian-origin PB1 gene, but the adaptive advantage conferred by these genes remains incompletely understood. Here, we show that avian-origin PB1 genes enhance viral RNA replication by a seasonal human influenza virus polymerase at elevated temperature and enable replication of a human influenza virus at 40°C, a temperature relevant to both avian hosts and human febrile illness. PB1-dependent effects were observed in cell-based polymerase assays, with purified polymerase, and during authentic virus infection. These findings identify temperature-resistant genome replication as a potential adaptive property of avian-origin PB1 and suggest that acquisition of an avian PB1 may help influenza viruses overcome temperature barriers encountered during adaptation to mammalian hosts.

Biosecurity risk factors associated with outbreaks of high pathogenicity avian influenza (HPAI) on poultry farms in Lower Saxony (Germany) – A case-control-study

Authors: J. Held, C. Sauter-Louis, U. Gerdes, J. Schulz, K. Schulz
Original abstract (English)
Biosecurity is an essential component when considering risk factors for high pathogenicity avian influenza (HPAI) and may be an explanation for the phenomenon that not all poultry farms are equally affected by outbreaks - despite similar conditions in terms of location, surroundings and size as well as type of poultry and production. In a case-control study, the association of biosecurity measures with the risk of outbreaks of HPAI in poultry farms in Lower Saxony, Germany, was investigated. Original abstract (English)
Oseltamivir and zanamivir are the primary antivirals against H5N1 influenza, yet resistance profiles at the structural level remain incompletely characterized. Phenotypic screening requires one to two weeks in biosafety level 3 containment; during an active spillover event, this timescale is incompatible with clinical decisions. Original abstract (English)
Avian influenza is an important zoonotic disease of global concern, with poultry workers at particular risk because of their close and frequent contact with birds and contaminated environments. This study assessed the level of knowledge regarding avian influenza among poultry workers in selected poultry farms of Sangli and Miraj city, with a view to developing a self‐instructional module on its prevention. A descriptive cross‐sectional design was used, and 60 poultry workers were selected by simple random sampling from identified farms in the study area. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire consisting of demographic variables and 16 multiple‐choice items covering transmission, clinical manifestations in humans and birds, diagnosis, vaccination, biosecurity measures, safe cooking practices, and use of personal protective equipment. Knowledge scores were categorized as bad, average, good, or excellent. The findings showed that 70% of poultry workers had average knowledge and 30% had good knowledge; none had bad or excellent knowledge. More than half of the workers reported no prior knowledge of avian influenza, and among those who had heard about it, the main sources were social media, newspapers, television, and health workers. Important gaps were observed in the understanding of human‐to‐human transmission, recommended personal protective equipment, appropriate cooking practices, reporting of sick or dead birds, and farm‐level biosecurity. Chi‐square analysis revealed no statistically significant association between knowledge level and selected demographic variables such as age, education, type of work, work experience, and previous knowledge. In response to the identified deficiencies, a self‐instructional module was developed focusing on essential concepts and practical preventive measures. The study highlights the need for simple, locally appropriate educational interventions to strengthen knowledge and promote safer practices among poultry workers, thereby reducing the risk of avian influenza transmission at the human–poultry interface.

Authors: Clara Delecroix, Claire Guinat, Lisa Fourtune, Mathilde C. Paul, Timothee Vergne
Original abstract (English)
Animal infectious diseases threaten food security, livestock production and public health. While disease prevention strategies typically target risk factors associated with high-competence hosts or environmental conditions, we hypothesise that lower-competence species can reduce disease risk by diluting effective contact rates between farms. Using spatial statistical models calibrated on three successive French high pathogenicity avian influenza (HPAI) epidemics (2020-2023), we show that greater local diversity of livestock farms, including cattle and pig farms, is associated with significantly lower HPAI risk. Moreover, we show that the positive association between duck houses and HPAI risk weakens as the number of less-competent non-duck poultry houses increases, revealing a dilution effect within poultry production systems. These findings suggest that livestock diversity is a previously overlooked determinant of disease spread and open new research avenues on territorial diversification to complement traditional approaches for the long-term control of high-impact animal infectious diseases.

Original abstract (English)
Avian influenza is a serious global public health threat, and both humans and animals can be infected. Classical integer-order compartmental models are generally not capable of capturing the complex temporal dynamics of epidemic systems, especially memory-dependent phenomena such as delayed immune response and environmental virus persistence. In this review, we have surveyed the use of fractional calculus in the modeling of avian influenza epidemics with emphasis on disease transmission, memory effects, and intervention strategies. In detail, we consider four fractional operators, namely, the Caputo, Caputo-Fabrizio, Atangana-Baleanu, and fractal-fractional operators, and compare the structures of their kernels and their memory properties. We review progress in data-driven modeling, including calibration to real epidemic surveillance data, seasonal transmission patterns, and fractional optimal control formulations in a One Health framework. Based on the review of 16 studies combining fractional calculus with avian influenza or influenza transmission modeling, consistent evidence is provided that fractional-order models reproduce the real outbreak dynamics better than the classical ones. As far as surveillance data is concerned, the Atangana-Baleanu-Caputo operator yields the fewest prediction errors. Key research gaps are identified, and future directions are proposed, including hybrid fractional-machine learning frameworks for real-time adaptive forecasting.

13:45 CET Virtual meeting Reading time: 2 min (532 words) Wednesday, 14 October 2026, 12:30 –13:45 (CEST) Background Avian influenza A(H9N2) viruses continue to circulate widely in birds and have caused sporadic human infections, highlighting the ongoing risk of influenza viruses emerging at the animal-human interface. Addressing these risks requires timely risk assessment, coordinated One Health action, across human, animal and environmental health sectors, and sustained preparedness for pandemics through GISRS and its partners. Held shortly after the WHO biannual consultation on influenza vaccine composition, including the review of candidate vaccine viruses for pandemic preparedness, this webinar will provide the latest updates on A(H9N2) epidemiology, virus characterization, animal and environmental surveillance, and global risk assessment. By bringing together experts from human, animal, and environmental health sectors, the webinar will promote a shared understanding of current risks and strengthen coordinated preparedness for future zoonotic and pandemic influenza threats. Objectives The webinar will provide a One Health update and support awareness on avian influenza A(H9N2) and provides an opportunity for engagement around the emerging Strategic Framework to accelerate Quadripartite action on avian influenza. Specific objectives are to: Summarize the current epidemiological situation of avian influenza A(H9N2) at the human–animal interface, including recent human infections and the geographical scope and impact of its spread in bird populations; Describe the virological characteristics of circulating A(H9N2) viruses and those detected in sporadic human cases, including antigenic and genetic diversity, markers of mammalian adaptation, and implications for risk assessment, diagnostics and candidate vaccine virus development; Highlight the roles of WHO, FAO, WOAH and UNEP in surveillance, risk assessment, prevention and control of avian influenza, including wildlife, poultry production, ecosystem and public health considerations; and Show how data and expertise from GISRS WHO Collaborating Centres and OFFLU contribute to global monitoring, virus characterization, risk assessment and preparedness for zoonotic and pandemic influenza threats.
