Genomic detection of highly pathogenic avian influenza H5N1 in Antarctic seabirds reveals connectivity with South American viral lineages.

Authors: Francine C. B. Timm, Raíssa Gasparetto, Miguel L. Côrrea, Alanis Silva Melgarejo, Bruna S. Paredes-Galarza, Nicole Vieira Stone, Eduardo J. Pizarro, Gaspar Mejías, Carolina P. Lucca, Isabella Grazini-Capelin
Original abstract (English)
Abstract Emerging avian viruses increasingly threaten Antarctic wildlife, raising concerns about ecosystem health and biodiversity. In this study, we conducted a comprehensive investigation of avian influenza virus (influenza A virus, IAV) in both resident and migratory birds inhabiting the South Shetland Islands, Antarctica. During the 2024–2025 austral summer, 278 samples were collected and screened using real-time RT-PCR targeting the IAV M gene. IAV RNA was detected in 30 samples, and eight of these were found to be positive for H5. Complete genome sequencing was performed on samples from a gentoo penguin ( Pygoscelis papua ) and a southern giant petrel ( Macronectes giganteus ), revealing the presence of highly pathogenic avian influenza virus H5N1, clade 2.3.4.4b. Phylogenetic analysis demonstrated that these viral genomes closely cluster with contemporary South American strains, indicating a direct connectivity between Antarctic seabirds and the broader H5N1 transmission network. Our findings highlight the heightened vulnerability of Antarctic ecosystems to emerging infectious diseases and emphasize the critical need for sustained genomic surveillance. These efforts are essential to monitor wildlife health, inform conservation strategies, and implement effective biosecurity measures to safeguard Antarctic biodiversity.

In silico evaluation of selected plant-based compounds as potent dual target inhibitors against highly pathogenic avian influenza H5N1 virus.

Authors: S. Ari, M. Das, S. V. Krishna, P. Bandyopadhyay, S. Debnath, Anindya Sundar Panja
Original abstract (English)
Influenza viruses remain a serious global health issue due to their high morbidity and mortality rates. Among them, avian influenza H5N1 virus is highly pathogenic, causing up to 100% mortality in poultry. As the avian influenza H5N1 virus continues to mutate across different regions, understanding multiple viral proteins, particularly the mutated haemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA), is crucial for effective control.2. In this study, dual-target inhibitors capable of neutralising both HA and NA were developed, and a comprehensive in silico analysis was performed on different variants collected over the past decade. Structural modelling revealed conserved domains in NA (A0A1I9QM14, China) and HA (A0A0A0V4U2, Bali), which were selected for ligand docking.3. Twenty active compounds, traditionally used as natural feed additives in broiler chicken diets, were screened using protein-ligand docking, ADME profiling, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Quercetin and luteolin emerged as promising dual-target inhibitors, showing high binding affinity, drug-likeness, and favourable toxicity profiles. The binding free energy (ΔG = -8 ± 0.2 kcal/mol) from docking studies confirmed their inhibitory potential against avian influenza H5N1 virus HA and NA.4. The results demonstrated that virtual screening of natural compounds can effectively identify those effective against influenza virus surface proteins. This integrative computational-experimental strategy supported the development of novel plant-derived antiviral agents with potential applications for disease control.

Enhanced induction of inflammatory cytokines in human lung cells infected with highly pathogenic recent H5N1/2024 avian influenza virus.

Authors: Heejeong Seo, Abdul Rauf, Sang Heui Seo
Original abstract (English)
Highly pathogenic (HP) H5N1 avian influenza viruses are zoonotic pathogens and are endemic in wild birds and poultry around the world. Pro-inflammatory cytokines are supposed to contribute to the severe clinical symptoms in infected animals and humans. In this study, we determined the infectivity and cytokine induction in primary human lung cells infected with HP H5N1 avian influenza viruses and seasonal H3N2 influenza viruses. MDCK cells were used to measure viral titers in the supernatants of infected human lung cells by log10TCID50/ml. Quantitative real-time PCR was used to detect the induction of cytokine genes using mRNA from the infected primary human lung cells. HP H5N1 avian influenza viruses induced higher viral titers than seasonal H3N2 influenza viruses in supernatants of primary human lung cells. In addition, HP H5N1avian influenza viruses induced the genes of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-23, IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8 at significantly higher levels than seasonal H3N2 influenza viruses in primary human lung cells. Our data suggest that the high viral replication and the high induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the human lung cells infected with HP H5N1 avian influenza viruses may enhance pathogenicity in the infected humans as compared to seasonal H3N2 influenza viruses.

Innovation Scientists Develop Nasal H5N1 Vaccine Designed to Block Bird Flu Where the Virus Enters the Body Published Sep 15, 2026 11:00 AM EDT By Lucia Carter Editorial Illustration via AI Share on Twitter Share on Facebook Share on Pocket A nasal vaccine protected mice from a lethal strain of H5N1 and dramatically reduced the amount of virus in their noses and windpipes. The experimental vaccine gave two doses to the animals before they were exposed to the virus. All vaccinated mice survived, while those that did not receive the vaccine died. But survival was only part of the result. When researchers examined the animals' respiratory tissues, they found roughly 100,000 times less virus in the nose and trachea of vaccinated mice than in unvaccinated animals. The finding is particularly interesting because these are among the first places H5N1 can encounter the body after entering through the respiratory tract. The vaccine has not been tested in people, so it is far too early to know whether the same protection would occur in humans. Still, the results point to a different way of preparing the immune system for bird flu: meeting the virus at the respiratory surface instead of relying only on protection circulating deeper inside the body. The Vaccine Was Delivered Through the Nose Most vaccines are injected into muscle. A nasal vaccine takes a different route, coating the lining of the nose and upper airways with vaccine material. This can stimulate mucosal immunity, a type of immune defence that operates at surfaces where pathogens first enter the body. For a respiratory virus such as H5N1, researchers wanted to know whether building that local defence could help stop the virus earlier. To create the experimental vaccine, the researchers used a modified vesicular stomatitis virus, or VSV, as a carrier. The engineered virus was designed to deliver H5N1-related material and trigger an immune response without causing the disease associated with H5N1. The mice received two doses four weeks apart. Four weeks after the second dose, the researchers exposed them to a dose of H5N1 that would normally be fatal. Every Vaccinated Mouse Survived The difference was immediate. All of the vaccinated mice survived the H5N1 challenge. The unvaccinated control animals did not. The vaccinated mice also produced strong antibody responses. Some of those antibodies circulated through the body, while others were found in the respiratory tract, giving the animals immune activity close to the place where the virus was introduced. Researchers then looked more closely at where H5N1 had travelled inside the animals. That was when the nasal vaccine's most distinctive effect became apparent. The Nose and Trachea Had Far Less Virus The researchers measured viral levels in several tissues, including the nose, trachea and lungs. The vaccine substantially reduced the amount of H5N1 found in all three areas. But the reduction was particularly dramatic in the upper respiratory tract. Vaccinated mice had about 100,000 times less virus in their nasal and tracheal tissues than the unvaccinated animals. The reduction in the lungs was also substantial, although not as pronounced as in the upper airways. That pattern fits the idea behind the nasal vaccine. Rather than waiting for the immune system to respond after the virus has moved deeper into the body, the vaccine appears to establish strong local defences along the respiratory surfaces. The researchers also found that the vaccinated animals had less severe damage in their lungs and maintained better overall health after infection. The Protection Lasted for Months The researchers did not stop after the first H5N1 challenge. They wanted to see whether the protection would fade quickly. They followed another group of vaccinated mice for 250 days, measuring their immune responses over time. The animals maintained neutralising antibodies, which can prevent viruses from infecting cells. When the mice were later exposed to a lethal dose of H5N1, both vaccine doses tested again produced 100% survival. The vaccinated animals also continued to show markedly lower amounts of virus in the nose and trachea than the controls. For an experimental vaccine, that durability is notable. It suggests the nasal approach did not simply produce a short-lived burst of protection immediately after vaccination. Human Trials Will Have to Answer the Bigger Questions The findings are promising, but they come entirely from animal experiments. A vaccine that works in mice can still behave very differently in humans. Researchers will need to establish whether the modified VSV vaccine is safe, what dose would be appropriate, how well it stimulates mucosal immunity in people, and whether it can protect against H5N1 infection or severe disease. For now, the experiment offers a particularly striking result: after nasal vaccination, the mice that survived lethal H5N1 had dramatically less virus in the very tissues the virus encountered first. That makes the nose more than just the route used to deliver the vaccine. In this study, it became part of the defence. How does the virus get into these animals? Published: September 15, 2026 4:13pm EDT A rare Australian sea lion (Neophoca cinerea) on Kangaroo Island has been confirmed to have died from highly pathogenic H5N1 bird flu. The sea lion had recently been attacked by a shark, weakening it. Testing revealed the cause of death was the virus. Another dead sea lion is being tested. The first confirmed H5N1 death recorded for this species in Australia marks a significant and worrying moment for Australian wildlife. These sea lions are the rarest pinniped to breed on our coasts. They’re considered endangered, as their population is under 12,000. This species of sea lion breeds slowly, meaning even a limited outbreak of bird flu could be very bad news for breeding colonies. The strain of bird flu now circulating in Australia and New Zealand has evolved to infect some mammal species as well as birds. Since 2021, it has decimated some seals and sea lion species overseas, especially in South America. The virus is even capable of infecting and killing dolphins. But how does the virus pass between these animals? How do seals and dolphins actually get the infection? There’s much we don’t know. But here’s what we do. Bird (and mammal) flu? The highly pathogenic H5N1 strain of bird flu (avian influenza) was first detected in 1996. Highly pathogenic means it is capable of causing severe disease. In 2020, the virus mutated into a new, more deadly variant capable of infecting more species. It is no longer just a threat to birds. Since 2021, this strain has caused mass die-offs in pinnipeds (seals, sea lions, walruses and so on). In Argentina, more than 17,000 southern elephant seal pups died. After the virus reached Australia’s Heard Island, drone surveys identified more than 13,000 dead pups from the same species. Some pinnipeds were able to catch older bird flu strains. But these mainly affected their respiratory tracts. The newer highly pathogenic H5N1 strain can also invade the brain and nervous system of mammals. Sea lions have been found with encephalitis, brain inflammation and other neurological signs before death. In Florida, a bottlenose dolphin infected with H5N1 was found to have the highest concentration of virus in its brain. How do pinnipeds catch the virus? The way the virus gets into pinnipeds is likely from birds. When sick seabirds infected with the virus die on the islands used by sea lions for breeding, the virus can jump across to the mammal. This can be through direct contact, scavenging carcasses or exposure to contaminated sand, rocks, water, faeces or mucus. Sick birds can and do sneeze and cough, spreading viral particles. Direct transmission from mammal to mammal is suspected, though not yet conclusively proven in most wild marine mammal die-off events. But the fact so many elephant seal pups died rapidly suggests the virus is able to spread between them. Transmission between elephant seals is suspected to have occurred in Argentina. It’s highly likely that the virus can spread faster in dense seal and sea lion colonies. Pinnipeds, too, can sneeze and cough, which would help spread the virus. One researcher testing transmission reportedly had a seal sneeze in their face. They then contracted severe conjunctivitis in the eye. The bird flu virus was detected. Sea lions may be getting infected by contact with sick or dead birds and their excretions. atosan/Getty But what about whales and dolphins? So far, so logical. But how can dolphins and other cetaceans contract the virus? After a dolphin washed up dead near Goolwa, South Australia, it tested positive for H5N1 – the first case in Australia. How did it happen? SA chief veterinary officer Dr Skye Fruean told the ABC it was likely the dolphin had “eaten a bird or interacted closely with a wild bird”. This is possible, but we don’t know for sure. What we do know is that this H5N1 strain has been found in a range of dolphin species around the world. So far, there’s little evidence of large whales being infected with bird flu. A juvenile dead whale washed ashore at Victor Harbour was found not to have the virus. Dolphins are likely to get infected by contact with sick birds. Damian Lugowski/Getty How long can the virus survive in the environment? Viruses can survive outside a host, though typically not for long. The H5N1 influenza virus generally survives longest in cool, moist conditions. It can remain infectious for days and sometimes weeks in water, depending on temperature, salinity and sunlight. In hot, dry environments, survival is much shorter. Carcasses can act as temporary reservoirs of the virus while tissues remain fresh. This is one reason why beaches and breeding colonies can be transmission hotspots during an outbreak. What can we do? There’s no real way to stop the spread of H5N1 now that it’s here. But there are many ways we can reduce its impact. Removing carcasses safely is crucial, as it removes these viral reservoirs and stops it getting into Australia’s many scavenging animals. Investing in better monitoring and detection is essential if we are to slow the spread by helping us to target affected populations. We must ramp up protection for endangered species, reduce human pressures such as disturbance of seal colonies and plan to help depleted populations recover. Ultimately, the death of this sea lion shows us that the burden of bird flu will reach far beyond birds. This strain of the virus is a wide-ranging threat able to affect entire ecosystems.
